Detective Gillian Seed hunts androids that imitate people. Kojima's cinematic storytelling joined cyberpunk, the parser/menu adventure, and distinctive direction.

As the detective Gillian Seed you search Neo-Kobe, talk to witnesses, and find out which of the people around you is a Snatcher android. The investigation does not go through a text parser. You pick entries in a menu and sometimes switch into a short shooting scene when a weapon comes out.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/102-cover-1.jpg)

Hideo Kojima directed the game in 1988 at Konami for the Japanese computers PC-8801 and MSX2, apart from the Famicom action teams. Technically it is an adventure with fixed shots and, later, on disc, with voice as well. The original version relies on text, portraits, and editing.

Kojima takes his pacing from film: long dialogues, silence in an apartment, then sudden violence. The player is not an action hero all the time, only in tense moments. The result is a split game. The inquiry has the rhythm of a visual novel, and the shooting is a reflex test that only half fits the detective work.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/102-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 1988 cyberpunk is already familiar in books and in cinema. On the Japanese PC, Kojima translates it into menus, not into a free three-dimensional street. Later English versions, especially on the Mega-CD, opened the game to an audience outside Japan. For a seminar, though, the original computer form is what matters.

What has aged are the menus and the period science-fiction props. Some conversations drag, because the interface lacks today's economy. What has not aged is the desire to tell an action game like a film. Kojima later carries the same instinct into Metal Gear.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/102-screenshot-3.png)

The world is noir: a partner, memory, a city that lies. The player assembles the culprit's identity from conversations, not from a map of levels. When you compare Snatcher with Japanese adventures of the time, notice the editing. The camera and the silence do more work here than object puzzles.

Snatcher is a cyberpunk graphic adventure game developed and published by Konami. Snatcher is set in a future Japanese metropolis where humanoid robots dubbed "Snatchers" have been discovered killing humans and replacing them in society.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/102-screenshot-4.jpg)

The game follows Gillian Seed, an American expat and amnesiac who joins an anti-Snatcher agency in search of his past. Kojima wanted Snatcher to have a cinematic feel, so the setting and story are heavily influenced by science fiction films, like Blade Runner, Akira, The Terminator, and Invasion of the Body Snatchers.

It garnered a cult following and was remade as a role-playing game called SD Snatcher for the MSX2 in 1990. In 1994, Konami developed an English Sega CD version of Snatcher specifically for Europe and North America.

Snatcher has been retrospectively acclaimed as both one of the best adventure and cyberpunk games of all time, and identified as a foundation for the themes Kojima explored later in the Metal Gear series. Snatcher was last rereleased in 1996 on the PlayStation and Sega Saturn.

Snatcher has been described as both a graphic adventure game and a visual novel. The development of the original versions of Snatcher lasted around 18 months.

Originally Snatcher was going to be a PC-8801 exclusive, but it was also developed for the MSX2 at the request of Konami. Snatcher was released once again in Japan in 1996, this time for 32-bit game consoles.

Kojima has expressed interest in reviving Snatcher in some capacity, and has explained he does not have the time to work on the project himself but would welcome another director to lead it. Snatcher was the first translation project for Jeremy Blaustein, who went on to translate Kojima's Metal Gear Solid (1998).

The game was to be directed by Blaustein with former Snatcher staff making up other parts of the team. Snatcher was remade into a role-playing game called SD Snatcher for the MSX2, released on April 27, 1990.

Initially Hideo Kojima was not involved with the development of SD Snatcher, as he was busy with Metal Gear 2: Solid Snake at the time, but he and his team were brought in during the late stages of development to help complete SD Snatcher smoothly so they could resume development of Solid Snake. Suda credited Snatcher, along with works by Yu Suzuki, for igniting his interest in video games.
