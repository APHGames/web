Children travel a modern America full of aliens and absurd humor. A contemporary setting and a literary tone disrupted the fantasy conventions of the JRPG and led to EarthBound.

Children with psychic abilities walk through towns that look like contemporary America and fight aliens, cultists, and things from the refrigerator. Battles are turn-based and random encounters are frequent. Between them the game speaks an ordinary language: family, a telephone, school, a neighbor who is not a dragon.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/108-cover-1.jpg)

Shigesato Itoi, a copywriter and essayist, not a career dungeon designer, directed the game in 1989 for the Famicom. Pax Softnica programmed it, and Nintendo produced it together with Itoi's studio Ape. The engine is the classic Japanese RPG of the time: a map, a menu, a battle on a separate screen. What is unusual is the subject, not the way hits are calculated.

The decision to set swords and castles aside has a consequence for tone. A joke can live in a television commercial, and that is why a serious scene hurts more when it arrives. In 1989 Dragon Quest and Final Fantasy rule with a fantasy template. Mother answers them with a world the player recognizes from the street.

The game did not reach the West for a long time. Only a later release under the name EarthBound Beginnings showed what the entry the world knows as EarthBound grew from. What has aged is the frequency of battles and the grinding of characters. The system is plain, and the road between towns can be a tax paid for dialogue that is worth reading.

What has not aged is the literary nerve. Itoi writes the game as a feuilleton with monsters, and Nintendo lent him the Famicom machinery to do it. The inventory and the spells are servants. The main texture is in item descriptions and in whom you meet in a shop.

When you tell the family tree of the Japanese RPG, do not leave Mother as a side path. It is a conscious break with the sword and the king, and the whole later tone of the series is read from it. (known as EarthBound outside Japan) is a video game series that consists of three role-playing video games: Mother (1989), known as EarthBound Beginnings outside Japan, for the Family Computer; Mother 2 (1994), known as EarthBound outside Japan, for the Super Nintendo Entertainment System; and Mother 3 (2006) for the Game Boy Advance.

Written by Shigesato Itoi, published by Nintendo, and featuring game mechanics modeled on the Dragon Quest series, Mother is known for its sense of humor, originality, and parody. While visiting Nintendo for other business, Itoi approached Shigeru Miyamoto about making Mother.

Mother 3 was originally slated for release on the Nintendo 64 and its 64DD disk drive accessory as EarthBound 64, but was cancelled in 2000. Three years later, the project was reannounced for the Game Boy Advance alongside a rerelease of Mother and Mother 2 in the combined cartridge Mother 1 + 2.

Mother 3 abandoned the 3D graphics progress for a 2D style, and became a bestseller upon its release. EarthBound was rereleased for the Wii U Virtual Console in 2013, and Mother received its English-language debut for the same platform in 2015, retitled EarthBound Beginnings.

In 2022, Nintendo released Mother 1 and 2 to their Nintendo Switch Online service. Their projects include a full fan translation of Mother 3, a full-length documentary, and fangame attempts.

Ness, the protagonist of EarthBound and Lucas, the protagonist of Mother 3, received exposure from their inclusion in the Super Smash Bros. Other Mother series locations and characters have made appearances in the fighting games.

American developer Toby Fox considered pitching to Itoi that he could help him make a fourth game in the Mother franchise, but changed his mind after Itoi encouraged Fox to make a sequel to Undertale. In 1001 Video Games You Must Play Before You Die, Christian Donlan wrote that the Mother series is a "massive RPG franchise" in Japan comparable to that of Final Fantasy and Dragon Quest, though it does not enjoy the same popularity in the West.
