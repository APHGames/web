Mario cleans Delfino Island using the water device FLUDD. The water jetpack fundamentally changes the acceleration and aerial correction of classic Mario movement.

On Delfino Island Mario does not merely wash stains. FLUDD, Professor E. Gadd's water device, changes his jump: the hover nozzle lets him brake in the air and the rocket throws him higher. The levels are built around water as a new engine of movement. The precise, short Mario jump receives a correction here that makes it a different physics.

Nintendo EAD made it in 2002 under Yoshiaki Koizumi's direction, and Shigeru Miyamoto produced it. After Mario 64 it is the second large three-dimensional Mario and the first to give him a tool. The episodes without FLUDD restore the old precision and are among the hardest places. On them the player feels what the water pack was masking the whole time.

Blue coins and repeated returns to the squares are the price of an island that is cleaned gradually. The world is one space, not a row of isolated levels. In 2002, on the new GameCube, Nintendo does not show a realistic world but a sunny town whose dirt is a game resource.

The camera and some of the coin chases have aged. The hover and the feeling that a tool rewrites acceleration have not. Bowser Jr. as a false Mario uses the same tool against you. The conflict is about water, not about another genre pulled over Mario's movement.

The Yoshi sections take FLUDD away for a while and remind you that a pure platformer still runs beside the novelty. For movement students it is the class after Mario 64. When you change the correction in the air, you also have to rewrite the length of the platforms.

The voices and the sunny island hold the mood even where the textures have paled into their period. The mood has aged more slowly than the collecting detours. Watch the hover before the graffiti plot. The whole game is a sentence about what happens to Mario when you let him change his mind in the air.
