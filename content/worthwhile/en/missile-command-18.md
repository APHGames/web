With limited anti-missile defense, the player protects cities from bombardment. The mechanic of inevitable losses turned a score arcade into an unusually anxious resource system.

The player has a limited supply of anti-ballistic shots and protects cities from falling warheads; every city you lose no longer defends you in the next wave. A trackball aims the crosshair, and a salvo you fire for nothing is missing at the moment the sky splits into more trails.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/018-poster-1.jpg)

Dave Theurer designed it for Atari, and the machine was released in 1980, in years when nuclear anxiety is a public subject, not a science-fiction backdrop. The picture is a color raster and six cities under the hills; the beauty of the explosion is part of the horror, because a successful interception is a mushroom cloud too, only yours.

The decision that the game cannot be won for good had the consequence that the score measures a delay, not a conquest. The ending is part of the rules, not a balance bug. Warheads split, and the smart bomber punishes a player who can handle only the lower layer. Attention is a resource just like ammunition.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/018-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 1980 Pac-Man stands beside it as joy, and Asteroids as physics. Missile Command is an arcade game that takes contemporary politics as a subject of control. Theurer built it as a game about defense, not about attack. The player is not a pilot over a foreign city, but the last battery over their own.

What has aged is the abstraction of cities into silent silhouettes and the certainty that nuclear war fits into a score. What has not aged is the management of loss: what you save now changes what you can save at all later.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/018-screenshot-3.jpg)

Few arcade games of that season admit that the master is still only the one who lost later. In a seminar, ask when the students' table stops laughing at the explosion. That second is part of the design.

It is also noted for its manifestation of the Cold War's effects on popular culture, in that it features an implementation of National Missile Defense and parallels real-life nuclear war. Missile Command was a commercial success for Sega in Japan, where it was among the top ten highest-grossing arcade games of 1980.

In 1983, Softline readers named the Atari 8-bit version of Missile Command eighth on the magazine's top thirty list of Atari programs by popularity. On level thirteen, if the player exhausts their missile supply without scoring any points, at the end of the game the city on the right will turn into "RF" – the initials of the programmer Rob Fulop.

Missile Command was released for Atari 8-bit computers in 1981, followed by an identical version for the Atari 5200 in 1982. Missile Command has seen many re-releases in many Atari compilation titles: Sequels.

In late 1980, a two-player sequel Missile Command 2 was field tested but never released, although at least one prototype appeared in an arcade in Santa Clara, California. This game was similar to the original except that each player had their own set of cities and missile batteries and the players could cooperate to save each other's cities from the onslaught.

In 1992, Atari developed a prototype of an arcade game called Arcade Classics for their 20th anniversary, which included Missile Command 2 and Super Centipede. In 1982, Atari released a game called Liberator, which was seen by some as being a sequel to Missile Command with the situation essentially reversed; in Liberator, the player is the one attacking planetary bases from orbit.

]] An updated version called Missile Command 3D was released for the Atari Jaguar in 1995. Hasbro Interactive released a 3D remake of Missile Command for Microsoft Windows and PlayStation in 1999.

A port of Missile Command with high-definition graphics was released via Xbox Live Arcade for the Xbox 360 on July 4, 2007. Missile Command was released for the iPhone and iPod Touch for US$5 on September 23, 2008.

In March 2020, Atari released a new remake, Missile Command: Recharged, on mobile platforms, the first in the Atari Recharged series. While neither the Intellivision Amico version of Missile Command nor the Amico console itself have yet released, a mobile version was announced in late 2023, as part of Intellivision's Amico Home initiative.

Contemporary Missile Command clones include Missile Defense (1981) for the Apple II, Stratos (1982) for the TRS-80, Missile Control (1983) for the BBC Micro, Repulsar (1983) for the ZX Spectrum, and Barrage (1983) for the TI-99/4A. The gameplay of Missile Command, specifically, the contrails left by incoming ICBMs, and the visuals of cities being destroyed by nuclear warheads on a video display screen, strongly resembles the opening nuclear war scenes from the 1977 film, Damnation Alley.

In February 2010, Atari was talking with several studios to turn Missile Command into a film. 20th Century Fox acquired the rights to bring Missile Command to film the following year.

In May 2016, Emmett/Furla Oasis Films closed a deal to partner with Atari to produce and finance films based on Centipede and Missile Command. Missile Command has been popular in high-level play, both in marathon settings and tournaments.

In 1981, Floridian Jody Bowles played a game of Missile Command for 30 hours at The Filling Station Eatery in Pensacola. On July 3, 1985, Roy Shildt of Los Angeles set a world record in tournament-set Missile Command, with a score of 1,695,265, as verified by Twin Galaxies.
