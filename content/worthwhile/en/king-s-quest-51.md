Sir Graham explores a fairy-tale world and carries out tasks for the king. An animated character moved "inside" the graphic scene, by which Sierra advanced the graphic adventure beyond static illustrations.

Sir Graham walks a fairy-tale kingdom, collects objects, and carries out tasks for the king, while you still type verbs as in a text game. The character is no longer a cursor in a description. He is a little figure who walks in front of a bush and behind it, and the picture thereby gains depth, even though the world still waits for a sentence.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/051-screenshot-1.png)

Roberta Williams designed it for Sierra, and the game was released in 1984 on the IBM PCjr. It is a showcase title for a machine IBM intended as a home computer and that will not succeed in the market; the game outlives it. It runs on the AGI engine, the Adventure Game Interpreter. Instead of the static drawing of Mystery House there is an animated avatar and a fixed screen you can walk across.

The decision to let the hero step into the illustration had the consequence that a puzzle can be about the place you step on, not only about the object you name. The parser still punishes the wrong word, though. Three tasks for the king give the journey a skeleton. The world is more open than the single house of Mystery House, and yet it has a fairy-tale direction, not a sandbox.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/051-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 1984 Elite is released beside it on the BBC Micro as pure freedom of systems. King's Quest answers in the opposite way: a fairy tale with an author who knows how it is supposed to end. With this game Sierra founds a school in which death waits on a wrong step, and the joke sometimes consists in the game not warning you.

What has aged is the parser in the middle of a picture that already looks as if it could bear a click, and punishments that send a newcomer back a quarter of an hour with no explanation. What has not aged is the pleasure of a character having a body in the scene, and of a bush being not scenery but an obstacle with a direction.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/051-screenshot-3.jpg)

Sierra's later games will only refine this avatar, until the parser eventually gives way to icons. In a seminar, let the students hit the first unfair death and then name whether the culprit is the word or the step.

King's Quest: Quest for the Crown, originally released as King's Quest, is an adventure game developed by Sierra On-Line and published originally for the IBM PCjr in 1984 as the first entry in the King's Quest series. The game follows the young knight Sir Graham as he journeys through the pseudo-medieval fairy tale-inspired fantasy realm of Daventry, on a quest to recover three magical items and become the next king.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/051-cover-4.jpg)

The player interacts with locations and items using text commands, and must avoid numerous hazards and obstacles in their quest. King's Quest was developed by Sierra after it was approached by IBM to make a game similar to Sierra's Wizard and the Princess (1980) that would showcase the computing power of the upcoming PCjr with animation and complex graphics.

King's Quest has been credited with saving Sierra from the financial effects of the video game crash of 1983, and has been considered the start of the graphic adventure genre. King's Quest has been named as one of the most important computer games of all time, and in 2020 was inducted into the World Video Game Hall of Fame.

King's Quest is an adventure game set in the pseudo-medieval fairy tale-inspired fantasy realm of Daventry, in which the player controls the character Sir Graham to complete a quest for three magical items.
