The player steers a ship against flying saucers. It brought the university type of space action into a commercial coin-operated machine and foreshadowed Atari.

The player steers a ship in an open field and shoots at flying saucers that fire back; the result is a score, not a story. The controls want both hands: thrust, rotation, and fire are separate, and anyone expecting a simple fairground toy loses before they understand which way they are facing.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/002-screenshot-1.jpg)

Nolan Bushnell and Ted Dabney, then working as Syzygy, built the game as a commercial echo of Spacewar! and handed cabinet production to Nutting Associates. It is a coin-operated machine from 1971, still without a microprocessor in the modern sense: discrete logic and a television screen inside a fiberglass cabinet assemble the picture.

The decision to move a university duel into a bar had a direct consequence. The game was too complicated for a casual customer, and many people left the machine before they felt they had got their coin's worth of fun. The saucers do not fly like later invasion formations; the fight is still close to the laboratory game, only with a coin slot.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/002-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 1971 it stands beside electromechanical games and pinball machines. Computer Space is one of the first attempts to say that a picture generated by a circuit can be a product. It failed as a mass-market machine, but it succeeded as proof that Bushnell and Dabney could build a video game outside a university.

What has aged is the unreadable relationship between stick and ship for anyone who never saw Spacewar!. What has not aged is the problem the whole arcade inherits from it: how to take a deep system and turn it into behavior you understand in ten seconds.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/002-screenshot-3.jpg)

Pong, a year later, is a conscious simplification of this lesson, not a random idea from nothing. For a seminar, Computer Space is useful as a dead branch that still shows the road: commerce begins where the player understands the rule without an initiation.

Computer Space is a derivative of the 1962 computer game Spacewar!, which is possibly the first video game to spread to multiple computer installations. After the pair were unable to find a way to economically run the game on a minicomputer such as the Data General Nova, they hit upon the idea of instead replacing the central computer with custom-designed hardware created to run just that game.

The game spawned one clone game, Star Trek (1972), and Nutting produced a two-player version of Computer Space in 1973 without involvement from Bushnell and Dabney. Computer Space s release marked the ending of the early history of video games and the start of the commercial video game industry.
