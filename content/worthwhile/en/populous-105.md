The player, as a deity, shapes the terrain and supports their followers. Indirect control and manipulation of the world instead of units founded a significant branch of the "god game."

You do not play a hero, but a hand that raises and lowers terrain. People settle on flat ground, and you prepare soil for them or take it from the enemy. You do not send units one by one. Settlers multiply according to how much room they have and how strongly they believe in you, and you intervene with disasters and terrain.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/105-cover-1.jpg)

Peter Molyneux and the studio Bullfrog released the game in 1989 on the Amiga and the Atari ST, with Electronic Arts as publisher. It was a small British team, not an arcade line. The image is isometric and computed on 16-bit computers. The world is a field of heights that the player rewrites with a cursor hand.

The decision to forbid a direct order is the core of the god game. The consequence: victory looks like a landscape that settled itself, not like an army drawn up in ranks. In 1989 strategy is still mostly a turn-based wargame or an economic spreadsheet. Populous offers a view from above in which the tool is the land.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/105-screenshot-2.jpg)

Mana comes from worshippers. The more people follow you, the stronger the disaster you can call down, so care and destruction are one account. What has aged are the tiny figures and the abstract violence. An earthquake is an icon, not a scene, and a player today does not see a particular person's fate in it.

What has not aged is shaping the world. The same pleasure in landscape returns in later Bullfrog games and in the god games after them. The knight you send against the other side is still a consequence of faith, not a unit from a barracks. Production is religious, not industrial.

Compare it with Civilization two years later. There is no technology tree here; there is the height of a tile and the crowd standing on it. In a seminar, name indirect control precisely. The player cannot say go here; they can only change the conditions under which a character wants to go.

It is the first game in the Populous series, preceding Populous II: Trials of the Olympian Gods and Populous: The Beginning. Bullfrog produced Populous World Editor, which gave users the ability to modify the appearance of characters, cities, and terrain.

An expansion pack called Populous: The Promised Lands added five new types of landscape (the geometric Silly Land, Wild West, Lego style Block Land, Revolution Française, and computer themed Bit Plains). In addition, another expansion disk called Populous: The Final Frontier added a single new landscape-type and was released as a cover disk for The One.

Populous is both highly original and amazingly addictive, with a constant challenge on offer". STart in 1990 gave "kudos especially to Peter Molyneux, the creative force behind Populous".

Most video games posit you as a mere sword-wielding, perilously mortal human; in Populous you're a deity. By 2001, Populous had sold four million copies, making it one of the best-selling PC games of all time.

In 1990 Computer Gaming World named Populous as Strategy Game of the Year. In 1994, PC Gamer US named Populous as the third best computer game ever.

The editors hailed it as "unbelievably addictive fun, and one of the most appealing and playable strategy games of all time." In 1999, Next Generation listed Populous as number 44 on their "Top 50 Games of All Time", commenting that, "A perfect blend of realtime strategy, resource management, and more than a little humor, it remains unsurpassed in the genre it created." In 2018, Complex placed the game 98th on their "The Best Super Nintendo Games of All Time". IGN ranked Populous 70th on its "Top 100 SNES Games of All Time." Legacy.

In 1990 Bullfrog used the Populous engine to develop Powermonger, a strategic combat-oriented game with similar mechanics to Populous, but with a 3-dimensional graphical interface. In 1991 they developed and released a true sequel, Populous II: Trials of the Olympian Gods, and in 1998 a further direct sequel, Populous: The Beginning.

Populous was also released on the Super NES, developed by Infinity Co., Ltd and published by Imagineer as one of the original titles for the console in Japan, Populous has been re-released through GOG.com and on Origin through the Humble Origin Bundle sale. The browser-based game Reprisal was created in 2012 by Electrolyte and Last17 as a homage to Populous.

Godus (formerly Project GODUS) was revealed as a URL on the face of Curiosity – What's Inside the Cube?, and "is aimed to reimagine" Populous.
