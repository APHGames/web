A civil aircraft is flown according to instruments and a simple 3D landscape. Early proof that a PC could support a long-developed technical simulation instead of arcade abstraction.

The player flies a civil aircraft by the instruments and the view from the cockpit, holds altitude, heading, and speed, and tries not to depart from what a real flight would bear. There is no wave of enemies and no score for a kill. Success is boring correctness: to land, not to win a gunfight.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/037-cover-1.jpg)

Bruce Artwick developed it at subLOGIC out of work on three-dimensional graphics, and Microsoft released version 1.0 in 1982 for the IBM PC. The landscape is a simple wireframe and flat model, and the instruments are part of the screen. A computer the public knows as an office machine is here calculating a flight.

The decision to take lift, thrust, and instruments seriously had the consequence that the game refuses the arcade shortcut. Anyone who only wants to tilt a joystick will break the aircraft. In 1982 the IBM PC is new and games are not the main reason to buy one. Flight Simulator is an argument for why game seriousness might sit exactly here, not in the arcade.

It is not a military cockpit. Civil flight is intentional: the audience is not a cadet, but a person who wants to try a craft. What has aged is the poverty of the landscape and a control scheme that, without key shortcuts and without patience, will not let a newcomer even onto the runway.

What has not aged is the respect for the instrument: altitude, heading, and speed are three truths at once, and none of them is decoration. The whole later tradition of civil simulators on the PC sits on this precedent, including games that now photograph the landscape instead of drawing it with a line.

In a seminar, do not force the students into a combat demonstration. Let them hold a heading for ten minutes. That is the game.
