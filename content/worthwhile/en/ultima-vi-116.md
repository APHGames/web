The Avatar resolves the conflict between the Britannians and the gargoyles in a continuous world. Removing separate dungeon modes brought the RPG world closer to a model of unified simulated space.

As the Avatar you resolve the conflict between Britannia and the gargoyles in one continuous world. Town, wilderness, and underground are the same space, not a separate dungeon mode. You talk to characters through keywords and you pick objects up off the ground. The world is a simulation of a place, not just a map of transitions between screens.

Richard Garriott and Origin released the game in 1990 on DOS as the sixth entry of Ultima. It is still his world of virtues, but without the technical excuse that a dungeon has to be a different game. The engine is tile-based and viewed from above. There are no textures on the walls as there will be two years later in Underworld; the continuity is in the rules, not in the perspective.

The decision to abolish the separate dungeon changes how the player thinks about a journey. The consequence: the underground is a district of the world, so the moral conflict with the gargoyles can stand on concrete places, not on an abstract map. In 1990 a large computer RPG still often breaks the world into modes. Ultima VI claims that one scale is enough.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/116-screenshot-2.jpg)

The gargoyles are not just another enemy sprite. They have their own reading of the virtues, and the player is meant to understand that Britannia can be in the right and in the wrong. What has aged is the keyboard control and the dictionary dialogue. Without a hint you try words, and the interface resists.

What has not aged is the unity of place. The seventh entry will carry it into the daily schedules of characters, and the immersive simulation will take its ambition from it. Combat is still turn-based and statistical. The tension is not in the strike; it is in whether the world you walked through gives your violence a reason.

When you draw the path from the dungeon crawler to the open world, mark this game as the moment the underground stopped being a different program. Garriott is still playing on the Avatar's identity. The technical change serves an old question: how virtue behaves when you put it on a concrete street.

Ultima VI: The False Prophet, released by Origin Systems in 1990, is the sixth part in the role-playing video game series of Ultima. Ultima VI sees the player return to Britannia, at war with a race of gargoyles from another land, struggling to stop a prophecy from ending their race.

In Ultima VI, the whole game uses a single scale, with towns and other places seamlessly integrated into the main map; dungeons are now also viewed from the same perspective as the rest of the game, rather than the first-person perspective used by Ultima I-V. The development of the Ultima series originated on the Apple II and every prior game had been developed primarily on that platform.

Major changes were made that distinguished Ultima VI from earlier Ultima games. One such change was the world design, The software routines that governed every element of movement and combat was developed by 25 year-old Boston programmer Herman Miller, who previously wrote the MS-DOS conversions of Ultima V and Times of Lore.

The conversation system, the means by which the player talks with characters, was developed by 26 year-old Chinese programmer Cheryl Chen, who developed her own programming language for the game called UCS (Ultima VI conversation system). Slightly improved versions of the Ultima VI engine were also used for the Worlds of Ultima spin-off series.

Origin produced a deluxe edition of Ultima VI for sale by mail order at the same $69.95 price as the retail version. The editors of Strategy Plus named Ultima VI the best role-playing game of 1990. The magazine highlighted Ultima VI s "interesting and important story, dynamite graphics ...

In 1992, Computer Gaming World added Ultima VI to the magazine's Hall of Fame for games that readers highly rated over time. In 1996 the magazine ranked it as the 44th best game of all time, stating that Ultima VI "hit new heights in virtuality with the defined objects in the game world" and "also presented a brilliant treatise on the danger of prejudice".

A fan-made recreation of Ultima VI using the Dungeon Siege engine, The U6 Project (aka Archon), was released on 5 July 2010. Another remake project uses the Exult engine, using graphics from Ultima VII.
