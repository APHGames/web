Two spaceships fight around a gravitational star. One of the most important early examples of a real-time graphical computer game, physics, and direct multiplayer control.

Two ships circle a star on the screen and fire torpedoes; you lose if a shot hits you, or if gravity pulls you into the sun. The ship has inertia, limited fuel, and limited ammunition, so every burn and every shot is a decision, not an automatic reflex.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/001-screenshot-1.jpg)

Steve Russell wrote it at MIT in 1962 with Martin Graetz, Wayne Wiitanen, and others around the PDP-1, with no publisher and no plan to sell the game. It ran on DEC's Type 30 vector display: the picture is drawn by a beam, not a television scan line, which is why the ships and torpedoes are clean lines.

Peter Samson added a starfield based on real constellations, and Dan Edwards added gravity; a demo of what the display could do became a fight with tactics. Hyperspace can save the ship with a jump and can just as easily destroy it, so escape has a price and panic is part of the rules.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/001-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 1962 computers are still laboratory machines; this game shows that the same device can carry two people reacting in real time. Commercial video games do not exist yet. Spacewar! spreads as a demonstration program for the PDP-1, so an audience can see what the machine can do.

What has aged is the control scheme tied to the switches of one specific mainframe, which you now meet in a museum or an emulator. What has not aged is the basic contract of an action game: a shared space, physics, limited resources, and an opponent who is a person.

Nolan Bushnell played it at the University of Utah, and that experience stands at the start of the road to Computer Space and later to Atari. When you show it to students, it is still readable without a manual: ignore the gravity, and you lose even with the better aim.
