Two players in one living room steer tanks or planes and try to hit the opponent before they themselves are blown out of the game. The arena is small, the shot flies straight or bounces, and an obstacle turns each variant into a different sport with the same joystick.

Combat is the launch cartridge of the Atari VCS in 1977, packed in with the console so the buyer has something to play on the day they bring the machine home. The picture is assembled by the TIA chip, designed for a cheap console attached to a television: a few sprites, a playfield, and shots, with no bitmap map in the modern sense.

The decision to sell dozens of variants on one cartridge had the consequence that a home game looks like a box of rules, not like one fixed pastime. The model is Tank, the 1974 arcade game from Kee Games. Combat takes a coin-operated duel and asks whether it still works when coins are not the point and the players are neighbors.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/009-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 1977 the first wave of home consoles stands beside it, and Pong is still alive. The VCS wins by promising interchangeable games, and Combat is that promise in the hand. It is not an auteur statement but a showcase of hardware: what the chip can do, the game shows twice, once as a tank and once as a plane.

What has aged is the coarseness of the sprites and the fact that without a second player the cartridge has almost no reason to exist. What has not aged is the pleasure of a variant: the same body of rules, a different obstacle, and an argument about which version is fair.

Home multiplayer thus gets a pattern that later consoles will keep proving by packing one shared game in with the hardware. In a seminar, do not play it as an Atari curiosity, but as proof that the content of a console in 1977 is mainly a way to share a screen.

Based on Atari's 1974 arcade video game Tank, Combat was initially developed by Steve Mayer and Ron Milner. By the early 1980s, Combat 's graphics and gameplay were seen as being out-of-date.

A sequel titled Combat Two was planned for the Atari 2600, but never completed. In his article "Combat in Context", Nick Montfort stated it was difficult to place the game genre to Combat as assigning one to it would be considered anachronistic.

Combat was programmed while the hardware of the Atari Video Computer System was still under development. In his academic journal article Combat in Context, Nick Monfrot stated that the game was coded in assembly language by Joe Decuir, who was working on the final VCS hardware with Jay Miner.

Decuir has deferred much of the credit on the game, stating that "Ron Milner and Steve Mayer conceived of what became Combat, I was an implementer [...] I worked on it mostly as a test case for the hardware". Combat was re-released in various compilation formats, such as the Atari 80 in One for Windows in 2003 and the Atari Anthology for PlayStation 2 and Xbox in 2004. From contemporary reviews, Video compared the Atari VCS's launch titles, and gave tank pong the highest rating of the variations on Combat with a seven-and-one-half rating for Tank-Pong and Invisible Tank Pong, six-and-one-half for invisible tank game, and six for all the other variations.

Caufield declared Combat as "still an outstanding two-player game", finding that what it lacked in graphics it made up for in game play. In 1995, Flux magazine ranked Combat #48 on their list of "Top 100 Video Games".

The article stated that despite what the publication described as poor-quality graphics, the game play made Combat earn its position on the list. In 2012, PCMag included Combat in their "The Greatest Atari Games of All Time" list, with writer Eric Griffith noted the dated graphics by 2012 standards, while finding that the game felt "pretty magical in the 70's." Legacy.

A sequel to Combat went into development for the Atari 2600 and was to be titled Combat Two. Combat Two was included as part of the Atari 50 (2022) compilation for Nintendo Switch, PlayStation 4, Steam, and Xbox One.
