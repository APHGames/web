A compact fantasy adventure based on text commands. It showed that a parser adventure could be taken from mainframes onto home microcomputers and sold commercially.

The player types two-word commands and moves through a small fantasy world, collecting objects and solving obstacles that fit in the memory of a home computer. Compared with mainframe Zork, the map is short and the vocabulary is narrow; the adventure is designed to be finished in an evening, not a semester.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/012-cover-1.jpg)

Scott Adams wrote it in 1978 for the TRS-80 and sold it under the name Adventure International. It is one of the first attempts to take a parser game out of the university and put it in a box. To fit into a few kilobytes, every description does not sit in memory like a novel. Adams separated the game data from the interpreter, so the rooms are a table, not free text.

That decision had the consequence that a commercial text adventure on a microcomputer is a system with a limited vocabulary, and the player learns to speak the program's language. The model is Colossal Cave, but the scale is different: instead of an endless cave for people with a PDP-10 account, there is a product for the living room.

In 1978 the TRS-80 is a fresh home machine and the software market is only just forming in magazine advertisements. Adventureland shows that an adventure can be advertised as goods. Adams turns this into a whole line of games on the same interpreter. The work is a format, not just one map.

What has aged is the fragility of two-word sentences and puzzles that punish a synonym. What has not aged is the courage to sell a slow, text game to people who had just bought a computer for the screen.

Without this shortcut between the mainframe and the micro, graphic adventures would not have had the commercial audience they build on. In a seminar, read it as a translation, not as a miniature: what has to be thrown out of a universal cave so that a product remains.

Adventureland is a text adventure video game for microcomputers, released by Scott Adams in 1978. Adventureland, Adams' first program, was inspired by the earlier Colossal Cave Adventure, though it is not on the same scale.

The source code for Adventureland was published in SoftSide magazine in 1980 and the database format was subsequently used in other interpreters such as Brian Howarth's Mysterious Adventures series. Adventureland was written for the TRS-80, then ported to other systems, most of which didn't exist in 1978: Apple II, Atari 8-bit computers, TI-99/4A, PET, VIC-20, Commodore 64, IBM PC, ZX Spectrum, BBC Micro, Acorn Electron, Dragon 32/64, and Exidy Sorcerer.

In 1982, Adventureland was re-released with graphics, thus enabling the player to view visible representations of the scenery and objects.
