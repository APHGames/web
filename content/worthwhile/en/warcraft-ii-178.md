Blizzard released the game in 1995 on DOS and the Mac. After the first Warcraft this is their more confident language: more units, more personality in the briefing, less apology that the genre is new. The engine still handles unit paths imperfectly, but the fog and the distinction between sea and land are a design leap. Information is a resource just as wood is.

The decision to take the fog seriously has a consequence. A scout is not a weak unit for the opening; it is the way you are allowed to decide at all. In 1995, together with Command & Conquer, this pair brings real-time strategy to the center of the PC. Westwood holds the bar and the video; Blizzard holds the camp and the scouting.

The asymmetry of the factions is harder than in the first game. It is no longer fair to expect both sides to have the same sentence with a different portrait. What has aged are the pathfinding and some sieges that want more clicks than decisions. A map can be labor.

What has not aged is the appetite for a briefing that has a voice. A mission is still a small story, not just a map with a condition to destroy everything. Warcraft II: Tides of Darkness is a fantasy real-time strategy (RTS) computer game developed by Blizzard Entertainment and released for MS-DOS and Microsoft Windows in 1995 and Mac OS in 1996 by Blizzard's parent, Davidson & Associates. A sequel to Warcraft: Orcs & Humans, the game was met with positive reviews and won most of the major PC gaming awards in 1996.

In 1996, Blizzard released an expansion pack, Warcraft II: Beyond the Dark Portal, for DOS and Mac OS, and a compilation, Warcraft II: The Dark Saga, for the PlayStation and Sega Saturn. The Battle.net edition, released in 1999, included Warcraft II: Beyond the Dark Portal, provided Blizzard's online gaming service, and replaced the MS-DOS version with a Windows one.

In Warcraft II, as in many RTS games, players collect resources to produce buildings and units to defeat an opponent in combat. Warcraft II was a commercial hit, with global sales above 3 million units by 2001; roughly two-thirds were sold in the United States.

In 1996, Blizzard announced Warcraft Adventures: Lord of the Clans, an adventure game in the Warcraft universe, but canceled the project in 1998. Warcraft III: Reign of Chaos, released in 2002, used parts of Warcraft Adventures characters and storyline, but extended the gameplay used in Warcraft II.

Warcraft II allows players to play AI opponents in separate Human and Orc campaigns, and in stand-alone scenarios. The response to Warcraft: Orcs & Humans also allowed Blizzard to recruit additional top-class developers.

In 1997 Electronic Arts published Warcraft&nbsp;II: The Dark Saga for the Sony PlayStation and Sega Saturn, which combined the campaigns of Warcraft&nbsp;II: Tides of Darkness and Warcraft&nbsp;II: Beyond the Dark Portal. In 1999 Blizzard published the Warcraft II: Battle.net Edition for Windows and Mac, which combined the original game and the expansion pack, retained the Macintosh facilities and replaced the DOS version with a Windows version that included Blizzard's online service, Battle.net, for multiplayer games.

Warcraft II achieved worldwide sales of 500,000 units within three months of release, and of over 1.2&nbsp;million by November. To capitalize on this success, Blizzard released the Warcraft Battlechest on November 11, which bundled Warcraft: Orcs & Humans with Warcraft II and the Beyond the Dark Portal add-on.

Warcraft II claimed position 13 in PC Data's monthly sales rankings for January 1997, while the Warcraft Battlechest SKU took sixth place. The Warcraft Battlechest was the United States' 17th-highest computer game seller of the year, with sales of 262,911 units.

By April 1998, Warcraft II s basic SKU had sold 1.02&nbsp;million units and earned $39.8&nbsp;million in revenue in the United States. Warcraft II as a whole reached 2 million global sales by July 1998, and passed 2.5&nbsp;million by year's end.

Sales continued the following month, when the Warcraft II Battle.net Edition SKU finished 20th for October in the country. As of 2001, worldwide sales of Warcraft II had surpassed 3 million units, with two-thirds derived from the United States.

Warcraft II: Tides of Darkness earned enthusiastic reviews, elevating Blizzard to the elite along with Westwood Studios, id Software and LucasArts. Andy Butcher reviewed Warcraft 2: Tides of Darkness for Arcane magazine, commenting that "Warcraft&nbsp;2 is a great game.

The ability to play either side means that there are a vast number of missions on offer, and a level designer gives yet more longevity." PC Gamer US named Warcraft II the best game of 1995. The editors called it an "easy" choice, and wrote that "Warcraft&nbsp;II stand[s] out&nbsp;— way out&nbsp;— as the most impressive, most entertaining, game of 1995".

The magazine also presented Warcraft&nbsp;II with the award for 1995's "Best Multi-Player Game". The editors wrote that Warcraft&nbsp;II "will keep you glued to the computer for hours on end" and noted that it "could have won had the competition not been so strong".

The IGN review considered WarCraft II in general a well-balanced game, In 1998, PC Gamer declared it the 9th-best computer game ever released, and the editors called it "a sequel that isn't just more of the same; it's bigger and better in every way". GameSpot described the ports of the consoles Sony PlayStation and Sega Saturn, combining Warcraft II: Tides of Darkness and Warcraft II: Beyond the Dark Portal, as practically identical to the PC version.
