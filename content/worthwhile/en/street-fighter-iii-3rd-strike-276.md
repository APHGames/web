Precise fighters use parry to neutralize attacks. Parry shifts defense from passive blocking to a risky timing skill; the title became a long-term competitive canon.

A parry is not a block. It is a tap forward at the moment of impact that erases the attack and opens a punish, so defense is as timed a skill as a strike. The round is short and the animation is hand-drawn on CPS-III hardware. The body carries the information of the frame: whoever does not read the drawing does not read the game, even if they know the move list.

Capcom put it into arcades in 1999 as the third and sharpest Street Fighter III. Beside the new characters it also brings back part of the old guard, and the parry, which the III series had from the start, grows up here into a competitive language. The decision to move defense from passively holding a direction to a risky timing had a consequence for entry. A newcomer gets more than a block, and a master gets a tool with which one correct instant can turn a whole round.

In 1999, when the 3D fighter feels like the future, this game is a conscious defense of the drawn plane. It is not afraid to be the last word of 2D, and in the end it is more of a long word. The competitive scene will play it for years after the cabinets leave the streets. A few years later one tournament parry will become a public memory of the game and show that the rule is a spectacle even without an explanation in the commentary.

What has aged is the strictness of entry and the fact that the characters are not an equally walkable road to a win. What has not aged is the readability of the drawing and the parry as a reason to watch a fight, not only to play it. In seminar, let students only block and then only parry one attack. The difference between passive and active defense is an entire lecture on fighting games.

Against Tekken 3 there is the plane here and a different ethics of space. 3D circles the body, 3rd Strike reads time, and both schools in 1999 are right for different hands. The arcade origin keeps the game in a short round. Home versions will come, and the core remains: two people, one timer, no apology in a story.

The game is useful as proof that a genre does not have to change dimension in order to be modern. It has to change what the hand can catch in one frame. Anyone who opens it only for the tournament legend should first die on an ordinary mid. The legend sits on a rule that hurts in the first round.
