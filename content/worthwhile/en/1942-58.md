A fighter plane fights over the Pacific and escapes by rolls. A more accessible rhythm and a distinctive defensive roll helped define Capcom's arcade school of shooters.

A fighter flies upward over the Pacific, shoots down formations, and in an emergency does a roll that hides the plane from the bullets for a second. Power-ups change the width of the fire, and accompanying planes add wings. The player collects not only points, but the shape of their own weapon.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/058-poster-1.jpg)

Yoshiki Okamoto designed it for Capcom, and the machine was released in 1984. It is an early game from a company trying out a signature on a vertical shooter: readable, sharp, and repeatable. The picture is a color raster with military silhouettes. The history of the Second World War is not a simulation here, but scenery against which an enemy draws well.

The decision to give the player a roll as a defense had the consequence that escape is not only a move to the side. For a moment you vanish from your own fire and from the enemy's, and you pay for it by not shooting. Horizontal waves and large aircraft at the end of a level teach you to read the screen in bands. Whoever panics upward flies into a pattern they should have gone around.

In 1984 the arcade, after the collapse of the American market, is more cautious, and Japanese companies are by contrast running at full speed. 1942 is part of that second energy: Capcom introduces itself as a school of shooters. The war theme is played lightly, almost as a sport. That is a decision which reads differently today than it did in a Japanese arcade in 1984, and a lecture ought to say so.

What has aged is exactly that lightness, with which the Pacific war becomes a backdrop for a score, and the simplicity of enemies who are a pattern, not pilots. What has not aged is the roll as a verb: a defense that has a price, because it disarms you for a second.

A thread runs from Capcom of this season to later vertical shooters that still believe in a readable wave and in one special defense. In a seminar, let the students survive a minute without the roll and a minute with it. The difference in breathing is the design, not extra difficulty.

1942 is a 1984 vertically scrolling shooter video game developed and published by Capcom for arcades. 1942 is set in the Pacific Theater of World War II, and is loosely based on the Battle of Midway.

In Japan, Game Machine listed 1942 as the fourth most popular arcade game of December 1984. 1942 was Capcom's breakaway hit, eclipsing in popularity the company's preceding three titles: Vulgus, Sonson, and Pirate Ship Higemaru.
