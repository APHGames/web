A group of heroes travels through epochs in order to avert an apocalypse. Visible enemies, combined attacks, multiple endings, and an elegant time structure minimize the friction of the classic JRPG.

A party travels through epochs to stop an apocalypse, and you see enemies on the map before a battle begins. A random ambush out of nothing is not the basic contract here. Two heroes can perform a combined technique, and there are several endings. A new game plus lets you back in with knowledge, so time is a structure, not just scenery for the plot.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/176-screenshot-1.jpg)

Square assembled the game in 1995 as a collaboration of Hironobu Sakaguchi, Yuji Horii, and Akira Toriyama. Masato Kato held a large part of the scenario, and Yasunori Mitsuda wrote most of the music. The engine is two-dimensional on the SNES in a year when the market is already looking at the PlayStation. With that, Square defends the idea that 16 bits can still carry the most elegant Japanese RPG the studio knows how to make.

The decision to abolish random battles and to shorten the friction has a consequence. The player spends time on the decision of where to go, not on a road that punishes them with a menu. In 1995 this is a late peak of the SNES. Final Fantasy VI a year earlier showed the range; Chrono Trigger shows an economy in which almost every scene belongs somewhere.

Visible enemies turn the map into a plan. Avoiding a fight is a rule, not an exploit, and that is why you hold the tempo of the quest. Little has aged in the skeleton itself. Some late battles are easier than the system could have made them, which is the tax for a game that does not want to grind you down.

What has not aged is the structure of time. The epochs return like rooms of one house, and the player still feels that a change in the past is an act, not a recoloring. Multiple endings are not a trick to make you buy twice. They are a consequence of the quest having more than one way to land, including ways that are not ceremonial.

Compare time with Day of the Tentacle. LucasArts turns it into a puzzle across three years; Square turns it into a quest in which combat and a sentence share a calendar. In a seminar, draw the epochs as floors and draw arrows only where the game actually allows an intervention. The map will be shorter than an open world's, and legible for that very reason.
