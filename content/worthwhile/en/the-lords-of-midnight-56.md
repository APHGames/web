The player leads heroes and armies against Doomdark in a vast fantasy landscape. It joined first-person exploration, strategic war, and thousands of views of the world into a remarkably open system.

The player leads several heroes and an army against Doomdark in a landscape seen from the character's eyes, and at the same time fights a war on a map that is much larger than one view. You can win by campaigning, or by completing a quest. Two victories mean that the world is not a corridor to one boss, but a choice of scale.

Mike Singleton wrote it for Beyond, and it was released on the ZX Spectrum in 1984. One author assembles a system that pretends to be an epic, on a machine with a few kilobytes. The first-person landscape is composed from a small set of images according to height and direction, a technique called landscaping. Thousands of views are not thousands of drawings, but a rule about where you are looking.

The decision to join a panorama and a war map had the consequence that the player switches between what a knight sees and what a commander knows. The information is divided on purpose. Morkin's journey for the Ice Crown and Luxor's army are two tempos of one match. You can neglect one and lose on the other.

In 1984 the British scene can do Manic Miner as a room and Elite as a universe. The Lords of Midnight is a third ambition: a fantasy that wants to be a book, a chess game, and a view all at once. The game is released with a paper aid and with the expectation that the player will be a commander, not just a shooter. The Spectrum audience buys that seriousness.

What has aged is the slowness of switching between characters and battles that are reports, not a scene, if you are expecting an arcade game. What has not aged is the double victory and the feeling that a mountain on the horizon is a real direction, not scenery behind a fight.

Few games on so small a machine offer so large a world without pretending to have painted it square by square. In a seminar, compare one panoramic view with the map. The students should see that both are the same datum, only said differently.

The Lords of Midnight is an epic fantasy video game combining aspects of wargames and graphic adventures, written by Mike Singleton and originally released in 1984 for the . The player must destroy Doomdark, the evil Witchking who has locked the Land of Midnight in perpetual winter.

It was followed by Doomdark's Revenge also in 1984, and Lords of Midnight: The Citadel in 1995. The second is as a wargame, recruiting other lords and their armies until they are strong enough to defeat Doomdark's armies and storm his citadel in the far north.

The game featured a groundbreaking technique called landscaping to depict the lands of Midnight from a first-person perspective. CRASH awarded Lords of Midnight 10 out of 10 in the adventure column, highlighting the panoramic views, detailed units and "wonderfully coherent" storyline.

Lyndsey Paton reviewed The Lords of Midnight for White Dwarf #60, and wrote that "this should provide a sustained challenge for the experienced adventure gamer, and a complex and absorbing introduction to the complete novice". A sequel titled Doomdark's Revenge took place in a land north of Midnight called the Icemark.

Lords of Midnight: The Citadel was released for the PC platform much later, but did not repeat the original's success, as by then graphics had become more advanced. There has also been some work into creating a new modernized version of The Lords of Midnight for iPhones and iPads, with a collaboration between the original author of the game, and the author of the game ports for Windows, Chris Wild.

As part of the release of the ZX Spectrum Next in 2020, a port of The Lords of Midnight was made by Matt Davies and Simon Butler was made based on Singleton's original code.
