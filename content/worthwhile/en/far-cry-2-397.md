A mercenary hunts an arms dealer in a fictional African war. Fire, weapon malfunctions, malaria, and minimal scripting create a "frictional" systemic world instead of a comfortable power fantasy.

In Far Cry 2 you are a mercenary in an unnamed African war and you drive to kill an arms dealer, the Jackal. In between, the savanna burns, a weapon jams, and malaria asks for a pill in the middle of a firefight. Fire spreads through the grass and changes the cover you just had. The map is not a stable arena; it is a material your own shot can destroy.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/397-cover-1.jpg)

Clint Hocking led it creatively at Ubisoft Montreal, and in 2008 it came out on PC and on consoles on the Dunia engine. It is an open immersive sim in a year when the shooter would rather tidy the player into cover. The consequence of the friction, the jams, the illness, and the scarcity of scripts, is a world that does not want you as a power fantasy. The road between missions is content, because a malfunction can stop you, not only an enemy written around the corner.

The missions nevertheless repeat: drive, shoot, drive. That complaint is fair and belongs to the history of the game as much as the fire does. The system is richer than the structure of the tasks that employ it. In 2008, beside Crysis, which offers the suit as power, Far Cry 2 is a sentence about discomfort. Both are outdoor shooters and they do not agree on whether the player should be sovereign.

The repetition of missions and the density of the map have aged into fatigue. The fire, the jamming, and the hostile world have not aged as an argument, and they echo in later systemic shooters. The buddy who pulls you out of unconsciousness is a limited mercy. The game admits that without an occasional rescue the friction would be unbearable, and at the same time it makes that rescue part of the fiction, not a quickload as the only ethic.

For Hocking students it is the second half of the pair with Chaos Theory. There, a quiet architecture. Here, an open friction. Both refuse comfortable power. Diamonds are the currency, and the doctor and the weapon are paid for. The economy is small on purpose, so that every expense hurts more than in a sandbox where money overflows.

The country is unnamed and the war is traffic, not a history lecture. The game thereby avoids one specific front and at the same time carries the complaint that Africa is a systemic stage here. When you play it today, let a weapon reach a jam and put out a fire you started yourself. The whole design of 2008 is in that pair of discomforts.
