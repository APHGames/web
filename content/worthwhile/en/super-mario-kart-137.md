Mario characters race and use items against one another. Karting joined readable racing with "weapons" and comeback mechanics; a new mass subgenre arose.

Characters from Mario drive karts and, instead of pure passing, they throw bananas, fire shells, and expect that second-to-last place is not yet the end. Items are not a reward for leading. They more often help whoever is behind, so the race is social, not only a matter of speed.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/137-screenshot-1.jpg)

Nintendo EAD released the game in 1992 on the SNES. Mode 7, which F-Zero used for serious speed, here spins colorful tracks for several players at one television. The engine rotates the plane of the track and lays car sprites on it. Real terrain with height comes only in later entries; here the depth is a trick that is enough for a drift.

The decision to put weapons into a race has a consequence for the genre. Karting is born as a mass branch in which fairness hides inside chaos, not inside identical cars. In 1992 multiplayer on the SNES is still a rare celebration. Mario Kart turns it into a reason to own more controllers.

Battle mode separates the weapons from the lap. The map is an arena and the goal is not to finish but to hit, which shows that the items are already a game of their own. What has aged is the offer of tracks once you compare it with later entries. The basic idea, though, fits into a few circuits, and there is still something to solve on them.

What has not aged is the comeback contract. A player who does not drive the best still has a reason to hold the controller until the last lap. Speed classes hold the difficulty without a different rule. The same track is a different game when the time to react is shorter.

Compare it with F-Zero. There the boost takes life; here an item takes position from someone else. Both games are Mode 7, but they teach a different envy. In a seminar, watch who gets angry. The anger at a shell is a designed emotion, not a balance error, and the genre will protect it.
