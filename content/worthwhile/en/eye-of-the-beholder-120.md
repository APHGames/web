A party explores the Forgotten Realms in a real-time first-person dungeon. It transferred the dynamics of Dungeon Master into the popular AD&D license and made the real-time crawler substantially more accessible on the PC.

A party of four crawls in real time through the underground of the Forgotten Realms. On a grid you turn, open doors, and strike while time runs. There is no turn that waits for reflection. If you hesitate in a corridor, the enemy catches you, so position is a reflex and a plan.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/120-screenshot-1.jpg)

Westwood developed the game in 1991 for DOS, and SSI published it with the AD&D license. It is their dungeon crawler before the world knows them from strategy. The image is a first-person view by cubes, not the free movement of Ultima Underworld. The engine keeps a legible grid and a fast encounter on a small machine.

The decision to carry the dynamics of Dungeon Master onto a popular license has a consequence. A player who knows Gold Box and turns suddenly has to dodge in time. In 1991 AD&D is a major thing on the PC. Eye of the Beholder pulls it out of a paper menu into a corridor where the wall is visible.

Character portraits and spells keep the language of the role-playing game. Space keeps the language of the maze. Neither layer is perfunctory, but both serve the tempo. What has aged is finding the path and the manual map in your head. Later entries add an automap; here the loss of orientation is part of the tension and of the difficulty.

What has not aged is the party's vulnerability in a narrow corridor. You can still feel that the back row is safe only because the front row is holding the door. Westwood is practicing real time and hostile architecture here. A year later the same studio, in Dune II, moves that time to the surface, to bases and armies.

When you divide dungeon crawlers, separate this branch from Underworld. One is a grid and the pressure of the clock; the other is physics and a free step. The Forgotten Realms license is not just a logo on the box. The names of cities and monsters give the maze a reason that a pure Dungeon Master did not have to borrow from a novel.

Eye of the Beholder is a role-playing video game for personal computers and video game consoles developed by Westwood Associates. The game has two sequels: the first was Eye of the Beholder II: The Legend of Darkmoon, which was also released in 1991; after Westwood Studios was acquired by Virgin Interactive in 1992, Eye of the Beholder III: Assault on Myth Drannor was released in 1993.

The adventurers enter the city's sewer, but the entrance gets blocked by a collapse caused by Xanathar, the eponymous beholder. Once the eponymous beholder is killed, the player would be treated to a small blue window describing that the beholder was killed and that the adventurers returned to the surface where they were treated as heroes.

As a first-person party-based dungeon crawler, Eye of the Beholder takes place in an underground environment rendered from a first-person perspective in pseudo-3D graphics. Eye of the Beholder was reviewed in 1991 in Dragon #171 by Hartley, Patricia, and Kirk Lesser in "The Role of Computers" column, who gave it 5 out of 5 stars.

The One praises Eye of the Beholder's gameplay, stating that "in contrast to previous AD&D titles, there's more emphasis on puzzle-solving than combat – a refreshing change ... Despite this, The One expresses that Eye of the Beholder is on par with Dungeon Master and Chaos Strikes Back, but states that Eye of the Beholder is still "an essential purchase for followers of the AD&D series".

In 1991, PC Format placed Eye of the Beholder on its list of the 50 best computer games of all time. The Eye of the Beholder series overall, including the game's two sequels, reached global sales above 350,000 units by 1996.

In January 1991, SSI participated in Computer Gaming Worlds Top Ad contest and their cover art for Eye of the Beholder came in first place among voting readers, despite the magazine publisher's objection to the piece. From February till October, SSI started up a contest "Beholder Bonus", which required players to find a bonus feature (easter egg) in each level of the game, indicated by an onscreen message.

There were two sequels: Eye of the Beholder II: The Legend of Darkmoon used a modified version of the first game's engine, added outdoor areas and greatly increased the amount of interaction the player had with their environment, along with substantially more 'roleplaying' aspects to the game. Eye of the Beholder III: Assault on Myth Drannor was not developed by Westwood, the developer of Eye of the Beholder and The Legend of Darkmoon, but rather in-house by the publisher SSI.

Eye of the Beholder Trilogy (1995, SSI) was a rerelease of all the three games for MS-DOS on CD-ROM. Several modules for Neverwinter Nights (2002) have been created by fans as remakes of the original Eye of the Beholder game.
