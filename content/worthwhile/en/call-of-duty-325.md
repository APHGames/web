Soldiers of several armies pass through cinematically staged battles of the Second World War. AI comrades and a dense scripted battlefield shifted the FPS from a lone super-soldier toward the illusion of mass war.

In the first Call of Duty you pass through battles of the Second World War as soldiers of several armies. You are not alone on the map; beside you runs a squad that shoots, takes cover, and shouts orders. Scripts open the front in waves: artillery, a landing, a passage down a street. The player is inside a spectacle that has direction, not inside an arena to walk around at will.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/325-cover-1.jpg)

Infinity Ward, a studio founded by people from Medal of Honor: Allied Assault, published it in 2003 through Activision on PC. The engine is a modified id Tech 3. The consequence of the fellow soldiers is an illusion of mass war. The player still decides the aiming, but the image claims that he did not win the battle alone.

In 2003 Medal of Honor is the established war shooter. Call of Duty shifts the genre from a lone hero to the feeling that you are one man inside a script that runs even without you. The linear corridors and the gray-brown palette have aged into a template the series itself will later wear out. The loop of the squadmate and the staged battle became the norm for years ahead.

The American, British, and Soviet campaigns change the weapon and the tone, not the rules. Switching sides is a narrative tool, not three different games. For level students it is a class about the script. The event around the corner is not a simulation of an army; it is a carefully timed trigger, and yet it feels like chaos.

Jason West and Vince Zampella lead the studio toward a campaign that can be finished like a film. Multiplayer exists, but the historical center of gravity of 2003 is in that film. Later games, especially the second and then Modern Warfare, take this illusion and make it an industrial standard. The first is still the rawer and shorter version of the same argument.

When you play the Soviet line today, notice when the game does not give you a rifle at once. Defenselessness in a crowd is a stronger sentence than another firefight. The sound of battle and the squad radio have aged more slowly than the models. They still sell a number the screen cannot actually carry.
