The player pilots a combat helicopter through tactical missions. A significant milestone of "serious" military simulation on home computers.

The pilot of a combat helicopter flies tactical sorties: holds altitude, reads threats, and uses weapons according to what an instrument sees right now, not according to what would look spectacular in a shooter. Failure is often a bad decision about speed or about when to climb, not just a missed target. The machine can kill you even without an enemy shot if you do not listen to it.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/082-cover-1.jpg)

MicroProse published it in 1986 on home computers, led by DOS and the C64. The company of Bill Stealey and Sid Meier builds its identity on a military simulation for the living room, and Gunship is its helicopter chapter, not an arcade port. The cockpit is full of instruments, because a computer of the time can already carry a table of systems, even if the landscape is still simple. The seriousness is in the procedures, not in a photograph of the terrain.

The decision to simulate an attack helicopter as a craft had the consequence that the game needs a manual and a training flight. Anyone who wants immediate action is in the wrong genre, and the program will not adapt to them. Individual missions compose a campaign in which the machine returns damaged and the pilot carries the consequences. It is not one round on a coin.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/082-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 1986 Out Run is selling speed without responsibility beside it. Gunship sells responsibility, and thereby chooses a smaller, more patient audience that MicroProse will feed for a whole decade. It is not a document of a specific war with reportage. It is a model of a machine, set in the hot places of the Cold War, and a lecture ought to keep that distinction.

What has aged is the thickness of the key commands and a landscape that today cannot bear comparison with what the word simulator promises. What has not aged is the respect for a helicopter being a system of sensors and limits, not a sprite with a machine gun.

A direct line runs from MicroProse of this season to later combat simulators on the PC that still begin with a manual. In a seminar, do not force a kill in the first minute. Let the students take off and keep the machine in the air. If they consider that boredom, you have named the boundary of the genre.

Gunship is a combat flight simulation video game developed and published by MicroProse in 1986. Commercially and critically successful, Gunship was followed by Gunship 2000 and Gunship!.

MicroProse intended Gunship to simulate an urban helicopter akin to Blue Thunder, but found that city graphics were too difficult. Gunship was MicroProse's third best-selling Commodore game as of late 1987.

Info magazine gave Gunship for the Commodore 64 five stars out of five, describing it as "without qualification, the best combat flight simulator ever released for an 8-bit computer!" Praising its graphics, weapons, sound, controls, physics, and documentation, the magazine concluded that it was "PRIME!" Computer Gaming World stated "this reviewer heartily recommends Gunship ... Gunship received the Origins Award for "Best Screen Graphics in a Home Computer Game of 1986".

In 1987, Computer Gaming World named Gunship as its action game of the year, in 1988 the game joined the magazine's Hall of Fame for games highly rated over time by readers, and in 1996, the magazine ranked it as the 102nd best game of all time. A follow-up game, Gunship 2000, was released by MicroProse for several platforms between 1991 and 1996.
