An undercover police officer completes tasks in automobile cities inspired by 1970s films. Cinematic driving and open city maps represent an important intermediate step toward 3D GTA.

Tanner, an undercover police officer, completes jobs in cities that want to be the automotive 1970s, and between missions you can just drive, which in 1999 is still a novelty. Before the game lets you into the city, it wants a list of stunts in a garage: a slide, a spin, braking. A number of players never cross that threshold, and the tutorial becomes a wall that says more about the game than the first mission.

What has aged is the garage control scheme as an entrance exam and the repetition of missions that are variations on drive and do not get caught. What has not aged is the camera of the chase and the city as a lot, not as a corridor. In seminar, let students say in which minute of the garage they would turn the game off. That minute is a lesson in when a tutorial stops teaching and starts selecting an audience.

Against Gran Turismo there is little respect for the car here and a great deal of respect for the shot. The simulation is in how a slide looks in the camera, not in what the gearbox is. Edmondson's team builds an intermediate step. It is historically valuable exactly as an intermediate step: without it the jump from top-down GTA to Liberty City would look like a miracle from nothing.

Open driving between missions is a promise the missions often do not keep. A seminar should name that contradiction, because the genre will be learning from it for a long time. The game wants you to know how to drive before the story starts. That is the opposite contract from games that teach you driving in the first hour, and that is why so many people stayed in the garage.

Driver (subtitled "You Are the Wheelman" in North America) is an action driving video game and the first installment in the Driver series. The game's success led to further sequels, including Driver 2 in November 2000 and Driver 3 in June 2004.

It also bears significant thematic resemblances to the 20th Century Fox movie The Driver (1978). NYPD detective and former racing driver John Tanner is sent undercover by Lieutenant McKenzie to investigate a crime syndicate; McKenzie instructs Tanner to go to Miami and meet a pimp named Rufus.

After arriving in Miami, Tanner uses his driving skills to prove himself to some gangsters in a parking garage, and becomes their getaway driver. Series creator Martin Edmonson was inspired by the very first film he saw at the cinema, 1978 crime thriller The Driver, with the tutorial level being directly lifted from a scene in the film.

Jeff Lundrigan reviewed the PlayStation version of the game for Next Generation, rating it four stars out of five, and stated that "a movie buff's dream – but Driver is still great even if you aren't big on movies". Perry said of the original PlayStation game: "In the history of driving games for PlayStation, there is nothing that comes close to the comprehensive, deep, and thoroughly pleasurable experience that's embedded deep in the heart of Driver [...] It fulfills driving enthusiasts' deepest desires to drive as fast as possible through major US cities and to slam into just about anything without any repercussions.

At the 1999 E3 Game Critics Awards, Driver won "Best Racing Game", and in 2002 it was ranked No. The Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences named Driver as a finalist for "Console Game of the Year" and "Console Racing Game of the Year" during the 3rd Annual Interactive Achievement Awards.
