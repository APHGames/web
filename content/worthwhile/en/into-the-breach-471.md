Mechs protect cities from the Vek on small turn-based maps. Enemy attacks are known in advance; the game is therefore an almost deterministic chess puzzle about manipulating the future.

In Into the Breach you deploy mechs on a small grid and, before the turn, you see where the Vek will strike. The player therefore does not guess a probability as in XCOM; they move a future blow, push an enemy into its own attack, and protect buildings whose hit points are the score. A round is short and almost deterministic. Randomness is in what the next map brings, not in whether your cannon hits the tile you chose.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/471-cover-1.jpg)

Subset Games, Justin Ma and Matthew Davis, released the game in February 2018 as the opposite lesson to FTL. There, there was fog and fire; here, there is a chessboard and perfect information. The decision to show the enemy attack in advance changes the genre from a tactical bet into a puzzle. The consequence is that a loss feels like your blindness, not like a betrayal by the die, and so people return to the turn in their heads even after the match.

The pilot who survives the fall of a timeline is the only persistent reward. The studio thereby keeps roguelike progression without having to inflate mech statistics forever. Buildings are not scenery. Every damaged city tile is a point you will not get back, so saving civilians is literally geometry.

In 2018 this is a small strategy game that gets cited whenever someone promises a fair tactical game. Beside the big 4X titles it reminds you that tension can last five turns. Islands and evacuation goals give a run an arc. There is no campaign with cutscenes; there is the repeated question of which city you will let crack today.

The variety has aged beside the deckbuilders that arrived immediately afterward. After dozens of runs you recognize typical Vek patterns, which is the tax of readability. Perfect information holds. It is still the cleanest example of how to turn a future you can see into a toy, not into a cutscene you merely endure.

Different mech teams are different grammars of displacement. One team pushes, another pulls, a third sacrifices position, and the grid stays the same. In class, set it immediately after a game with hidden percentages. Students should name which loss is theirs and which belonged to the generator.

Into the Breach is a turn-based strategy video game developed and published by indie studio Subset Games, consisting of Jay Ma and Matthew Davis. Into the Breach is their second game, following FTL: Faster Than Light.

Into the Breach is set in the far future where humanity fights against an army of giant monsters collectively called the Vek. Into the Breach came out of ideas that Ma and Davis had following the success of FTL.

Subset said at the time of its reveal that Into the Breach was still a ways off from release, as they had enough in place to show off the game's ideas and preliminary art, but they still lack a lot of content for the game, and prefer to perfect the game at their own pace. Ben Prunty, who had composed the FTL soundtrack, provided the music for Into the Breach.

Into the Breach received praise upon its release, holding a 90 out of 100 on review aggregation site Metacritic Critics lauded the simple but strategic gameplay, high difficulty, artstyle and soundtrack.
