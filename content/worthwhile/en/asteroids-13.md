A ship with momentum destroys asteroids in open space. Vector imagery, inertia, and a wrap-around arena created an extraordinarily clean systems design.

A ship with inertia rotates in an open field, shoots asteroids, and a large rock splits into smaller ones that are more dangerous precisely because there are more of them. The screen wraps: what flies off the left returns from the right. Escape is not the edge of the map, but a bad estimate of a vector.

Ed Logg programmed it for Atari in 1979; the prompt came from Lyle Rains, who needed a new game for the vector hardware after Lunar Lander. A vector monitor draws lines, not pixels, which is why the rocks, the ship, and the shots are outlines. Hitting a large object is a clean geometry problem.

The decision to leave the ship's physics almost without friction had the consequence that stopping is as hard as accelerating, and the space punishes panic. The flying saucer shoots more accurately than you expect, and hyperspace is a rescue that can drop you straight into a rock. Escape has the same grammar as in Spacewar!.

In 1979 Space Invaders stands beside it as a marching grid. Asteroids answers with the opposite: emptiness, vectors, and a player who chooses their own direction. It is a peak of Atari's coin-operated vector school, not a home game. The score and the record table make it a sport of the arcade venue.

What has aged is the mute line where you now expect an explosion and a camera. Anyone who does not know vectors sees poverty first. What has not aged is the cleanliness of the systems: mass, angle, wrap, and a growing number of threats from one good hit.

A thread runs from this ship to lateral shooters and to every game that takes inertia seriously. In a seminar, set it beside Spacewar!. You will see what happens when university physics is trimmed to one player and a score.

Asteroids is a 1979 multidirectional shooter video game developed and published by Atari, Inc. The object of the game is to shoot and destroy the asteroids and saucers while avoiding colliding with either or being hit by the saucers' counterfire.

Asteroids was conceived and designed by Lyle Rains and Ed Logg, who decided to use hardware developed by Wendi Allen (then known as Howard Delman) that was previously used for Lunar Lander. Asteroids was based on an unfinished game titled Cosmos; its physics model, control scheme, and gameplay elements were derived from Spacewar!, Computer Space, and Space Invaders and refined through trial and error.

Asteroids was one of the first major hits of the golden age of arcade games; the game sold 47,840 upright cabinets and 8,725 cocktail cabinets, and proved both popular with players and influential with developers. Each level starts with multiple large asteroids drifting across the screen.

As the player shoots asteroids, they break into smaller asteroids that move faster and are more difficult to hit. Once the screen has been cleared of all asteroids and flying saucers, a new set of large asteroids appears, thus starting the next level.

The game gets harder as the number of asteroids increases until after the score reaches a range between 40,000 and 60,000. Paul Mancuso joined the development team as Asteroids technician and engineer Wendi Allen contributed to the hardware.

Logg received Allen's modified board with five buttons, 13 sound effects, and additional RAM, and he used it to develop Asteroids. Asteroids was released for the Atari VCS (later renamed Atari 2600) and Atari 8-bit computers in 1981.

Asteroids was so popular that some video arcade operators had to install large boxes to hold the number of coins spent by players, Asteroids went on to become the highest-grossing arcade video game of 1980 in the United States, dethroning Space Invaders. Atari manufactured 76,312 units from its US and Ireland plants, including 21,394 Asteroids Deluxe units.

Asteroids received positive reviews from critics, and has been regarded as Logg's magnum opus. [...] If blasting asteroids is the thing you want to do then this is the game, but at this price I can't wholeheartedly recommend it".

In 1999, Next Generation listed Asteroids as number 29 on their "Top 50 Games of All Time", commenting that "Asteroids was a classic the day it was released, and it has never lost any of its appeal". Asteroids was ranked fourth on Retro Gamer list of "Top 25 Arcade Games"; the Retro Gamer staff cited its simplicity and the lack of a proper ending as allowances of revisiting the game.

In 2012, Asteroids was listed on Time All-Time 100 greatest video games list. Entertainment Weekly named Asteroids one of the top ten games for the Atari 2600 in 2013.

In 2021, The Guardian listed Asteroids as the second greatest video game of the 1970s, just below Galaxian (1979). By contrast, in March 1983 the Atari 8-bit port of Asteroids won sixth place in Softline Dog of the Year awards "for badness in computer games", Atari division, based on reader submissions.

The Atari Flashback series of dedicated video game consoles have included both the 2600 and the arcade versions of Asteroids. Asteroids Hyper 64 made the ship and asteroids 3D, and added new weapons and a multiplayer mode.

A technical demo of Asteroids was developed by iThink for the Atari Jaguar but was never released. Unofficially referred to as Asteroids 2000, it was demonstrated at E-JagFest 2000.

Different versions of Asteroids were included in several Atari games compilations, such as Atari Anniversary Edition (2001) for the Dreamcast, PlayStation, and Microsoft Windows, Atari Anthology (2003) for both Xbox and PlayStation 2, Atari Greatest Hits Volume 1 (2010) for the Nintendo DS, Atari Collection 1 and 2 (2020) for the Evercade, and Atari 50 (2022) for the Atari VCS, Nintendo Switch, PlayStation 4, PlayStation 5, Windows, Xbox One, and Xbox Series X/S. The Xbox Live Arcade port of Asteroids, released in November 2007, has revamped HD graphics along with an added intense "throttle monkey" mode.

In 2005 Asteroids was released for the Game Boy Advance with Pong and Yars' Revenge also being included on the same package. A remake, Asteroids: Recharged, was released in December 2021 for the Nintendo Switch, PlayStation 4, PlayStation 5, Windows, Xbox One, and Xbox Series X/S, developed by Adamvision Studios and SneakyBox and published by Atari.

Quality Software's Asteroids in Space (1980) was one of the best selling games for the Apple II and voted one of the most popular software titles of 1978–80 by Softalk magazine. In December 1981, Byte reviewed eight Asteroids clones for home computers.

In July 2009, Universal Pictures offered Roland Emmerich the option to direct the film adaptation of Asteroids, with Matt Lopez writing the script and Lorenzo di Bonaventura producing the film adaptation. On July 23, 2026 Atari signed a major development deal with Universal Pictures and Entertainment 360 to adapt 10 classic video games into feature films, including Asteroids.
