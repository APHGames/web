Amanda Ripley survives a space station while pursued by a Xenomorph. The Xenomorph is not a rigidly scripted jump-scare machine; a combination of AI layers creates constant uncertainty.

Amanda Ripley in Alien: Isolation survives Sevastopol station, and most of the time the player is not attacking but listening to the motion tracker, hiding under a table, and deciding whether to make a sound at all. The xenomorph is not a wave of enemies. It is one presence because of which you rewrite the path to the next terminal, while androids and humans are different, more predictable threats.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/439-screenshot-1.jpg)

Creative Assembly, the studio known for Total War, released the game through Sega in October 2014. It deliberately returns to the 1979 film Alien, not to the action sequels, and it builds Amanda as Ellen Ripley's daughter. The creature's artificial intelligence is layered: the being's senses and a higher direction are not supposed to behave like one scripted jump scare. The consequence the studio wants is uncertainty about whether the corridor is really looking for you right now.

The decision to refuse a reliable weapon against the main threat means the tool in your hand often only changes the problem. A flamethrower and noise are bets, not wins. Saving at stations is deliberately inconvenient. The studio brings back a horror punishment for the greed that, in modern action, would want a quick save anywhere.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/439-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 2014 this is a risky big horror without the shooting fantasy many people expect from the Alien license. The aesthetic of CRT monitors and analog interfaces is part of the fear and of the respect for 1979. Sevastopol is long and backtracking. The game believes that exhaustion from repeated hiding is the genre, not a pacing mistake, and in that it misses part of the audience.

The length has aged, as have some stretches where the threats rotate mechanically. Some of the human enemies are also more ordinary than the creature you came for. The uncertainty of the xenomorph holds, and so does the fidelity to the film's design of objects. It is still the measure for a horror that promises the monster is not an edit.
