American George Stobbart unravels a Templar conspiracy in Europe. A "serious cartoon" aesthetic and elegant point-and-click UX rank among the peaks of the European adventure.

George Stobbart, an American tourist, becomes a witness to an assassination in a Paris café and then clicks across Europe on the trail of the Templars; the work is conversation, examining a place, and using an object, not combat. The controls are point-and-click with a cleaner cursor than the verb coins of some older adventures. The action follows from the object and the place, not from a menu of verbs across half the screen.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/214-cover-1.jpg)

Charles Cecil and Revolution Software made it in York, and Virgin published it in 1996 on PC and PlayStation. It is a European adventure with an American hero who gets lost in Europe on purpose. It stands on the Virtual Theatre engine, which Revolution had already used for Lure of the Temptress and Beneath a Steel Sky. Characters move around the scene on their own; the world is not only a static painting with hotspots.

The decision to play a serious conspiracy in a drawn style had a consequence for the tone. The game is not a parody and it is not dark realism either; it is a thriller that can carry color and still hold the fear of a historical cult. Nico Collard is not a prize at the end of a puzzle. She is a partner with her own profession, and the relationship of the two characters carries the series further than the Templars themselves.

In 1996 the classic adventure is still a major genre on PC, but it already feels the pressure of 3D. Broken Sword bets on painting, voice acting, and places you recognize as Paris, Ireland, or Syria. What has aged are some puzzles whose logic belongs to the author more than to the world, which is a tax of the genre, not an accident of one puzzle.

The willingness to take a cartoon as a vehicle for an adult plot has not aged, and neither has the courtesy of an interface that does not shout with its inventory. In seminar, put it beside Grim Fandango two years later. You will see two answers to the question of what an adventure should look like once a verb menu and a painted background are no longer enough.

Cecil's team shows here that a European studio can take a global conspiracy plot and still anchor it in particular streets. For design students, the courtesy of the cursor is useful. The player should be testing the world, not fighting a menu of verbs.
