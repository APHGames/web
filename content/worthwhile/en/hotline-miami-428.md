A masked killer completes brutal missions in a hallucinogenic Miami. Instant restart changes death from a punishment into a rhythm of iteration; the violence is at once intoxicating and thematically unsettling.

In Hotline Miami you have a mask, one hit drops both you and an enemy, and after death you begin the room again almost immediately. The player plans the floor plan like a puzzle and then breaks it in half a second of violence. Weapons on the floor are sentences with an expiration date. You picked up a knife, a gun, or you go with bare hands because someone in the corner has not managed to fire yet.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/428-cover-1.jpg)

Dennaton, that is Jonatan Söderström and Dennis Wedin, released the game through Devolver Digital in October 2012. Söderström comes from freely distributed games, and Hotline Miami carries that economy: a small team, a sharp tone, no open map. The top-down view and the grid of rooms recall older action games, but the restart is a modern decision about rhythm. Death does not cost a load, so the experiment is cheap and brutality becomes iteration.

The consequence is a game that forces you to be fast and, after a chapter, also asks whether you like it. The violence is intoxicating in the controls and unpleasant in the theme, and the studio does not hide that conflict behind a realistic war. Masks change the rules, for example the animals' vision or a harder strike. They are modifiers that turn the campaign into a set of variants of the same floor plan.

In 2012 this is an independent shot against blockbuster shooters. Synthwave, Miami, and the dirty palette are a period style that immediately becomes quotable. The story is deliberately broken and, in the second half, less and less certain. Whom the phone calls, and who is in the room, is part of the vertigo, not a detective story with a clean solution.

That story ending has aged, as have some of the references that belong to the internet culture of 2012. Some levels are also a puzzle with one fragile solution. The rhythm of death and restart holds. It is still the cleanest example of how to turn punishment into a metronome.

Sound and blocks of color make the readability. In the chaos from above you can still tell who is armed, which is the condition of a fair instant restart. In class, use it for violence as form: ask what happens to a moral reading when the game rewards a perfect slaughter and then reproaches you for it.
