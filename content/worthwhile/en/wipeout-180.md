Antigravity machines race futuristic tracks to electronic music. A club aesthetic, licensed electronica, and technical design helped the PlayStation reach a more adult audience.

In an antigravity craft you drive a futuristic track, and you handle weapons and the shield at the same speed that throws you out of the first corner if you do not read it. The visual is not an accompaniment. The Designers Republic holds the typography, the color, and the logo so that the game looks like a club poster, not like a toy.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/180-cover-1.jpg)

Nick Burcombe and Psygnosis prepared the game in 1995 in Liverpool on the PlayStation. The European release carries licensed electronica, among other names Leftfield, The Chemical Brothers, and Orbital. The engine is a polygonal race on a new console that needs to distinguish itself from a childish image of games. Wipeout is an argument that the PlayStation belongs to a different age of audience.

The decision to join the race to a club aesthetic has a consequence off the track. The advertising, the box, and the soundtrack say the same thing as the controls: this is not Mario Kart. In 1995, in the window of the Western PlayStation launch, this is, beside Twisted Metal, proof that the new console can be hard and stylized. One game fights with cars; this one fights with speed and design.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/180-poster-2.jpg)

The difficulty is part of the identity. The track does not forgive and the musical tempo does not wait, so the master is not the one who collects items, but the one who finishes at all. What has aged is precisely that strictness when you take the game as a party race. It is not built so that every guest finishes; it is built to look inaccessible and then relent.

What has not aged is the art direction. Even when the racers' models age, you still recognize the graphic system of the box from a distance. Psygnosis, a studio with an Amiga history, moves here onto the console that will make it more famous than floppies did. Liverpool is not scenery; it is the European origin of the image.

Compare the drift with Ridge Racer. Namco teaches the slide as an arcade craft; Wipeout teaches the line as a style in which a weapon is part of the poster. In a seminar, lay the box beside a shot from the track. If they speak the same language, the design is consistent even where the car's physics are of their period.

Wipeout (stylised as wipE′out″) is a 1995 racing video game developed and published by Psygnosis for the PlayStation. The first installment in the Wipeout series, it was a launch title for the PlayStation in Europe.

Unique at the time, Wipeout was noted for its futuristic setting, weapons designed to slow opponents and its marketing campaign designed by Keith Hopwood and The Designers Republic. Wipeout is a racing game that is set in 2052, where players compete in the F3600 anti-gravity racing league.

Wipeout features a multiplayer mode using the PlayStation Link Cable, allowing two player to race against each other and the six remaining AI competitors. Wipeout was developed and published by Liverpudlian developer Psygnosis (later known as Studio Liverpool), with production starting in the second half of 1994.

The name "Wipeout" was given to the game during a pub conversation, and was inspired by the instrumental song "Wipe Out" by The Surfaris. The poster branded Wipeout "a dangerous game", with Wipeout s lead artist Neil Thompson suggesting&mdash;and designer Nick Burcome denying&mdash;that the "E" in Wipeout stood for ecstasy.

Wipeout was ported to the Sega Saturn in 1996 by Tantalus Entertainment. The development of Wipeout placed a strong emphasis on its music, which was a key component of the game's identity.

The game's advertising reinforced this connection to club culture, depicting players continuing the party atmosphere at home with Wipeout and positioning it as the “afterparty” game. Edge cited that it was hard to criticise "such a beautifully realised and well-produced game which [exploited] the PlayStation's power so well", but did show similar concerns over the game's longevity regarding its "reliance on track-based power-ups" that would "limit Wipeout's lifespan" in comparison to Super Mario Kart.

GamePro gave the PlayStation version a rave review, predicting that "Wipeout's taut action and grueling courses will lure many diehard racing fans to this new system." They particularly praised the challenging gameplay and precise controls. Maximum opined that of all the games in the PlayStation's European launch line-up, "not one title can match up to the awesome nature of Psygnosis' WipeOut.

The game's initial success led to Psygnosis developing several sequels which would later become part of the Wipeout franchise. A direct sequel, Wipeout 2097, was released for the PlayStation and Sega Saturn in 1996, which was met with positive reviews, especially aimed towards the vastly improved game engine and new physics the game offered.

A Nintendo 64 spin-off, Wipeout 64, was released in 1998 and was met with considerable praise from critics, but was noted to be too similar to the original Wipeout. Wipeout has been described as being synonymous with Sony's debut gaming hardware and as an early showcase for 3D graphics in console gaming.

In 2016, game journalist Luke Plunkett wrote "the visual influence the game has had is staggering" and "its bright, neo-Tokyo style still being admired today (you can see echoes of it in everything from Mass Effect to Mario Kart 8 to Destiny)." In 2023, an art book entitled WipEout: Futurism was announced to be published in 2024, focused on commemorating the game's artistic and graphic style. Based on this leaked code, there were two source ports by enthusiasts: WipeOut Phantom Edition for Microsoft Windows, which is closed source and wipEout Rewrite for Windows, macOS, Linux and WebAssembly by Dominic Szablewski, which is source-available.
