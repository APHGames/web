Large teams fight in an open landscape with jetpacks. "Skiing," verticality, and team roles were ahead of later large-scale online shooters.

On a large map you pick a loadout at a station, turn on the jetpack, and in capture the flag the winner is whoever can pull speed out of a slope, the speed that came to be called skiing. Skiing came out of the physics of sliding, and players made it the main skill. A design that did not count on it as a tutorial eventually has to treat it as the language of the game.

Dynamix of Eugene, Oregon released it in 1998 with Sierra, under the name Starsiege: Tribes. A campaign exists, and it is not the reason to remember the game; the reason is a team in an open landscape. The engine carries outdoor terrain, vehicles, turrets, and deployables at a time when most FPS games still sell a room. The network game is the assumption, not a mode after the story.

The decision to give everyone a jetpack and a large map had a consequence for roles. Someone defends the flag, someone carries it, someone builds, and a deathmatch shooter becomes a small sporting society. In 1998, as Quake teaches the duel and Half-Life is only just teaching the campaign, Tribes is an early argument for a large team space. The industry will arrive there later through other games and other player counts.

What has aged is the graphics and the loadout interface, which wants the patience of a LAN party. What has not aged is movement as a dialect and the flag run as a composition of roles, not as a chase of everyone against everyone. In seminar, draw one successful flag run as the cooperation of three people. Students will see that a map without roles is only a large empty arena.

The loadout at the station is an economy before the round. Death returns you to a choice and turns the respawn into tactics, not a punishment of ten seconds of boredom. Against Counter-Strike, which will arrive as a mod a year later, Tribes is about movement and terrain, while Counter-Strike will be about the angle and about money. Both schools of the team shooter are already standing in those years.

Verticality is a rule, because a jetpack without height is only a jump. Level design here has to count on the sky as a road. The game is useful as an example of a technique the community names and thereby finishes the design. Skiing is not in the tutorial, and it is still the game.

It is the first of the Tribes video game series and follows the story from Metaltech: Earthsiege and Starsiege. An expansion pack, Tribes Extreme, was cancelled; it was supposed to add single-player missions, multiplayer maps, and bot AI.

Conflict has broken out between several factions of humans, the four largest of which are the Children of the Phoenix, who descend from people isolated from the Great Human Empire during the "jumpgate diaspora", Blood Eagle, a force of imperial knights originally sent from the Empire to subdue Humans who have gone 'tribal' over time, and Diamond Sword and Starwolf, who are of secondary importance, with innumerable other splinter tribes constantly fighting for territory. The player assumes the role of a warrior loyal to one of the four major tribes battling in the front lines of the conflict.

Next Generation said, "Tribes has the design and the technology to be the next standard in Internet action gaming. The Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences named Starsiege: Tribes the winner for "Online Action/Strategy Game of the Year" at the 2nd Annual Interactive Achievement Awards; it was also nominated for "PC Action Game of the Year" and "Computer Entertainment Title of the Year", although it ultimately lost both awards to Half-Life.

PC Gamer US awarded the game its 1999 "Special Achievement in Innovation" prize; the staff raved that "Tribes re-wrote the rules of online combat", and that it features "airtight networking code and what is still today one of the best self-contained online interfaces we've ever seen." It was a finalist for the magazine's "Best Multiplayer Game" award, as well as PC PowerPlay s "Best Online Only" award, but lost both of them to Team Fortress Classic. Sierra licensed the franchise to Irrational Games for a third installment, Tribes: Vengeance, which was released in October 2004.

Vivendi Universal released Starsiege: Tribes and Tribes 2 for free on May 4, 2004 on a DVD-ROM with Computer Gaming World magazine and on FilePlanet in order to promote the release of Tribes: Vengeance.
