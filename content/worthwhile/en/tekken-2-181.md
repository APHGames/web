The King of Iron Fist continues with an expanded roster and techniques. It sped up and deepened the original limb-based combat and became an early demonstration of the quality of PS1 arcade ports.

In the King of Iron Fist sequel the roster is wider and the movement faster. You still press four limbs, but the sentence you compose from them is longer, and the punishment for the wrong leg comes sooner. The new characters are not just more costumes. They add wrestling and comic silhouettes, so the tournament stops being only a school of one karate.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/181-poster-1.jpg)

Namco built the game in the arcade in 1995 and soon carried it to the PlayStation. The home version is early proof that a console can carry a 3D fighting game almost as the arcade promised it. The engine continues from System 11. The higher fluidity is not decoration; it is the condition for a faster neutral game to be readable at all.

The decision to speed up and expand, instead of inventing new buttons, has a consequence. The limb philosophy of the first game turns out to be deep enough to carry a sequel, not just a demo. In 1995 this is Namco's answer to Virtua Fighter 2. Sega defends stance and the throw; Tekken 2 defends tempo and a growing cast that people begin to know by name.

The PlayStation port in 1996 will be, for many, the first contact. For the design, though, the arcade comes first, where the speed was tested on a coin, not on a console box. What has aged are some infinite situations and a throw that can shorten a match into a puzzle only one player at the cabinet knows.

What has not aged is the readability of which limb is working at the moment. Even at speed you still sense what you promised by the press. Training and the rematch hold the life of the sport. The single-player mode is a parade; the real depth is that the same character looks different in two pairs of hands.

Compare the first and the second as a lecture on when a control scheme becomes a series. The four buttons are no longer an experiment; they are an expectation. In a seminar, let a student move from the first game straight into the second. The difference in tempo is the whole argument for why a sequel need not change the rule in order to be a new game.
