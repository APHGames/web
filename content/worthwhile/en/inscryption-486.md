A card roguelike in a cabin gradually reveals several further layers of its fiction. It rewrites its own rules, genre, and software-fiction frame, and it uses knowledge of the previous "act" as a tool of surprise.

In Inscryption you sit in a cabin opposite Leshy and sacrifice your own cards so that others are even allowed onto the table. The player solves the cabin as an escape room between matches and gradually discovers that the card roguelike is only the first floor of a fiction that will exchange the rules, the genre, and the software around you. Blood, squirrels, and the scale on the board are the first grammar. Once you know it, the game takes it away and gives you another board, in which knowledge of the previous act is the surprise, not a tutorial.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/486-cover-1.jpg)

Daniel Mullins, after Pony Island and The Hex, released the game through Devolver Digital in October 2021. It is still a small production that pretends to be a broken program and thereby continues his older games about software that lies. The decision to rewrite its own frame in the middle of the game means the first hour is a contract the author cancels. The consequence is a strong one-time shock, and a card layer that has to be good enough to hold you even after you know the cabin is not the whole truth.

Sacrifices on the table turn a deckbuilder into a horror about cost. You are not only an optimizer of numbers; you are a person feeding the opponent your own pieces. The ARG and the recordings around the game extend the fiction beyond the window. The studio counts on a player who has already seen how Mullins breaks an interface and still wants to catch them again.

In 2021 this is the cleanest example of a game that uses knowledge of a genre as ammunition. Beside honest deckbuilders of the Slay the Spire type, it is a trap for people who think they already understand the form. The second and third acts change the camera, the economy, and who the narrator is. They are not bonus modes; they are the continuation of the argument.

That shock of the rewrite has aged, and it works fully only the first time. The card cabin, by contrast, holds even on a repeated run, because sacrifice and the scale are still a game. The courage to throw away its own tutorial holds. It is still a teaching text on how an act can be a change of software, not only a new chapter of the map.

Leshy as gamemaster has rules that cheat on their own. The opponent is a designer, which is the point, not only a character joke. In class, let students finish at least through the first break and then ask which rules the group considered fixed. That firmness was scenery.
