A tank fights in a vector landscape from the first person. One of the key early precedents of a 3D first-person combat space.

The player sits in a tank and looks through a periscope into a wireframe landscape, searching for enemy machines, missiles, and obstacles. The radar and the gunsight are two pieces of information at once: the world is three-dimensional, but you see a frame of it and a dot on an instrument.

Ed Rotberg programmed it for Atari, and the machine was released in 1980 as vector first person, in a cabinet the player leans into as if into a turret. A vector generator draws the horizon, the volcano, the cubes, and the enemies as lines in depth. It is not a sprite that grows, but a scene calculated in space.

The decision to hide the player behind a periscope had the consequence that the controls are a role, not just a joystick on a panel. The body plays a soldier before the first shot is fired. Battlezone is a 1980 first-person shooter tank combat video game developed and published by Atari, Inc. Battlezone is considered to be one of the greatest video games of all time.

Battlezone is housed in an upright full-sized arcade cabinet with a "periscope" viewfinder. He cited Atari's top-down arcade shooter game Tank (1974) as the primary inspiration behind Battlezone, essentially a 3D version of that game.

While Battlezone also has similarities to a first-person tank simulation for the PLATO system, Panther, Rotberg said he had never played that game before, but had heard of it; he stated that it "may have inspired whoever originally suggested the idea at the brainstorming meeting where it was proposed, but I seriously doubt it". In the 1980s, Battlezone was ported to the Apple II, Atari 2600, Commodore 64, VIC-20, IBM PC compatibles, ZX Spectrum, and later the Atari 8-bit computers and Atari ST.

Although not as successful as Asteroids, Battlezone eventually produced another 15,000 sales for Atari. Battlezone was well received, earning an Honorable Mention for "Best Commercial Arcade Game" in 1982 at the Third Annual Arkie Awards.

David and Sandy Small called it "addictive" and mentioned the Battlezone Tunnel Vision, which makes the players drive strangely during rush hour. Computer and Video Games magazine awarded the Spectrum version of Battlezone 30 out of 40 points, praising its technical achievements such as the implementation of hidden line removal.

A version called The Bradley Trainer (also known as Army Battlezone or Military Battlezone) was designed for use by the U.S. Approaching Atari in December 1980, some developers within Atari refused to work on the project because of its association with the Army, most notably original Battlezone programmer Ed Rotberg.
