BattleMech pilots wage a war of the clans in heavy combat machines. The combination of mecha systems simulation with 3D action created an iconic PC cockpit experience.

In the cockpit of a BattleMech you overheat weapons, turn the torso separately from the legs, and accept that the machine is slow on purpose. Speed is not the promise; mass is. You fire weapon groups in steps. A salvo of everything at once is a decision about heat, not about your having many buttons.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/189-cover-1.jpg)

Activision released the game in 1995 on DOS with the BattleTech license. The Clan war gives the campaign two sides and machines that are not just skins of a tank. The engine is three-dimensional, in software and with early acceleration. The cockpit is the interface: radar, heat, and limb damage are the game, not a HUD decoration.

The decision to simulate heat and the torso has a consequence. A player who stands front-on and fires loses to a machine that can present only a shoulder and hide the rest. In 1995, beside the BattleTech board games, this is the PC translation of a licensed fantasy. It does not want to be an arcade shooter with a large robot; it wants you to drive a system.

Missions can be opaque in navigation. The map and the objectives are austere in the manner of the period, so part of the tension is in whether you are walking the right ridge at all. What has aged is precisely that navigation and the image once you compare it with later entries. The feeling of a step that shakes the camera has aged less.

What has not aged is the contract that a weapon has a price in heat. It is still strategic not to shoot, which a pure Doom will not teach. The Clan sides hold the campaign as a choice of identity, not as a tutorial and then a mirror. The machine you are given is politics, not just a reward.

Compare the cockpit with X-Wing. There, energy moves among three systems; here it is heat, torso, and a salvo, but both games believe that procedure is entertainment. In a seminar, let a player overheat the machine on purpose. The lesson about the limit is faster than a weapon table in the manual.

MechWarrior 2: 31st Century Combat is a vehicle simulation game developed and published by Activision, released in 1995 as part of the MechWarrior series of video games in the BattleTech franchise. The game was originally developed for MS-DOS and was ported to Windows, Classic Mac&nbsp;OS, and the Saturn and PlayStation consoles as MechWarrior&nbsp;2: Arcade Combat Edition.

MechWarrior 2: 31st Century Combat received a follow-up expansion pack called MechWarrior&nbsp;2: Ghost Bear's Legacy and a stand-alone expansion titled MechWarrior 2: Mercenaries. The game was originally developed for MS-DOS but was ported to other platforms including Windows, Apple Macintosh, and the game consoles Sega Saturn and Sony PlayStation (as MechWarrior&nbsp;2: Arcade Combat Edition).

Gregory Fulton began his career in the video game industry as a tester on MechWarrior&nbsp;2, and later served as the lead game designer of Heroes of Might and Magic&nbsp;III. MechWarrior 2: Ghost Bear's Legacy is the follow-up expansion pack for MechWarrior&nbsp;2 that was released in November 1995.

MechWarrior 2: Mercenaries was released in September 1996 as a stand-alone expansion to MechWarrior&nbsp;2: 31st Century Combat; it is the last BattleTech game made by Activision.
