Thirty futuristic machines race at high speed. It sacrificed graphic detail for stable speed and an enormous peloton — design subordinated to the readability of movement.

Thirty machines on a track that is a ribbon, a tube, or a jump into the void, and a fall means an explosion. You are not driving a race for pretty form; you are driving to read the next corner at sixty frames a second. The machines are almost without textures. Nintendo sacrificed surface so that the field would stay large and the frame rate stable, and the compromise is the look of the game.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/239-cover-1.jpg)

Nintendo EAD released it in 1998, and Shigeru Miyamoto produced it. The N64 at that time can do richer textures in other games, and here you consciously do not get them. The decision to subordinate graphics to the readability of movement had a consequence that outlived the hardware. The game is still fast even in an emulator, because the speed was a rule, not a motion-blur effect.

The Death Race and the individual cups are different contracts over the same physics. One time you drive a championship, the next time survival, and the track does not turn into a simulation of a real circuit. In Japan an expansion kit for the 64DD also came out, with a track editor. The main game did without it, and the editor shows that the series already wanted the track as a player's tool, not only as content.

In 1998, when consoles are competing in films and textures, F-Zero X is almost ascetic. The asceticism is the argument: thirty opponents are more expensive information than a poster on the wall. What has aged is the barrenness for a player who wants a world, and the N64 controller in a long corner. What has not aged is the speed and the imagination of tracks that a road simulation would not permit.

In seminar, hide the number of machines from students for a moment and let them drive an empty track. Without the pack the design collapses, and that names what was sacrificed to the texture. Against Wave Race or other showcases of the N64, this game is an anti-showcase. It teaches that a showcase can also be a refusal of detail.

A colleague in the pack is an obstacle and a reference point. Without them you have no measure of speed, and the game knows it. The short lap is the arcade memory of the series. The home console does not stretch the race to an hour here; it returns it as a repeatable shock.

It is the sequel to the original F-Zero (1990), and the first F-Zero game with 3D graphics. The F-Zero X Expansion Kit, an expansion pack for the 64DD featuring 12 more tracks and track and vehicle editors, was released in Japan in 2000.

F-Zero X introduced the ability to attack other racers, a Death Race mode, and a random track generator called the "X Cup". Critics generally praised F-Zero X for its fast gameplay, abundance of courses and vehicles, track design, and maintaining a high framerate, although it has been widely criticized for its lack of graphical detail.

F-Zero X was ported in 2004 to the iQue Player and had re-releases through the Virtual Console for Wii in 2007, later on Wii U, and through the Nintendo Classics service, featuring online multiplayer, in 2022.
