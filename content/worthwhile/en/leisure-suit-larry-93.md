Hapless Larry looks for romance in the parodic city of Lost Wages. An adult theme, humor, and a strong authorial voice showed the widening audience of graphic adventures.

Larry Laffer in a white suit walks a parody of Las Vegas, tries his luck at the bar, in the casino, and in bed, and usually receives a slap, death, or both. You still type, or choose verbs, as in Sierra's other games. The joke is not in a new control sentence, it is in what that sentence is asking.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/093-screenshot-1.jpg)

Al Lowe wrote it for Sierra, and in 1987 it was released on DOS as a rebuilding of the older text game Softporn Adventure by Chuck Benton. Lowe takes an existing skeleton and reworks it into a comedy with a specific unfortunate man. The opening quiz is meant to verify that the player is an adult. The questions themselves are a period test of popular culture, and for that very reason they now say more about 1987 than about morality.

The decision to make the main character a loser, not a hero, had the consequence that an adventure can be a situation comedy. Failure is a joke the game expected, not only a punishment for the wrong verb. The city of Lost Wages is small and legible. This is not about the acreage of King's Quest, it is about a series of gags held together by one embarrassing desire.

In 1987 Maniac Mansion is also joking beside it, but about houses and kids. Larry is a consciously adult product and it shows that the graphic adventure no longer has one audience. Some of the jokes rest on harassment and on women characters being obstacles or rewards. That is a design, and it is fair to say that a large part of that contract has aged badly.

What has aged is exactly this humor and the quiz, whose allusions are no longer a shared memory but an archive. What has not aged is the willingness for a game to have an authorial voice and for a loss to be a gag you want to see again.
