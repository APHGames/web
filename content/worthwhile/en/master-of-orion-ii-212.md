An interstellar empire combines colonization, diplomacy, research, and tactical battles. It substantially expanded the first game and became a long-term comparative measure of space 4X.

You found colonies, negotiate with alien species, design ships from parts, and occasionally drop into a tactical battle that you can also leave to the calculator. You assemble a race from traits. Each game is therefore a different puzzle before you ever leave the home system.

Simtex, the studio of the first Master of Orion, released it in 1996 with MicroProse. The second game substantially expands the board-game depth of the first and becomes the measure to which space 4X is still compared. It runs in DOS and Windows as a classic turn-based strategy with a separate tactical screen. It is not a real-time fleet; it is an empire that thinks in rounds.

The decision to separate strategy and tactical combat has a consequence students still know from other 4X games. Autoresolve is a temptation, and a hand-played battle is both a punishment and a pleasure. The Antarans are a late pressure from outside. The design uses them to say that victory over the neighbors is not the end of history, and that technology needs an enemy you did not colonize.

In 1996, when the RTS is taking the attention of the network, Master of Orion II is a game for a player who wants one research decision in an evening, not twenty clicks a minute. Modular ships are a toy inside the game. The consequence is that the technology tree is not a list of bonuses but a workshop, and two empires with the same technology can sail with different doctrines.

What has aged is the micromanagement of dozens of colonies in the late game, and an interface that assumes patience for spreadsheets. The list of racial traits has not aged as a way to make many different matches out of one set of rules, and neither has the seriousness of the diplomacy.

In seminar it is a comparative meter. When someone says a new space strategy is like Master of Orion II, ask whether they mean the ships, the diplomacy, or only a map with stars. Against Civilization II there is less history here and more board game. Both games in one year show that the turn can still carry a large simulation.

Master of Orion II: Battle at Antares is a 4X turn-based strategy game set in space, designed by Steve Barcia and Ken Burd, and developed by Simtex, who developed its predecessor Master of Orion and Master of Magic. Master of Orion II won the Origins Award for Best Fantasy or Science Fiction Computer Game of 1996, and was well received by critics.

Master of Orion II is more complex than Master of Orion, incorporating game mechanics from Master of Magic as well as new gameplay options. Instead of the one planet per star system found in Master of Orion there are now multiplanet star systems that can be shared with opponents.

In Master of Orion II, space combat occurs only within star systems, either over a planet one side is attacking or on the outskirts of a system, if one side is driving away the other's blockaders or trying to prevent an enemy build-up. For Master of Orion II, Simtex provided additional pre-defined races, the option to create custom races, and multiplayer options.

MicroProse released Master of Orion II: Battle at Antares for IBM-compatible PCs in 1996, Reception. Master of Orion II secured 10th place on PC Data's computer game sales chart for the month of November 1996.

Master of Orion II was a finalist for the Computer Game Developers Conference's 1996 "Best Strategy/War Game" Spotlight Award, but lost the prize to Command & Conquer: Red Alert. Master of Orion II was named the 39th best computer game ever by PC Gamer UK in 1997.

The Master of Orion series set a new standard for space-based 4X games, with a retrospective review by Chick describing it as "a towering monolith in the genre that has cast an eight-year-long shadow over everything that's followed... Other games have been noted for their similarities and differences with Master of Orion II.

One review of Space Empires IV made several comparisons with Master of Orion II, commenting favorably on the complex tactical combat, while criticizing the relatively "sparse graphics and sound", concluding that it was the most sophisticated game available in the genre, but that it built "on the basic foundation of Master of Orion" instead of "breaking new ground".
