B. J. Blazkowicz shoots Nazis in fast first-person labyrinths. Smooth raycasting and immediate action helped create the mass language of the PC FPS.

B. J. Blazkowicz runs at right angles through corridors, shoots, and opens secret walls. The tempo is almost arcade-like; the dungeon is only a floor plan. You do not look up or down, and there cannot be another floor above you. Raycasting holds the world as a maze on a single plane.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/130-screenshot-1.jpg)

The engine, in C and assembly, casts rays into a grid. On DOS that produces a smooth movement Dungeon Master never had. The decision to sacrifice simulation to speed has a consequence. The player stops economizing steps and starts circling an enemy, because a hit is immediate and movement is cheap.

Ultima Underworld comes out the same year. Two first-person games, two schools: id sells tempo, Looking Glass sells physics and conversation. The shareware of the first episode repeats the Commander Keen model. The player tastes it and pays for the later episodes, so the shooter spreads on a floppy.

What has aged is the flat space and the cartoon violence. The corridors are legible to the point of being schematic, and the enemy is a sprite that does not turn into the third dimension. What has not aged is a body running with a gun. The language of the PC shooter, short range, a fast loop, receives its mass form here.

Hitler as the boss of the last episode is a period mark, not a fine satire. Take him as the boundary of what shareware was allowed to shout in 1992. When you say the first shooter, stop. Maze War, Catacombs, and Underworld come before; Wolfenstein is the first that carried the language widely.

In a seminar, start an episode and measure how quickly you understand the whole control scheme. That speed of learning is part of the design, not just a lucky tutorial. Wolfenstein 3D is a 1992 first-person shooter game developed by id Software and published by Apogee Software and FormGen for MS-DOS.

It was inspired by the 1981 Muse Software video game Castle Wolfenstein, and is the third installment in the Wolfenstein series. In Wolfenstein 3D, the player assumes the role of Allied spy William "B.J." Blazkowicz during World War&nbsp;II as he escapes from the Nazi German prison Castle Wolfenstein and carries out a series of crucial missions against the Nazis.

Wolfenstein 3D was the second major independent release by id Software, after the Commander Keen series of episodes. After a design session prompted the company to shift from the family-friendly Keen to a more violent theme, programmer John Romero suggested remaking the 1981 stealth shooter Castle Wolfenstein as a fast-paced action game.

Wolfenstein 3D features artwork by Adrian Carmack and sound effects and music by Bobby Prince. Wolfenstein 3D was a critical and commercial success and is considered one of the greatest video games ever made.

Id Software never returned to the series, but did license the engine to numerous other titles before releasing the source code for free in 1995, and multiple other games in the Wolfenstein series have been developed by other companies since 2001. Wolfenstein 3D is a first-person shooter presented with rudimentary 3D graphics.
