An IndyCar race models car setup, tires, and a full 500 miles. One of the decisive steps from arcade racing toward a serious PC sim-racing tradition.

You sit in an IndyCar on an oval and, before the race, reach for wing, gear, and tire settings. The drive begins in the garage, not at the start. You can run a short test, or the full five hundred miles. The length is not a menu punishment; it is a different discipline, in which tires and fatigue change.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/110-cover-1.jpg)

Papyrus, the studio of David Kaemmer and Omar Khudari, released the game in 1989 on DOS through Electronic Arts. It was their decisive step into simulation racing. The image is polygonal, from the cockpit and from outside, on hardware that does not yet have a 3D card as a matter of course. Speed is held by a low object count and a fixed oval.

The decision that setup changes the car's behavior separates the game from Out Run. The consequence: two drivers on the same track are not driving the same machine, even if they hold the same wheel. In 1989 a computer race is still often an arcade game with a shoulder. Papyrus claims that an Indianapolis viewer also wants tire pressure.

Opponents run a race, not just colored cars on the horizon. Passing is a question of line and wear, not of a turbo hidden in a banana. What has aged are the image and the limited intelligence of the field. The cars cannot fight for position the way they do today, and the graphics are schematic once you compare them with the later NASCAR Racing from the same studio.

What has not aged is the garage as a game. A player who does not want only to turn a wheel still gets a reward for having built the car. Papyrus grows from this title into the home school of sim racing. Without Indianapolis the later PC sagas would not have so clear an origin.

When you divide racing games, do not cut them only into arcade and simulation. Here there is a third thing: mechanical setup as its own branch of skill. The oval is deliberately poor in decoration. That makes it easier to see whether the line is right, because in the corner there is nothing else to solve.

Indianapolis 500: The Simulation is a racing video game developed by Papyrus Design Group and published in 1989 for MS-DOS. The game attempts to be a full simulation of the Indianapolis 500 race.

The field and qualifying order are true to the 1989 Indianapolis 500 starting grid, with one exception: the player's car, numbered 17, replaces Car #29 of Rich Vogler, who qualified in 33rd and last place. For example, an error in programming resulted in there being two cars numbered 20; in the MS-DOS version, one of these was numbered 12, correctly reflecting the 1989 Indianapolis 500 grid.

In 1994, PC Gamer UK named Indianapolis 500 the 38th best computer game of all time. In 1996, Computer Gaming World declared Indianapolis 500 the 122nd-best computer game ever released.
