Knights on ostriches collide with opponents in the air. Inertia and wing-flapping created physics-based combat that worked equally well cooperatively and competitively.

A knight on an ostrich flaps, gains height, and tries to ram the opponent from above; whoever is lower is vulnerable. Movement is not walking. Each flap is an impulse, and inertia carries you onward, so the fight is more a flight than a brawl on the ground.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/031-poster-1.jpg)

John Newcomer designed it for Williams, and the machine was released in 1982. Two players can ride together, or against each other, on the same screen with lava below. The picture is a fixed arena with platforms, not a scrolling landscape. The pterodactyl and the eggs change the priority: sometimes the goal is the enemy, sometimes the goal is to survive a new wave.

The decision to tie the attack to height had the consequence that cooperation and competition use the same physics. Helping a partner and knocking them down is one movement. The lava punishes a fall. A platform is not just a stage but the only ground, and whoever falls off it has no second chance in that round.

In 1982 Robotron stands beside it as pure gunfire. Joust, inside the same company, is proof that the arcade can do a physics joke for two people without a shot. The ostrich is absurd and readable for that very reason. Nobody expects a riding simulation; everyone understands that height is a weapon.

What has aged is the confusion when too many wings meet on the screen, and controls that drop a newcomer into the lava before they understand the rhythm. What has not aged is the wager on relative height: the winner is the one who, at the moment of impact, is above the opponent.

Few games of that season make cooperation and betrayal the same button. In a seminar, play it with two people and no prior agreement. The rule reveals itself at the moment one deliberately lets the other fall.

Joust is a 1982 action game developed and published by Williams Electronics for arcades. While not the first two-player cooperative video game, Joust s success and polished implementation helped popularize the concept.

It was followed by a less popular arcade sequel in 1986: Joust 2: Survival of the Fittest. While Joust was ported to numerous home systems and included in several multiplatform retro game anthologies, efforts to adapt it to other media and create updated video game remakes have been fruitless.

Joust is a platforming game where the player controls a yellow knight riding a flying ostrich (player 1) or a stork (player 2) from a third-person perspective. Like other Williams arcade games, Joust was programmed in assembly language.

Anghelo stenciled the cabinet artwork on a wooden frame, Newcomer conceived Joust as a "flying game" with cooperative two-player gameplay, but he did not wish to emulate the popular space theme of previous successful flying games like Asteroids and Defender. Because it provided an interesting method to perform a sneak attack on an opponent below the gap, and because of limited time available, The same day, it registered Joust s program, visuals, and cabinet artwork with the United States Copyright Office.

Williams Entertainment would later include Joust in several of its multiplatform arcade compilations over a decade after its initial release: the 1995 Williams Arcade's Greatest Hits, the 2000 Midway's Greatest Arcade Hits, the 2003 Midway Arcade Treasures, and the 2012 Midway Arcade Origins. In 2000, a web-based version of Joust, along with nine other classic arcade games, was published on Shockwave.com.

Joust was released via digital distribution in 2007 on GameTap, Xbox Live Arcade, and the PlayStation Network. Joust, along with many other Midway arcade games, appear in the 2016 Midway Arcade Level Pack expansion of Lego Dimensions.

Lego versions of Joust characters appear outside the emulated games in the expansion's virtual Lego world. A few months after its release, Joust topped the United States Play Meter arcade charts in January 1983; a feat it repeated on the RePlay upright arcade cabinet charts from January to February 1983.

A writer for Video Gaming Illustrated called Joust "exotic" with lifelike animation. The staff concluded that Joust was "Atari's finest since Star Raiders" in 1980.

For its 1985 Arkie Awards, Electronic Games staff named Joust the "Best Arcade-to-Home Translation" of 1984 for Atari's faithful reproduction of the visuals across several platforms. Alex Huhtala of Official Dreamcast Magazine praised the accurate emulation of the Dreamcast port and Joust s gameplay for holding up against contemporary standards.

GamesMaster magazine's reviewer Robin Alway considered Joust one of the few "all-time classics" in the Dreamcast collection. Video game historian Steve Kent considered Joust one of the more memorable games of its time.

Retro Gamer writer Mike Bevan called the game's physics "beautifully" realized and described Joust as one of the "most remarkable and well-loved titles" of the Williams library. In 2004, Ellis described Joust as an example of innovative risk absent in the then-current video game industry.

Weiss praised the accuracy of the conversion to the Atari 7800, citing Joust as a "perfect example" of the "relatively good arcade translations" the system was known for. Steve Wright, Atari's former head of its special project department, included Joust on his list of games to be stranded on a desert island with, citing the new type of competitive and cooperative gameplay it introduced.

Several publications have included Joust among the top video games released. The reviewer described Joust s premise as "spectacularly weird" and lamented the diminishment of such creative premises from the golden age of arcade games due to modern mainstream commercialization.

Williams released an arcade sequel, Joust 2: Survival of the Fittest, in 1986. While updated technology allowed Joust 2 to feature improved audio-visuals and new gameplay elements, it sold significantly fewer units than its predecessor.

Twin Galaxies, who officiated and tracked competitive high scores for players, recorded high scores for Joust and its sequel into the 2000s. Since its release, developers have both copied and built upon Joust s design.

The flying mechanics in the 2000 action-adventure game Messiah, which allowed the character to fly further by repeatedly pressing a button, were inspired by Joust. The 2013 arcade game Killer Queen was heavily inspired by Joust and mixes it with elements of real-time strategy and multiplayer online battle arena games.

Joust has been parodied and referenced in popular culture, including Robot Chicken s episode "Celebutard Mountain" and the Code Monkeys episode "Just One of the Gamers". Additionally, the book Ready Player One includes a scene featuring Joust.

A 3D remake titled Dactyl Joust was in development for the Atari Jaguar in 1995. Tentatively referred to as Joust 3D, Joust X, and Joust 64, the game featured 3D arenas with split screen for the two-player battles.

Since its release, Newcomer believed that Joust could be successfully translated into a film. Midway Games later optioned Joust s movie rights to CP Productions in 2007.

The newly formed production company intended to launch Joust as a new franchise, with graphic novel and video game adaptations based on the film. Interactive Entertainment purchased most of Midway's assets, including Joust, with the intent to develop film adaptations.
