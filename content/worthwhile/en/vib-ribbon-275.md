A wireframe rabbit overcomes obstacles generated from music. The game could analyze the user's music CD and create a level from it — an elegant link between an external medium and procedural content.

Vibri, a wireframe rabbit, jumps obstacles that a level designer did not invent for one song. The music you put into the machine invented them, and when you fall the rabbit simplifies, and when you last it grows. The game loads into the PlayStation's memory and the disc is swapped for a music CD. The limitation of a mechanism that cannot read the game and a foreign disc at once is bypassed by the software already living in memory at that moment.

Masaya Matsuura and NanaOn-Sha released it in 1999, after PaRappa. In Japan it is from that year, it will reach Europe, and it is missing from the original American PlayStation lineup, which is part of its reputation, not only a note in a region table. The decision to generate a level from someone else's album had a consequence that procedural games still deal with. The content is theoretically infinite and personal, and the picture has to stay abstract so it can carry any song, not one the team composed.

In 1999 a rhythm game is either PaRappa's lesson or the DDR floor. Vib-Ribbon is a third branch: it is not a teacher and it is not a gym; it is a reader of a medium you already have at home. What has aged is the dependence on a CD mechanism today's machine does not have, and the simplicity of the obstacles, which after a few songs reveal their alphabet. What has not aged is the idea that a level can be an analysis of a thing the player loves.

In seminar, bring two different CDs if you can get the hardware, or at least describe what happens with a quiet track and a loud one. The generator is a reader, not a spell, and silence has to produce a different level from noise. Devolution and evolution are difficulty in the body of the character. You do not only see a score; you see the interface falling apart, which is a more honest fail state than a game-over sign.

Against later music visualizers there is still a punishment for a mistake. It is not a screensaver; it is a game, because the rhythm can kill the rabbit. Matsuura, for the second time on the same console, proves that a music game can be both authorial and open. PaRappa had a teacher; this one has your disc.

The wireframe model is an economy and a style. When the content comes from outside, the studio must not compete in texture with every possible mood of an album. The game is short as a tool and long as a principle. On a syllabus of procedural content it belongs beside generators that invent a world from nothing, as the opposite: here the world is read.

(stylized vib-ribbon) is a 1999 rhythm video game developed by NanaOn-Sha and released by Sony Computer Entertainment for the PlayStation. The graphics for Vib-Ribbon are simple, consisting of straight, white vector lines forming crude, angular drawings of the level and the player character, named Vibri.

Vib-Ribbon has received generally positive reviews from critics, praising its minimalist visuals and innovative concept, and has garnered a cult following; though the game has also received criticism for its simplistic gameplay and design. It spawned two Japan-only follow-ups: Mojib-Ribbon (2003) and Vib-Ripple (2004).
