A party explores a vast first-person fantasy world. It combined an open world, dense statistical progression, and long-term exploration into a lasting RPG series.

A party of six walks in first person across a large fantasy world, picks up tasks in towns, and in a dungeon resolves combat as a table of statistics, not as an arcade reflex. The map is large enough to get lost between towns. Progress is not one descent as in Wizardry, but a long journey on which you age in numbers.

Jon Van Caneghem wrote it at New World Computing, and the first installment, Secret of the Inner Sanctum, was released in 1986 on the Apple II. It is an auteur series by a person who builds the world as a life's work, not as a studio commission. The wireframe, and later more colorful, dungeon follows Wizardry, but the outdoor map wants to feel like a continent. The memory of a home machine holds both only because the picture is modest and the system is dense.

The decision to let the player believe for a long time that they are in pure fantasy had a consequence that appears only at the end: the scenery lies, and the world has a technological origin. The genre you were playing is rewritten at the close. Until then the game is hard housekeeping with food, with spells, and with who in the front rank survives the next random battle.

In 1986 Dragon Quest is coming into being on the consoles beside it as a welcoming ritual. Might and Magic is still the computer school: stricter, longer, and less willing to lead you by the hand. Van Caneghem turns one installment into a line that will mix fantasy and technology over and over. The first game matters because it does not tell that trick in the manual.

What has aged is the pace of random battles and an interface that, without paper and without patience, looks like bookkeeping. What has not aged is the appetite for a large world to have a secret about what it actually is, not only about where the next sword lies.

From the Inner Sanctum a line runs that reminds the American RPG that a dungeon and a continent can be one program. In a seminar, do not reveal the ending in advance. An hour of play under the belief that this is ordinary fantasy is part of the game's architecture.

Might and Magic Book One: Secret of the Inner Sanctum (also known as simply Might and Magic) is an early role-playing video game, first in the popular and influential Might and Magic franchise. At the end of the game they go through the "Gates to Another World" and travel to Cron, the setting of Might and Magic II, not knowing that Sheltem has also escaped to that world.

The characters in Might and Magic and its successors are defined by a number of rules, conforming loosely to the fantasy role-playing archetypes. Characters have "statistics" (analogous to Dungeons and Dragons Ability scores) of Might, Endurance, Speed, Accuracy, Personality, Intelligence and Luck.

There are two types of magic spells in Might and Magic: Sorcerer spells and Clerical spells. Van Caneghem had difficulty finding a publisher to distribute Might and Magic, so he self-published as New World Computing, handling the distribution himself from his own apartment until he was able to broker a distribution deal with Activision.

A port of Might and Magic was released for Nintendo Entertainment System in 1992. The game was later included in Might and Magic VI: The Mandate of Heaven Limited Edition and Special Edition.

In 1988 Might and Magic joined the magazine's Hall of Fame for games highly rated over time by readers.
