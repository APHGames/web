The player does not rule a state, but a dynasty of medieval nobles. Inheritance, personality, marriages, and intrigues generate "stories" from grand-strategy systems without a scripted protagonist.

In Crusader Kings II you do not rule the map of a state, but a person who ages, marries, schemes, and dies. The player then continues as the heir, even if he is an incompetent son, because the dynasty is the save file, not a chosen hero. War needs a pretext, vassals have an opinion, and personality traits turn the council into people who can betray you out of pride, not out of an enemy faction in a menu.

Paradox Development Studio, led by Henrik Fåhraeus, released the game in February 2012 on its own Clausewitz engine. The studio at that time is already teaching players that grand strategy is a service with years of expansions. Inheritance, religion, and culture are tables from which stories appear without a screenwriter. The technology is not in a cinematic scene, but in the fact that two characters have a relationship the game can calculate.

The decision to refuse an eternal ruler means success cannot be conserved. The consequence is that the player experiences decline as often as expansion and tells stories about a poisoner aunt, not about a conquered province. Casus belli and vassal opinion slow down a power that in other strategy games would only be a troop count. The Middle Ages here are legal and familial, not only a map of flags.

In 2012 this is still a game for Paradox's patient audience. Over the years it becomes a reference generator of political anecdotes, and later also a game the studio eventually offers for free so that it can carry the wall of expansions. The start dates, especially 1066, are historical playgrounds. You can play a minor duke or a king, and the two sessions are about different problems, not a different tutorial.

That wall of expansions has aged, as has the interface, which does not lead a newcomer. Some events are also a text template you see through after hundreds of hours. The principle of a character instead of a state holds. The later third game inherits it as a finished language, not as an experiment.

Marriage is diplomacy with a biological clock. An heir is both a resource and a risk, which no purely military strategy simulates so thoroughly. In class, run it as a counterpoint to Civilization. There you build an empire; here you inherit people and their bad traits.

Crusader Kings II is a grand strategy video game developed by Paradox Development Studio and published by Paradox Interactive. Set in the Middle Ages, the game was released on February 14, 2012, as a sequel to 2004's Crusader Kings.

Crusader Kings II attracted a wider audience than Paradox's previous games, contributing to the growth of the company. Crusader Kings II is a grand strategy video game, in which the player controls a medieval dynasty from 1066, the Norman Conquest, to 1453, the Fall of Constantinople.

When a Crusader Kings II (CK2) game is launched, Paradox servers collect information about the game setup such as game version, single player or multiplayer, and what mods are in use. A large number of total conversion mods are also available: Paradox actively encourages modding, and the developers regularly tweaked the game in order to make modding easier, furthermore there exist in-depth guides on how to mod in the Crusader Kings II wiki.

GameSpot reviewer Shaun McInnis stated: "Through a complex system of diplomacy and backstabbing, Crusader Kings II makes every power struggle an engrossing one" and he lauded the gameplay while noting the "lackluster tutorials". By September 2014, Crusader Kings II had sold more than 1 million copies, with the expansion pack and DLC sales totaling over 7 million units.
