In Gone Home Katie returns to an empty house in Oregon in 1995, and the player turns on lamps, opens drawers, and assembles a sister's story from objects, not from firefights. There is no inventory of puzzles in the classic sense. An object is a sentence you read, and the house is the order in which you are willing to look into someone else's life.

The decision to drop combat means the dramaturgy stands on curiosity and on which doors are locked. The nineties, the tapes, and the family secret are concrete, not a universal horror house. The studio bets on the player's cultural memory as much as on the floor plan.

Beside Dear Esther and later games, Gone Home becomes the center of the debate about so-called walking simulators. That name is often a reproach; the game itself is a carefully ordered short prose. Sam's story unlocks through the environment, not through a dialogue menu. The player is not a judge with choices; they are an archivist who arrived late.

That argument about the definition of a game has aged, as has the expectation of length for the price at the time. The house itself is still readable, and short on purpose. The craft of the prop holds. Later narrative walking is still judged by whether it has a concrete life, or only empty corridors and an audio diary.

No jump scare is the engine. The tension is in what the family did not say aloud and what it left in the basement and in a bedroom. In class, use it as an exercise in environmental storytelling: turn the captions off and ask which sentence an object carried and which one only a letter did.

Gone Home is a first-person walking simulator video game developed and published by The Fullbright Company. Gone Home was first released for Microsoft Windows, OS X, and Linux computers in August 2013, followed by console releases for the PlayStation 4 and Xbox One in January 2016, the Nintendo Switch in September 2018, and iOS in December 2018.

Set in the year 1995, Gone Home puts the player in the role of a young woman returning from overseas to her rural Oregon family home to find her family currently absent and the house empty, leaving her to piece together recent events. Gone Home does not feature much interactivity, but instead has the player explore the house at their own pace and determine what has transpired by examining items, journals, and other items left around the various rooms.

On 7 June 1995, 21-year-old Katie Greenbriar (voiced by Sarah Elmaleh) returns home from overseas to her family's new home in fictional Boon County, Oregon. Her family consists of her father, Terry, a failed writer who makes a living reviewing home electronics; her mother, Janice, a wildlife conservationist who recently got promoted to director; and her 17-year-old sister Samantha (voiced by Sarah Grayson).

The initial concept for Gone Home was for the player to explore a smart house that included a central artificial intelligence running everything with robots running around to complete tasks. Narratively, Gone Home was designed around family; Gaynor had stated that for all the science fiction trappings of Minerva's Den, its core narrative was about a man, his wife, and how the man's past was affecting that relationship, and used the same approach for Gone Home.

Additionally, they established that the family had only recently moved into the home; Gaynor had worried that the player would have difficulty identifying key elements that would be present among all the other objects within a well lived-in home, while within a recent move, only objects of immediate importance would have been unpacked, making it easy for the player to focus on these. Gaynor stated that Minerva's Den was more focused on environmental storytelling than action-driven gameplay, and set the approach and necessary design elements they needed for Gone Home.

Other elements that informed Gone Home from their BioShock experience was non-linear levels to explore, and discovering narrative elements through audio logs that the player would have to actively seek out, both which supported the exploration elements of the game. Gone Home was released for the Nintendo Switch on 6 September 2018 through support of publisher Annapurna Interactive.

Gaynor asserted that Gone Home was specifically developed for the video game medium, stating that they considered it an "edited discussion" between Fullbright and the player, as "[t]he content that you encounter appears differently than for someone else. The interactivity of it makes the experience unique to yourself." The lack of interactivity led to Gone Home to be called "walking simulator" at its release, though since then, the industry has come to accept "walking simulators" to describe similarly contemplative narrative games with little direct interactivity, like The Stanley Parable, Firewatch, and What Remains of Edith Finch.

Gone Home s approach to exploratory storytelling was a point of influence for parts of Uncharted 4: A Thief's End and Prey. Polygon named Gone Home among the most important games of the 2010s for its unique approach of conveying plot through examining objects, and its focus on queer relationships.

Due to both the atypical nature of Gone Home and its narrative centered around LGBT issues, it became a central point of discussion at the onset of the Gamergate controversy in August 2014. A portion of video game players, some who supported the Gamergate movement, criticized games like Gone Home and Depression Quest for lacking the qualities of normal video games while earning praise from the gaming press, as well as including topics like LGBT issues.

By the time of release of Tacoma, Fullbright's second game released in October 2017, Gone Home had sold more than 700,000 copies. Gone Home was nominated for several categories at the 2014 Game Developers Choice Awards, including "Game of the Year", "Innovation Award", "Best Narrative", and "Best Downloadable Game", while Fullbright won as a studio for "Best Debut" for Gone Home.

Awards, the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences nominated Gone Home for "Downloadable Game of the Year".
