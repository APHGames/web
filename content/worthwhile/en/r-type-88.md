The R-9 fights the biomorphic Bydo empire with a detachable Force module. The Force functions as a shield, a weapon, and an independent object; the levels thus require a spatial solution, not only reflexes.

The R-9 flies sideways against the biomorphic Bydo empire, and the Force module can sit in front of you, behind you, or be released as an independent body that shoots and covers. Charging a shot wants calm in the middle of a pattern. Whoever mashes constantly has a weak shot; whoever waits risks the wave hitting them at the very moment of power.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/088-poster-1.jpg)

Irem released the machine in 1987. The visual tone is bodily and alien, close to biomechanical illustration, and that tone is exactly what separates the game from Capcom's military silhouettes. The Force is not a power-up that disappears into a number. It is an object in the world you can lean on, that you can lose, and that changes which hole in the pattern you will fly through.

The decision to make the main weapon a spatial tool had the consequence that a level is a puzzle about place, not only about reflex. The same corridor wants the Force in front, and a screen later it wants it behind. The third stage, with a large ship, is a notorious test of memory. The arcade still assumes that the next coin is a continuation of the lesson, and R-Type keeps that contract harshly.

In 1987 Gradius has already taught the bar beside it. R-Type answers differently: fewer items in a menu, more of one object you have to place. Death often means the loss of the Force as well. Progress is property, as in Gradius, but the property has a shape and a position, not just an icon.

What has aged is the cruelty of stages that assume you will run them several times, and a density of shots that is not readable without that memory. What has not aged is the Force as a sentence about space: shield, weapon, and satellite in one body that you have to put somewhere.

From R-Type on, we measure when a shooter is about configuration and when it is about where in the pattern you are physically allowed to be. In a seminar, freeze the game and ask where the Force belongs in the next second. Whoever points to a place, not to the name of a weapon, has understood Irem.

R-Type was commercially successful and praised by critics for its graphics and addictive gameplay, but was criticized for its difficulty. A remake of the game was released as R-Type DX for the Game Boy Color in 2000.

A 3D remake, R-Type Dimensions, was released for the Xbox 360 in 2009; for the PlayStation 3 in 2014; for the Nintendo Switch, PlayStation 4, and Windows in 2018; and for iOS in 2019. Hudson Soft developed a fairly accurate port of R-Type for the PC Engine/TurboGrafx-16 console, although it suffered from a slightly lower resolution, reduced color palette, sprite flickering and slowdown.

The TurboGrafx-16 version of R-Type has a boss at the end of Stage 6, rather than a prolonged wave of enemies as in all other versions. The Xbox Live Arcade game R-Type Dimensions was released on February 4, 2009, by Tozai Games.

The TurboGrafx-16 version of R-Type was one of the first games confirmed for Nintendo's Virtual Console. In 2010, DotEmu developed an iOS port of R-Type, published by Electronic Arts, released also for Android in September 2011.

Tozai Games returned in 2018 and released an updated digital-only version of R-Type Dimensions, now carrying the title R-Type Dimensions EX, for Microsoft Windows (via Steam), PlayStation 4, and Nintendo Switch. In Japan, Game Machine listed R-Type as the most successful table arcade cabinet of July 1987.

Clare Edgeley of Computer and Video Games selected R-Type as the "game of the month" in July 1987. According to Mega Guide in 1992, R-Type had sold "squillions on hand-held".

In a retrospective review, Allgame editor Shawn Sackenheim described the TurboGrafx-16 version of R-Type as "(the) most accurate home conversion (of the arcade version) of the game", furthermore stating that "it's the shooter that started it all and it's still a thrill ride from beginning to end". Irem has developed a number of sequels and related games, including R-Type II and Armed Police Unit Gallop.

Their XMultiply, although not part of the series, has similar themes and gameplay, and was released around the same time as R-Type II. In 1991, Konami released Xexex, a game heavily influenced by R-Type and XMultiply.

The game used similar weapons and concepts, such as the "Flint" being a combination of R-Type Force and X Multiply tentacle equipment. Other games similar to R-Type are Rezon, released by Allumer in 1991, Aicom's Pulstar and Blazing Star (the latter published under Yumekobo) for the Neo Geo, and Technosoft's 1992 title Thunder Force IV for the Sega Genesis.

On April 1, 2019, Granzella announced the development of R-Type Final 2, a sequel to R-Type Final.
