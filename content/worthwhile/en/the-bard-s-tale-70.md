A party of adventurers clears the city of Skara Brae through turn-based battles. Distinctive graphics, a musical bard, and elaborate party progression helped the CRPG reach a wider home audience.

A party moves through the city of Skara Brae, descends into dungeons, and in turn-based battles relies on classes, while the bard is not a mascot but a profession whose songs are spells. The city between dungeons gives the game a rhythm of rest: inn, shop, temple. The adventure is not one pit, it is a shift and a return.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/070-cover-1.jpg)

Michael Cranford designed it for Interplay, and Electronic Arts published it in 1985 on the Apple II. Brian Fargo, as producer at Interplay, makes it a pillar of the American home RPG beside Ultima and Wizardry. The dungeons are more colorful and more illustrative than Wizardry's wireframe. Portraits and animated clashes are meant to sell the fantasy to people who do not want to draw a map on graph paper for a whole semester, even though a map still helps.

The decision to give the bard music as a mechanic had the consequence that support of the party can be heard. A role is not just a number in the back rank, it is a song that keeps the others alive. The story of a city under threat is more readable than Wizardry's pure descent. The player knows why they are climbing down, not only how deep the floor is.

In 1985 Ultima IV is pulling the RPG toward ethics, and Wizardry is still pulling it toward the maze. The Bard's Tale sits between them as an adventure that also wants to be a spectacle. A series comes out of it quickly. The first installment matters because it shows both the publisher and the player that a party on the Apple II can have a face.

What has aged is the pace of the turns and a dungeon that, after some hours, admits it is a grid with a table of enemies. What has not aged is the bard as a role that is neither fighter nor wizard, and the city as a place you return to gladly, not only in order to save.

A thread runs from Skara Brae to Interplay's later series, including games that will take a party into a much darker world. In a seminar, listen for whether the students take the bard's song as music or as healing. If they say both, Cranford's decision still works.

The Bard's Tale is a fantasy role-playing video game franchise created by Michael Cranford and developed by Brian Fargo's Interplay Productions (1985 –1992) and inXile Entertainment (2004–present). The Bard's Tale II: The Destiny Knight dropped the Tales of the Unknown series title, as did all ports of the original game after 1988.

Both Cranford and Fargo have refuted the assertion that the original projected titles for the second and third installments were The Archmage's Tale and The Thief's Tale. The series was not continued for many years and is thus still often referred to as the "Bard's Tale trilogy".

In 2018, Alex Santa Maria stated that The Bard's Tale was "One of the most classic franchises in PC gaming". Official Cluebooks were published by Electronic Arts for the first three Bard's Tale games.

A series of novels based on The Bard's Tale were published by Baen Books during the 1990s. As part of the crowdfunding campaign for The Bard's Tale IV, six novels set within the redefined setting from The Bard's Tale IV and the Remastered edition of the original trilogy were provided to backers in EPUB format in 2018.
