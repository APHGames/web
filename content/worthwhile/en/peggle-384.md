In Peggle you shoot a ball into a field of pegs and try to clear the orange ones. The rest is physics, chance, and a plan of where to place a bank before the ball disappears at the bottom. The last orange peg launches an exaggerated celebration to the Ode to Joy. The reward is deliberately larger than the act, and the craft of the casual game is exactly in that disproportion.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/384-screenshot-1.jpg)

PopCap published it in 2007 on PC, in the same workshop as Bejeweled. It is a peak of the downloadable casual scene, which is not yet a mobile display case but already knows how to be as exact as an arcade. The consequence of the celebratory juice is that the player wants the next shot for the feeling, not for progress in a story. The game has no need of a campaign, because the feedback is the campaign.

Green and purple pegs and a sliding bucket add an extra rule without breaking readability. Every element is visible, and therefore chance can be partly read in advance. In 2007, beside the big console games, this is an argument that a small game can be finished further than a large one. The length of one round is a whole evening if the celebration convinces you to stay.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/384-cover-2.jpg)

It has barely aged. It is still used to teach that juice, the sound and image after a success, is design, not a decoration that can be unplugged without losing the game. The master characters are frames for sets of levels. Each set teaches a different kind of bank, and therefore progress is not merely a harder field; it is a new physical sentence.

For chance students it is a class about visible risk. The ball falls by laws you can see, and therefore a loss does not feel like a hidden dice roll in the code. A limited number of balls makes the field a puzzle with a budget. Whoever shoots without a plan runs out of balls before they run out of orange pegs.

The game later appears even as a joke inside other bundles. The original Peggle is still PopCap's pure statement of what a reward should look like. When you play it today, play it with the sound on. Without the Ode to Joy a pinball remains. With it, the reason the casual craft is still discussed remains.

PopCap, a subsidiary of Electronic Arts, announced Peggle 2 at E3 2013. Inspired by pachinko and bagatelle, each level of Peggle challenges the player to shoot a limited supply of balls at a field of colored pegs to clear out specifically marked pegs while attempting to achieve a high score through skilled shot planning.

Special powers associated with a diverse cast of cartoon "Peggle Masters" can be activated to aid the player in this task. Peggle initially sold slowly but was boosted by the inclusion of a specially designed demonstration in Valve's The Orange Box, and has since been downloaded over 50 million times.

Within the game's main "Adventure" mode, Peggle is divided into fifty-five levels. In each of these modes, the game generally offers the ability for players to select which Peggle Master they wish to use.

Peggle Extreme was packaged with the Windows version of The Orange Box, featuring levels inspired by Half-Life 2, Team Fortress 2, and Portal. Peggle Extreme was eventually made free to download for anyone with a Steam account.

Peggle has also been ported as a minigame inside World of Warcraft in the form of a free, downloadable addon. It features levels based on the game, though including a limited set of Peggle Masters to use.

Warcraft players can also use Peggle to decide how to distribute loot from successful raids or dungeons. Subsequently, in July 2009, a free standalone, ten-level edition of Peggle themed around Warcraft in a similar manner as Peggle Extreme was released.

PopCap released a sequel to the original game, Peggle Nights, in September 2008 for Windows. The sequel expands upon Peggle by adding one new Peggle Master, adding new levels and challenges.

The Xbox Live and PlayStation Network versions of Peggle received Nights as a downloadable expansion to the game on November 19, 2009. Peggle Nights was made available as an in-app purchase for iOS devices.

Peggle: Dual Shot is a Nintendo DS port of the game, developed by Q Entertainment; the game includes content from both Peggle and Peggle Nights. IGN s Erik Brudvig cited Peggle as being "simple enough for anybody to pick up and instantly start having fun".

Upon its release on Xbox Live Arcade, Peggle was the top-selling game on the service for two weeks and remained in the top ten applications for several weeks afterward. Within a month, over 100,000 players were listed on the Xbox leaderboards for Peggle.

In a list compiled by the IGN staff in September 2010, Peggle was listed as the 10th best Xbox Live Arcade of all time. The release of Peggle on iOS was extremely popular, placing in the top ten applications purchased through the App Store for the first two weeks it was available.

Peggle is considered by some critics to be an addictive game, with Forbes noting that it entices the user to play "just a few more minutes". MSNBC named Peggle one of the "Top 5 most addictive computer games of all time".

PopCap funded a study at East Carolina University which found that Peggle players experienced a "45% decrease in depression" in individuals under the age of 25. Rock Paper Shotgun interpreted the same study more imaginatively, reporting: "Peggle was clear champ, improving the total 'mood' by 573% across all study subjects".

Peggle along with other PopCap games were also found to help children with ADHD improve their attention span and memory recall, in a survey done by Information Solutions Group on behalf of PopCap. Peggle was nominated for the 2007 Game Developers Choice Awards for "Best Downloadable Game", "Best Handheld Game" (for the iPod port), and "Innovation" awards.

Peggle appeared as #40 on the 2007 PC Gamer Magazine (UK) list of their 100 favorite video games of all time.
