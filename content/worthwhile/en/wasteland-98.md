Rangers survive in post-nuclear America and solve tasks by multiple paths. Persistent changes to the world, skill checks, and reactivity created a direct design line to Fallout.

A party of rangers survives in America after a nuclear war, resolves disputes by speech, by violence, or by a skill, and the world remembers what you did in a town even after you leave. A skill check is not only combat. You can heal, unlock, persuade, and so the same door can be opened with more than one sentence.

Brian Fargo produced it at Interplay, and Electronic Arts published it in 1988. Ken St. Andre and Michael Stackpole took part in the writing; it is an RPG that takes a paper game seriously and at the same time translates it into a desert, not into a dungeon with a dragon. Part of the text is not on the disk but in a printed book of paragraphs. That saves space and at the same time prevents you from reading the future on the screen before you reach it. A reference in parentheses is a mechanic.

The decision that a deed should remain in the town even after you leave had the consequence that a choice has a memory. You cannot clear a location and pretend you arrived at the next one as a stranger without a past. More than one path to a task means that the set of skills is a character. A party of killers and a party that can talk are not playing the same plot, even if they are standing in front of the same door.

In 1988 SSI's Gold Box is offering official D&D beside it. Wasteland offers an unofficial, dirtier America, and thereby opens a branch that Interplay will later name Fallout. The tone is dry and cruel without apocalyptic pomp. The joke is in the detail of a consequence, not in a speech about the end of the world.

What has aged is the leafing through a paper book and an interface that forces today's player into a patience the game considers obvious. What has not aged is the reactivity: a town that remembers you, and a skill that is a sentence, not just a bonus to an attack.

Fallout will later acknowledge this line openly. For a seminar, Wasteland matters as a design, not as trivia about what came afterward. In a seminar, give two groups a different skill and the same task. If their paragraphs diverge, the game is doing what it promised.

Wasteland is a role-playing video game developed by Interplay Productions and published by initially Electronic Arts in March 1988. The first installment of the Wasteland series is set in a futuristic, post-apocalyptic America, destroyed by a nuclear holocaust generations before.

A remastered version titled Wasteland Remastered was released on February 25, 2020, in honor of the original game's 30th anniversary. Critically acclaimed and commercially successful, Wasteland was intended to be followed by two separate sequels in the 1990s, but Electronic Arts dropped claims of Fountain of Dreams being a sequel and Interplay's Meantime was canceled.

Decades later, inXile Entertainment, founded by the game's director Brian Fargo, released two proper sequels: Wasteland 2 (2014) and Wasteland 3 (2020). Wasteland s game mechanics are based on those used in tabletop role-playing games, such as Tunnels & Trolls and Mercenaries, Spies and Private Eyes created by Wasteland designers Ken St.

Characters in Wasteland have seven attributes strength, intelligence, luck, speed, agility, dexterity, and charisma that allow the characters to use different skills and weapons. The player's party begins with four members and can grow to as many as seven by recruiting citizens and wasteland creatures.

Wasteland was one of the first games featuring a persistent world, where changes to the game's open world were stored and kept. In later interviews, Fargo said Wasteland came about after the success of The Bard's Tale and Interplay's desire to make another role-playing game for Electronic Arts separate from a sequel to the game.

Its author Michael Stackpole was announced as the writer for Wasteland in 1987. Wasteland was re-released as part of Interplay's 10 Year Anthology: Classic Collection in 1995, and also included in the 1998 Ultimate RPG Archives through Interplay's DragonPlay label.

Throughout the 1980s and 1990s, Computer Gaming World lauded Wasteland for its gameplay, plot, problem solving, skills system, non-player characters, and the moral dilemmas players face. In 1994, the magazine cited Wasteland as an example of how "older, less sophisticated engines can still play host to a great game".

Orson Scott Card gave Wasteland a mixed review in Compute!, commending the science fiction elements and setting, but stating that it lacked a meaningful overarching story. In Issue 11 of Gateways, Keith Post commented, "I greatly enjoyed playing Wasteland.

In 2000, Wasteland was ranked as the 24th-best PC game of all time by the staff of IGN for its innovations. According to IGN, "Interplay's inability to prise the Wasteland brand name from EA's gnarled fingers actually led to it creating Fallout in the first place." Fargo's inXile Entertainment acquired the rights to the franchise from Electronic Arts in 2003.

The game's production team included original Wasteland designers Alan Pavlish, Michael Stackpole, Ken St. In 2016, inXile announced a crowdfunding campaign via Fig to develop Wasteland 3.

In an August 2013 Kickstarter update for Wasteland 2, project lead Chris Keenan announced that they had reached an agreement with Electronic Arts to release the original Wasteland for modern operating systems. He added that it will be given for free to backers of Wasteland 2 on Kickstarter, in addition to being made available for purchase on GOG and Steam.

In November, Keenan announced that the re-release titled Wasteland 1: The Original Classic had gone gold, and had been submitted to GOG and Steam for approval. Those who backed Torment: Tides of Numenera and received Wasteland 2, also received the re-release for free.
