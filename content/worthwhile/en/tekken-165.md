Fighters compete in the King of Iron Fist tournament. Four limb buttons and 3D presentation founded a fighting philosophy distinct over the long term.

In the King of Iron Fist tournament you press four buttons, each of which is a limb. A strike is not a secret quarter-circle on the stick; it is an arm or a leg you can picture. Characters differ in how long a sentence they can make from those four buttons. A match is anatomy, not a list of motions you would copy from a slip of paper.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/165-screenshot-1.jpg)

Seiichi Ishii, who had previously worked on Virtua Fighter, designed the game in 1994 at Namco. The System 11 arcade hardware is kin to what will shortly carry the PlayStation. The engine is polygonal and, in 1994, still early. Namco is not competing with AM2 for the smoothest frame at any cost; it is competing to make the controls explainable on the fingers.

The decision to bind a button to a limb has a long-term consequence. The player learns a character through the body, and the same philosophy will remain in the series decades later. In 1994 Tekken is the second great 3D school beside Sega. One reads a fall and a stance; the other reads which limb you promised.

Kazuya, Jack, or Yoshimitsu are not variations on one karate. Their silhouette says the reach before you read a hint. What has aged is the small roster and the early juggling, which does not yet have the later discipline. Some matches end before the system shows its depth.

What has not aged is the pedagogy of the four buttons. A new player still senses what they pressed, even if they do not sense whether it was wise. The home version on the PlayStation will turn this arcade game into an argument for the console. For the history of the design, though, the arcade comes first, where the philosophy of the limbs was tested.

Compare the input with Street Fighter II. There a special is a sentence made from a stick motion; here a special is often a consequence of which leg you were holding. In a seminar, cover the characters' names and let the students name the buttons as body parts. If they can, Ishii's decision still works.

Tekken is noted as being one of the first fighting games at the time to use 3D animation. Tekken 2, as well as the third game Tekken 3, are considered landmark titles; they received critical acclaim for their gameplay and more immersive experience.

The Mishima saga covering the first ten games until Tekken 8 was described as a story revolving around the Iron Fist Tournament and a family aiming to destroy each other. All major installments were released as arcade games, except for Tekken 8, and the boards used to run them have traditionally been based on PlayStation hardware.

The first game in the series, Tekken, was released in 1994, first as an arcade game and as a port for the PlayStation in 1995. Tekken 2 was released in 1995 in arcades and in 1996 for the PlayStation.

The canon ending of Tekken 3 consists of Paul Phoenix defeating Ogre and leaving victorious. Many of the characters who were removed in Tekken 3 returned in Tekken 5.

The home version includes a mode known as Devil Within, a variant of the Tekken Force mode introduced in Tekken 3. The game features a Scenario Campaign mode, which follows gameplay from previous Tekken Force modes, which was playable online alongside standard versus. Few side playable characters' storylines have their endings from Tekken Tag Tournament 2 ties to this game.

Tekken Tag Tournament was released in 1999 in arcades and as a launch title for the PlayStation 2 in 2000. Tekken Tag Tournament features tag battles and includes almost all of the Tekken characters in the series up until that point in time, for a total of 34 characters.

The game ran on the same arcade board with an updated Tekken 3 engine, and thus saw major graphical upgrades when ported to the PlayStation 2. The home version features remixes of the characters' themes from the arcade version, and also features a bonus Tekken Bowl mode.

A remastered version of the game, Tekken Tag Tournament HD, is included in the 2011 collection Tekken Hybrid, which also contained a playable demo of Tekken Tag Tournament 2 and the film Tekken: Blood Vengeance. Its console version was released the next year and is based on the updated arcade version called Tekken Tag Tournament 2 Unlimited that contained new features.

The Wii U version, which served as a launch title for the console, includes a revised version of the Tekken Ball mode from Tekken 3 and exclusive Nintendo themed costumes for each character, as well as a new mode that makes use of the mushrooms from the Super Mario Bros. Like the previous iteration, Tekken Tag Tournament 2 includes nearly every character up to that point and a few exclusive characters.

Directed by Virtua Fighter designer Seiichi Ishii, Tekken was intended to be a fundamentally similar title, with the addition of detailed textures that were not blocky on the characters like Virtua Fighter 2 and twice the frame rate. Tekken, however, dedicates a button to each of the four limbs of the fighter.

From then on, starting with Tekken 2, characters automatically block while not moving forward or performing actions, a feature called "neutral guard." Standing or retreating characters will block high and middle attacks with no input from the player, while crouching characters will duck high attacks and block low ones. Tekken 3 introduced several gameplay possibilities that were retained in later games, including the ability to sidestep into the foreground or background.

Tekken 3 and later games also reduced recovery time after being knocked down and gave characters rolls to recover instantly after hitting the ground, allowing the player to get back into the fight more quickly at the risk of being hit while rolling. Tekken 4 was another leap, and gave characters even greater mobility by adding true 3D movement inside geometrically complex arenas with uneven ground, obstacles, and walls.

Tekken 4 received mixed feedback to the point of being considered by some as one of the worst installments in the series which led to major changes starting with Tekken 5. In retrospect, Harada believes Tekken 5, and Tekken 6, managed to attract a new group of fans, something Tekken 4 did not largely do.

Harada has even improved his thoughts on the game and has gone on to call it the best game or peak in the series during an interview for the new Tekken 8. According to Harada, the innovation went "the other way" or reverted to more conservatism beginning Tekken 5.

Much of the positive changes and reviews were not focused on in Tekken 4, and instead Harada focused only on the negative reviews against it, which in hindsight was not the proper way to make a fighting game starting with Tekken 5. Tekken 5 also saw a combination of walled and infinite arenas, while discarding uneven ground and terrain to allow more juggling.

Tekken 6 retains much of the design from Tekken 5, but it also includes a "Rage" mode, which activates when a character is near the end of his vitality bar and earns a damage multiplier. Tekken 6 also has destructible floors and walls that allow the fighters to blast through to new fighting areas when broken.

Tekken Tag Tournament 2 retains these elements while also adding a new kind of stage break ("balcony breaks", which is a combination of floor and wall breaks where characters will go through a breakable wall and fall to a lower level in the same sequence). The back walk animation is now similar to Tekken Revolution, featuring a more fluid movement away from the opponent as opposed to a slower shuffle.

Later Tekken 7 updates eventually add "Rage Drive" (also cost Rage mode, and is an equivalent of EX and lower level super moves) as of Tekken 7: Fated Retribution, with the console ports' post-Season 1 adds two new damage properties which were originally existed in previous characters and still can only work against an opponent while they are on the ground. Tekken 7 Season 2 adds "Wall Bound" mechanic, which bounces opponents from the wall to nearby them to an airborne state with a knockdown attack, originally used by both NANCY-MI847J in Tekken 6 and a guest character Geese Howard prior to Season 2.

The last update patch of Tekken 7 Season 4 adds "Wall Stun", which causes opponents to be bounced from the wall and entered a downed hit stun, whether they were being hit by or blocked their attacking opponents' attack. Tekken 8 introduced a new heat mode making the game oriented toward offensive play and creates more opportunities to turn the tide of a fight.
