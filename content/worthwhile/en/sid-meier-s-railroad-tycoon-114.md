The player builds a railway network, trades, and competes with rivals. An economic system reacting to transport infrastructure became the foundation of the tycoon school.

You lay track, build stations, and decide which train will carry which goods between cities, mines, and factories. The economy reacts to what you connect. A line is not decoration on the map; it is the condition for demand to appear somewhere at all.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/114-screenshot-1.png)

Sid Meier designed the game in 1990 at MicroProse, with Bruce Shelley beside him. It is their economic game just before Civilization. It runs on DOS as a turn-based map with menus. There is no three-dimensional train; the graph of connections and the accounts decide.

The decision to bind industry to transport has a consequence from which the tycoon grows. The player does not win because the line is pretty, but because the right thing flows along it. In 1990 computer strategy can do a war turn and the god hand of Populous. Railroad Tycoon adds a third reading: infrastructure as a business.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/114-screenshot-2.jpg)

Competitors take contracts from you, and the stock market can punish a bad company. You are not alone on an empty map. What has aged is the micro-interface. Assembling trains and signals takes a patience that later entries and fan versions tidied up.

What has not aged is the pleasure of the first line that pays for itself. The player sees a system, not just a model of a locomotive. Meier is testing here what he later stretches across all of history in Civilization: a short turn, many systems, the urge to click once more.

When you explain the word tycoon, do not start with a roller coaster. Start with a freight train that earns money only if it arrives somewhere on time. The map of the United States, England, or Europe is historical scenery. The rule stays the same: connect a source with someone who needs it.
