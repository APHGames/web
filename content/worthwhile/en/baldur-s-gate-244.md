A child of Bhaal travels the Sword Coast with a party of companions. The Infinity Engine and "real-time with pause" revived the party CRPG and joined the AD&D system with rich characters.

You walk the Sword Coast with a party that argues, and combat runs in real time until you stop it. The pause is a way to turn an AD&D round into something several characters on the screen can carry. The second-edition rules, THAC0 and armor class, are not hidden behind a simplification. The game trusts that the player will bear a table if they have a reason to go on because of a companion.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/244-screenshot-1.jpg)

BioWare of Edmonton, Ray Muzyka, Greg Zeschuk, and Augustine Yip, released it in 1998 with Interplay and Black Isle. After the mech game Shattered Steel this is their breakthrough, and the Infinity Engine is their tool: pre-rendered isometry and scriptable dialogue. The child of Bhaal is a pretext for the journey. The real content is a network of quests and characters who have their own sentences, which in 1998 is not yet an obvious standard of the computer RPG.

The decision to play combat in real time with pause had a consequence for readability. A table with dice fits on a screen with ten monsters, and at the same time the rules are harder to read than in a pure turn-based game. In 1998, beside Fallout 2, these are two schools of reactivity. One is the wasteland and SPECIAL, the other is a D&D party with a voice and with the Forgotten Realms license.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/244-cover-2.jpg)

What has aged is THAC0, the inventory, and the opening grind of levels. What has not aged is the party as a traveling cast and the pause as a way to think a simulation at all. In seminar, let a fight run without the pause and then with it. Students will name which mode is the game and which is only chaos from the dice.

The engine will become the platform of Planescape: Torment and of Baldur's Gate II. The first game is therefore already a tool as well, not only the Coast. The companions teach designers that a side character with a quest holds the player better than another dungeon. That sentence sounds ordinary today, and in 1998 it is paid for with writing by hand.

Against the Japanese RPG of the same year there is less of a fated hero here and more of a table. The camera is clerical, not cinematic, and all the more gets said. The game revives the party CRPG at a time when the genre on PC looked tired. The fatigue ended once the pause and the dialogue sat in one engine.

At the time of the game's release, PC Gamer US said that Baldur's Gate "reigns supreme over every RPG currently available, and sets new standards for those to come". Baldur's Gate was awarded "PC Role-Playing Game of the Year" by the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences during the 2nd Annual Interactive Achievement Awards.

According to IGN, Baldur's Gate did much to revive the role-playing video game genre. IGN ranked Baldur's Gate number five on their list of "The Top 11 Dungeons & Dragons Games of All Time" in 2014.

Baldur's Gate set the standard for other games using AD&D rules, especially those developed by BioWare and Black Isle Studios: Planescape: Torment (1999), Icewind Dale (2000), and Icewind Dale II (2002). Phil Savage of PC Gamer praised Bioware's level design for the titular city in Baldur's Gate, stating that "Baldur's Gate feels vast, exciting and dangerous—just like a proper city".

Writing for Gamesradar, Lorenzo Veloria cited Baldur's Gate as an influence on video games like Pillars of Eternity. Scholarly analysis of Baldur's Gate has examined the game's use of party members as part of its approach to computer role-playing game storytelling.

It was followed by the expansion pack Tales of the Sword Coast (1999), then the sequel Baldur's Gate II: Shadows of Amn (2000) and its expansion pack Throne of Bhaal (2001). In 2023, Baldur's Gate 3 developed by Larian Studios left early access and saw a full release on 3 August 2023.
