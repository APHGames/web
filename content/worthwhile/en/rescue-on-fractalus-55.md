A pilot rescues castaways in a procedural alien landscape. Fractal terrain created a surprisingly convincing 3D landscape on 8-bit hardware.

A pilot flies low over the mountains of an alien planet, looks for castaways, and takes them into the ship, while avoiding fire from ground positions. Landing is a commitment. You have to choose a place, slow down, and only then find out whether the figure you settled beside is even human.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/055-cover-1.jpg)

Lucasfilm Games developed it for home eight-bits, and after the collapse of the deal with Atari, Epyx published it in 1984. David Fox designed the game in a small team that was only just finding out what a game from a film studio should look like. The mountains are not painted by hand. Fractal terrain, tied to the work of Loren Carpenter, assembles ridges from a rule, and thus on the Atari 8-bit a landscape appears that feels larger than the machine's memory.

The decision to let the pilot step out of the cockpit with their eyes, even if only for a second of encounter, had the consequence that the flight is not a shooter. It is a rescue shift with the risk that you are rescuing the wrong body. Some castaways are a trap. The game uses your learned courtesy and punishes it, which is cruel and at the same time a clear rule, not a random jump scare without a system.

In 1984 Ballblazer is coming into being beside it as the second game from the same studio. Rescue on Fractalus! is the more serious one: a view from the cabin, altitude, and a map that is being calculated. Compared with Microsoft Flight Simulator, this is not about a civil craft. It is about an eight-bit machine carrying the feeling that there is a world under you, not a sprite.

What has aged is the jerkiness of the terrain and the slow search for dots in the valley, once the first astonishment at the mountains has passed. What has not aged is the wager that a rescue is more interesting than a kill, and that a landscape can be an equation.

A thread runs from this cockpit to later Lucasfilm flights in which the camera is part of the storytelling, not only a gunsight. In a seminar, freeze the game above a ridge and ask how much of that picture is a stored image. The right answer is: almost nothing.

The player controls a fictional "Valkyrie" space fighter (converted for search and rescue duty) from a first-person view, attempting to land and pick up downed Ethercorps pilots. According to head developer David Fox, this shock moment made Rescue on Fractalus!

The developers of Rescue on Fractalus wanted the game to be set in Star Wars universe but were not allowed to do so by George Lucas. On the Winter Consumer Electronics Show on January 5–8, 1985, with both Rescue on Fractalus!

Epyx finally published the Atari 8-bit versions of both games by May 1985, and the Commodore 64 version of Rescue by August of that year. Besides the Atari 8-bit and Commodore 64 computers, Rescue on Fractalus was ported to the Apple II and TRS-80 Color Computer; and to Amstrad CPC and ZX Spectrum computers by Activision, who published the game in Europe.

LucasArts and Factor 5 began work on a sequel titled Return to Fractalus for the Amiga in the late 1980s, but it was decided that the hardware couldn't handle the concept being worked on.

An Amiga port of Rescue On Fractalus to be published by Rainbow Arts was announced in a February 1991 issue of The One, but was never released. By 1994, Factor 5 believed that the current generation of 3D consoles had the technology the Fractalus sequel required and again began work on the game.
