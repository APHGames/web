A car laps a track against the clock. It helped define the physical language of automobile cabinets: a steering wheel, pedals, and a top-down view.

The player drives a car from above around a closed track and tries to set the best time before the clock runs out. You do not take a corner with a four-way joystick like later arcade cars; a steering wheel, pedals, and a gear lever force the body to play along.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/005-poster-1.jpg)

Atari released the game in 1974 as Gran Trak 10. It is still discrete logic and a television picture, but the cabinet is no longer a button panel; it is an imitation cockpit. The track is stored so the circuit can read it as a fixed shape, and the car runs on it from above, because full perspective would have been too much for arcade hardware of the time.

The decision to spend money on a wheel and pedals had the consequence that the game sells the feeling of driving, not only a reaction to enemies. At the same time it made the cabinet more expensive and harder to build. The physical controls are part of the design, not an accessory.

In 1974 Pong is still a wave that everyone copies. Gran Trak shows a second road: a themed machine that you stand at differently from ping-pong. Two players are not yet the main spectacle; the opponent is the racing line and the clock, which makes the game more of a test than a match.

What has aged is the coarse top-down track and a car that now looks like a symbol, not a vehicle. What has not aged is the language of the cabinet: hands on the wheel, a foot on the pedal, a top-down view of a corner you have to hit on an arc.

A thread runs from this cabinet to later racing games that still sell the driver's body before they sell a tire model. In a seminar, notice that the genre is born from the furniture as much as from the code.
