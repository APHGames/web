Players move through a maze from an eye-level view. It belongs among the earliest precedents of first-person navigation and networked competition.

The player moves through the corridors of a maze from eye level and meets opponents who are looking for them in the same space. This is not a score for shots in an open field, but orientation: get lost, and you lose before you ever fire.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/004-screenshot-1.jpg)

Steve Colley, Greg Thompson, and Howard Palmer wrote it around 1973 on Imlac machines, first at NASA's Ames Research Center, and later as a game of linked computers as well. The picture is wireframe and first-person; the perspective is recalculated when the player steps forward, which on the hardware of the time is not an effect but the main technical problem.

The decision to give each player their own eyes, rather than a map from above, had the consequence that information is unequal: you see only what is in front of you. The networked version added an opponent who is not a program on the same machine, but a person at another terminal. Competition moves from one room into a connection between machines.

In 1973 Spacewar! exists beside it as a ship duel seen from the side, and text games are only just coming into being. Maze War builds a space you enter. It is not a commercial product and it has no cabinet; it is a laboratory precedent that spreads among people with access to the machines.

What has aged is the slow wireframe drawing and a control scheme that assumes the player is a terminal operator. What has not aged is the idea that a first-person view is a limit on information, not just a camera, and that a maze is a map you have to carry in your head.

Later dungeon crawlers and first-person shooters inherit this cut: the world is larger than the frame you can see right now. In a lecture, do not present it as a finished genre, but as the moment when the player stopped looking at a board and started standing inside it.

Maze, also known as Mazewar, and Maze War, is a 3D multiplayer first-person shooter maze game originally developed in 1973 and expanded in 1974. Thompson and other programmers later developed several other versions of Maze, including a specialized hardware-based game by Thompson and other students as well as a version titled Mazewar by Jim Guyton, Mike Wahrman, and colleagues at Xerox for the Xerox Alto computer.

The Xerox version went on to inspire many different takes on the first-person maze game concept in the 1980s and 1990s, released under many different names. Maze is a multiplayer first-person shooter maze game in which players traverse a flat maze and shoot opponents to score points.

The maze layout is represented by a grid of spaces that are either empty or solid and form a flat plane containing walls of equal height. The game contains a default maze layout, but players can provide their own upon starting the game.

Other players in the maze are displayed as the letters of their usernames along with an indicator of which direction they are looking; later versions of the game replaced this with the image of an eyeball.
