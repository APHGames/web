Five traumatized people face the sadistic AI AM. A psychological theme and morally burdened branching showed the potential of the adventure for a difficult literary adaptation.

Five people whom the artificial intelligence AM lets live walk through scenarios built from their guilt. The puzzle is not an escape from a room; it is a moral trap set for a particular psyche. There are several endings, and some are wrong on purpose. The game does not want you to win by escaping the theme; it wants you to see what your choice does to the character.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/188-screenshot-1.jpg)

Cyberdreams built the game in 1995 from a story by Harlan Ellison. Ellison took part in the script and voiced AM, so the adaptation is not just a license on the box. The engine is point-and-click on a disc, with voices and painted shots. The technology serves claustrophobia, not an open world.

The decision to split the game into five psychological branches has a consequence. One character is not enough as the player's avatar; the player is, by turns, five different guilts. In 1995, beside commercial FMV horrors, this is the literary extreme of the adventure. The 1967 story does not turn into an action game; it turns into a series of tests that are meant to hurt.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/188-screenshot-2.jpg)

AM is not a mysterious boss at the end. He is a narrator who punishes you for playing, which is a contract the genre does not usually sign. What has aged is the interface and some cruelty of the puzzles that blends into the cruelty of the theme. It is not always clear what is design and what is the friction of the period.

What has not aged is the idea that an adaptation need not be an advertisement for a book. It can be a game that means a moral choice seriously, all the way to discomfort. The individual scenarios differ in tone. A seminar gets a use out of that: the same engine can carry five different ways of being a trap.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/188-screenshot-3.jpg)

Compare AM with SHODAN from System Shock. Both are intelligences that hate the player; one speaks from logs in a shooter, the other from an adventure that does not give you a weapon as comfort. In a seminar, do not describe the cruelest solution as a curiosity. Describe that the branching is ethical, and let the students find a particular guilt themselves if they open the game.

"I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" is a post-apocalyptic science fiction horror short story by American writer Harlan Ellison. "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" has been adapted into various media, including a 1995 computer game co-authored by Ellison, a comic-book adaptation, and a BBC Radio 4 play.

Upon awakening, Ted concludes that AM's sadistic nature stems from its inability to think creatively or move freely in spite of its miraculous abilities and boundless knowledge; in turn, this fuels its need for vengeance against humanity, who have condemned it to its own existence. When the five finally reach the ice caves, they find a pile of canned goods, but have no tool to open them.

Gorrister wails in pain, and his scream dislodges several ice stalactites from the ceiling of the cave. The story ends as an anguished, despairing Ted states that he has no mouth, yet he must scream.

Ellison derived the story's title, as well as inspiration for the story itself, from his friend William Rotsler's caption of a cartoon of a rag doll with no mouth.
