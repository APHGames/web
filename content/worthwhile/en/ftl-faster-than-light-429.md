A ship's crew flees across a random galaxy ahead of the Rebel fleet. Pausable real-time management of individual systems creates emergent crisis stories from a small number of rules.

In FTL you command one ship fleeing a rebel fleet and, in real time that can be paused, you put out fires, heal the crew, and decide which system gets the next unit of power. The player jumps across sectors, chooses events, and loses a run because the missiles or the oxygen ran out. The permanent death of crew is a small tragedy inside a larger escape.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/429-cover-1.jpg)

Subset Games, that is Justin Ma and Matthew Davis, funded the game through Kickstarter and released it in September 2012. Two people built a galaxy out of event tables, not out of films. The pause is the key interface technology. Without it a crisis would burn faster than it can be read; with it, real time becomes a board game that still pretends to be time.

The decision to let the rebel fleet keep advancing means no sector is safe farming. The consequence is pace: every interesting event is also a delay. The ship's systems, shields, engines, oxygen, doors, and weapons, argue over the same power. The design is in allocation, not in aiming.

In 2012 this is a distinct computer roguelike, still before the wave that will dilute the genre. A small team and a large variability of runs are part of the myth and of the actual production. The crew has species and names, so a loss is not a number. A story arises from the table that players still tell, because the game did not write it in advance.

The small interface has aged, as has some of the randomness that can end a run before a strategy shows itself. That is a tax of the genre, not an accident of development. The pause and the crisis hold. The free Advanced Edition later adds content without changing the basic contract: one ship, one fleet at your back.

Voluntarily opening the doors to vacuum in order to put out a fire is a sentence no tutorial has to deliver. The system allows it, and the player arrives at it in a panic. In class, take it as proof that an emergent story does not need a dialogue tree. It needs a few systems in conflict and a clock that is ticking.

FTL: Faster Than Light is a roguelike game created by indie developer Subset Games, which was released for Windows, MacOS, and Linux in September 2012. With positive responses from the players and judges at these events, Subset opted to engage in a crowd-sourced Kickstarter campaign to finish the title, and succeeded in obtaining twenty times more than they had sought; the extra funds were used towards more professional art, music and in-game writing.

The player controls a spacecraft capable of traveling faster-than-light (FTL), crewed by humans, aliens, or a mix of both. Unlike most space combat simulation games, the beginning idea was the player being captain rather than a pilot according to Davis, PC Gamer magazine offered an early preview of the game that created more media interest in time for the Independent Games Festival at the March 2012 Games Developer Conference.
