Two-member teams play exaggerated basketball full of dunks. "On fire," simple controls, and theatrical commentary defined arcade sport as a distinctive alternative to simulation.

Two against two, you play basketball in which a foul barely exists and a dunk is the reason you inserted the coin. When you hit several baskets in a row, a player catches on fire: endless turbo and a ball in flames. The streak is a rule, not a commentator's decoration.

Mark Turmell and Midway sent the game to arcades in 1993. The players' heads are digitized, the bodies drawn, and the commentary shouts a highlight, not a zone system. The hardware holds a fast sprite and a crowd. There is no simulation of defense in today's sense; there is a computer whose job is to look like a television montage.

The decision to reward a streak instead of a correct system has a consequence. The match breaks on a moment everyone at the cabinet can see, so the sport is theater. In 1993 an arcade sport does not have to imitate a broadcast. NBA Jam claims that the broadcast is already a game in itself if you leave only the dunk and the shout.

Secret characters and codes extend the life of the cabinet. Midway counts on the arcade discovering the content, not finishing it in an evening. What has aged are the rules that are absent. Whoever wants a pass and a defense will find a caricature; whoever wants a brawl under the basket will find exactly that.

What has not aged is the readability of being on fire. Even a player who does not follow basketball understands that now is not the time to step in the way. Two on two is an arcade economy. Fewer bodies mean larger sprites and a clearer reason to pass to the partner standing next to you.

Compare it with a serious sports simulation of the time. One school counts statistics; this one counts whether the arcade is shouting. In a seminar, take the commentary as sound design. It is not a radio reporter of reality; it is a metronome that says when there is a joke and when there is a point.

NBA Jam is a basketball video game series based on the National Basketball Association (NBA). Initially developed as arcade games by Midway, the game found popularity with its photorealistic digitized graphics, over-the-top presentation and exaggerated style of two-on-two basketball play.

The successor to Midway's Arch Rivals, the original 1993 NBA Jam allows players to jump many times above their own height, make slam dunks that defy human capabilities, and freely shove or elbow opponents out of the way without consequences. It also featured a variety of secret characters, as well as the ability to track player records and statistics between play sessions.

After the release of NBA Jam Tournament Edition, Acclaim, the publishers of the home versions of the NBA Jam games acquired exclusive rights to release games under the NBA Jam name under their Acclaim Sports brand. They produced their own games with the NBA Jam name starting in 1996 with NBA Jam Extreme, an arcade game featuring the trademark over-the-top style of the previous games.

Meanwhile, Midway continued its own series of NBA games with NBA Hangtime. After NBA Jam Extreme, Acclaim started publishing titles exclusive to home consoles with a more realistic style.

Acclaim then attempted to return the series to its arcade roots with one more console game, 2003's NBA Jam, before the company closed in 2004. 6 years later, Electronic Arts acquired the rights to the name and in 2010 released NBA Jam for the Wii, PlayStation 3 and Xbox 360.

The game was an attempt to replicate the original Midway games on newer consoles and original lead designer/programmer Mark Turmell was consulted on its development.
