A pair of Source Hunters investigates magical crimes in Rivellon. Elements such as oil, fire, water, and electricity create a combinatorial tactical system and prepared the way to BG3.

Two Source Hunters in Divinity: Original Sin investigate magical crimes, and on their turn the player sends fire into oil, water into lava, and electricity into a wet puddle, because the surface is part of the combat, not decoration on the floor. Cooperation of two people in one campaign means the dialogue sometimes argues even inside the party. The world of Rivellon is funny, bloody, and willing to burn.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/444-cover-1.jpg)

Larian under Swen Vincke funded the game through Kickstarter and released it in June 2014. The Belgian studio, which had carried the Divinity series for years on the margin, builds with its own Divinity Engine the tool on which Original Sin 2 and Baldur's Gate 3 will later grow. Elemental combinatorics is a design technology: oil, fire, poison, water, and electricity have a table of relations. The player learns to read the terrain before the weapon statistics.

The decision to let two players into the same story, including arguments in dialogue, means cooperation is not only a shared fight. The consequence is a party game that can be about disagreement, not only about splitting loot. The Enhanced Edition later smooths the game according to what the first year showed. The studio already treats release as the start of tuning, not as a closed box.

In 2014 this is an independent argument that a turn-based RPG with a large simulation still has an audience if you give it cooperation and a fire. Beside the big action RPGs it feels like a board game that refused to be ashamed. The tone is light and in places clownish. That is Larian's deliberate face before the more serious second game and before Baldur's Gate, not a mistake.

The inventory has aged, as have some of the jokes and the story, which is thinner than the systems. The interface also reveals that the studio is only just building the language DOS2 will sharpen. The surfaces hold, and so does the willingness to let the player break a room. Without this game, Larian's later combinations are harder to explain.

Combat on height and behind obstacles gives tactics back a third dimension that a purely paper turn often flattens. A puddle under the stairs is a plan, not an accident of the graphics. In class, use it as the origin of a system: show one room and let students set the floor on fire before you allow them to read the quest.
