A frog crosses a road and a river over moving obstacles. Precise timing and readable multi-lane systems are an early example of emergent "traffic" level design.

A frog jumps across lanes of a road and then across logs and turtles on a river, in order to occupy a home bay before the time runs out. Each jump is one square. You do not control speed, you control the moment, and the moving lane under you carries away even a correct decision.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/029-screenshot-1.jpg)

Konami designed the game, and Sega released it as an arcade machine in 1981, in America under the name Sega-Gremlin. It is an early case where the design and the operation of the cabinet are not the same company. The picture is a grid of lanes, readable even from the other end of the room. Cars, logs, and turtles have a direction you read before you understand the score.

The decision to split the crossing into two different kinds of traffic, a road and a river, had the consequence that the same jump means dodging in the lower half and riding someone else's movement in the upper half. A turtle that dives, and a crocodile in the bay, punish a player who reads only the nearest lane. Success wants a plan three jumps ahead.

In 1981 Donkey Kong stands beside it as a vertical rescue. Frogger is a horizontal crossing, and its enemy is not a villain but traffic. There is no shot here. The whole game is an agreement about when you may stand on a place that will not be safe in a second.

What has aged is the severity of one bad jump and the repetition of the same five lanes, if you are expecting a campaign. What has not aged is the readability of a system made of lanes: each band is a rule, and their composition is a level, even if it is not called one.

Every game that builds danger as a timetable learns from Frogger, from roads in later platformers to crossings in city simulations. In a seminar, cover the score. If the game is still tense, you can see that time and the lane are enough.

Frogger was positively received by critics upon its release, and is considered one of the greatest video games ever made. It was followed by numerous clones and several home-only sequels in the Frogger series.

She persuaded executives who denigrated Frogger as a "women and kids game" by reminding them of Pac-Man. Wanting to broaden the player base demographics, Jack Gordon, director of video game sales at Sega/Gremlin, noted that women shied away from the "shoot 'em ups" on the market, and that games like Frogger "filled the void".

Coleco released stand-alone Mini-Arcade tabletop versions of Frogger, which, along with Pac-Man, Galaxian, and Donkey Kong, had three million sales combined. Frogger is one of the 6 launch games for the 1983 Gakken Compact Vision TV Boy.

Frogger s success increased production, becoming one of the top-grossing arcade games in North America during 1981. In his 1982 book Video Invaders, Steve Bloom described Frogger as a "climbing game" along with Space Panic (1980) and Nintendo's Donkey Kong (1981).

In 2013, Entertainment Weekly named Frogger one of the top ten games for the Atari 2600. In March 2025, The Strong National Museum of Play named Frogger as one of 12 finalists for induction into the World Video Game Hall of Fame.

Video game clones include Ribbit for the Apple II (1981), Acornsoft's Hopper (1983) for the BBC Micro and Acorn Electron, A&F Software's Frogger (1983) for BBC Micro and ZX Spectrum, Personal Software Services's Hopper for the Oric-1 in the UK (1983) and a later release for the ORIC Atmos, Froggy for the ZX Spectrum released by DJL Software (1984), Solo Software's Frogger for the Sharp MZ-700 (1984) in the UK, and Leap Frog for the NewBrain. Several clones retain the basic gameplay of Frogger and change the style or plot.

Frostbite (1983), for the Atari 2600, uses the Frogger river gameplay with an arctic theme. Konami announced that a Frogger game show was in production for Peacock, produced by Konami Cross Media NY and Eureka Productions.

On November 26, 1999, Rickey's World Famous Sauce offered $10,000 to the first person who could score 1,000,000 points on Frogger or $1,000 for a new world record prior to January 1, 2000. The current Frogger world record holder is Michael Smith of Durham, North Carolina who on September 21, 2024, scored 1,404,570 points.
