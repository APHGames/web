Defenseless Daniel flees a castle from monsters and from his own past. Restricting direct combat and the psychological reaction to looking at a monster helped launch the modern first-person horror wave.

Daniel in Amnesia: The Dark Descent walks Castle Brennenburg without a weapon, hides in the dark, and reads his own past in diaries he does not want to finish. The player closes doors, throws a stone to mislead sound, and watches the lantern. Looking at the monster and staying in the dark are two different risks, both measured by sanity.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/417-cover-1.jpg)

The Swedish Frictional Games of Thomas Grip and Jens Nilsson continues the Penumbra series. They published the game themselves in September 2010 as a small PC horror at a time when big horror often means shooting through magazines. Their HPL2 engine, named after Lovecraft, carries physical doors, drawers, and the sound of footsteps. Technology here serves touch: the world is meant to be taken in the hand, not walked as a corridor with a trigger.

The decision to take direct combat away from the player and to punish looking at the monster means information and danger are the same thing. The consequence is a game that teaches you to look at the wall. Sanity is not only a meter for effect. It blurs controls and sound, so fear is a malfunction of the interface, not only a picture in a cutscene.

In 2010 Frictional thereby opens a wave of first-person horrors in which the main verb is to hide. Outlast and the others arrive only after this lesson. The castle is linear in its large arc and at the same time full of detours to diaries. The pace is held by the shortage of lamp oil as much as by the shortage of courage.

The monster models have aged, as has some of the artificial intelligence, which is more directed than the player in a panic admits. The visual roughness of HPL2 is also obvious today. The sound, the helplessness, and the structure in which the hero's past is worse news than the creature in the corridor all hold. That does not fall behind the graphics.

The physical puzzles with doors and crates are sometimes clumsy. That too is part of the risk of building horror on a simulation of hands. In class, set it against action horror: here the loss of shooting is design, not a budget saving, and the consequence is an entire genre current.
