Joel guides Ellie through post-pandemic America. Performance capture, environmental storytelling, and mechanical vulnerability strengthened the ambition of big-budget character drama.

Joel leads Ellie across post-pandemic America, and between conversations the player saves bullets, crafts, listens for clickers, and moves through spaces Naughty Dog builds like film shots. Stealth and shooting are fragile. One mistake gives you away, and the inventory is small, so violence is necessity, not a reward style.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/433-cover-1.jpg)

Naughty Dog's game was directed in June 2013 on PlayStation 3 by the pair Neil Druckmann and Bruce Straley. Troy Baker plays Joel, Ashley Johnson plays Ellie, and performance capture is part of the production, not an added gloss. The studio's own engine carries environmental detail and face animation on hardware that is already at the end of the generation. Listen mode and the sound of clickers turn information about space into a game system.

The decision to tie the shortage of ammunition to the intimate relationship of two characters means vulnerability is both theme and control. The consequence is a big budget that is not ashamed of the player often having nothing to shoot with. The environment tells stories even where dialogue is silent: notes, rooms, and furniture left in place. The studio thereby continues the craft from Uncharted, but takes the adventure gloss off it.

In 2013 this is an argument that console action can carry serious character drama. At the same time an argument begins about whether the violence of the controls and the violence of the story speak the same sentence. Winter in the middle of the game changes who depends on whom. The structure of the campaign is therefore not only a journey west, but an exchange of roles.

Some environmental puzzles with planks and ladders have aged, as has the enemy AI, which an experienced player can outplay. Listen mode is a period tool of readability. The performances hold, and so does the ending, which forces you to name what you were willing to do. Left Behind adds to that relationship off the main path.

Clickers, who see by sound, are a design, not only a monster. They force you to quiet the controller in your hand, which is a rare joining of fiction and input. In class, use it for budget and form: a big game here buys time for silence and for faces, not only a bigger firefight.
