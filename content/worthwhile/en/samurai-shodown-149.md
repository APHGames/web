Samurai fight with weapons that deal high damage. An emphasis on spacing, individual heavy strikes, and weapons created a counterpoint to combo-oriented Street Fighter.

With a weapon in hand you wait for the distance at which one blow decides the round. A long combo is not the main currency here; the main currency is the space between you and the blade. The rage meter and the recoil of a weapon change the tempo. Whoever slashes constantly is exposed, and whoever waits can receive a single blow that is enough.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/149-poster-1.jpg)

SNK released the game in 1993 on the Neo Geo, in the arcade and on an expensive home cartridge. Haohmaru and Nakoruru are the faces of a board that lives on one game being large and costly. The engine is two-dimensional and sharp. Large characters and a short round are held by hardware that does not have to compute polygons for a match to look heavy.

The decision to bet on high damage is a conscious counterpole to Street Fighter II. The consequence: the player learns to stand, not only to press a chain they already know. In 1993 the fighting game already has its combo school. Beside it, Samurai Shodown builds a school of spacing and of one heavy blow.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/149-cover-2.jpg)

Weapons are not an extra reach statistic. They change how close you may stand at all, so the match begins before the strike. What has aged is some extreme character dominance and the period cruelty of the animations. The balance is arcade balance: whoever knows one strong blow can win the evening.

What has not aged is the reading of a step. It is still tense whether the opponent will step into range or leave you slashing at nothing. The Neo Geo keeps the same content in the arcade and at home, if you can afford the cartridge. With that, SNK turns the fighting game into a collector's object, not just a coin.

Compare the length of a round with Street Fighter. There a match is composed; here a match often breaks on a single decision. In a seminar, let two students play without combos. If the game still works, you understand what SNK was defending against Capcom.

Samurai Shodown is a 1993 fighting game developed and published by SNK for the Neo Geo arcade and home platforms. In contrast to most fighting games at the time, which were set in the modern age and focused primarily on hand-to-hand combat, Samurai Shodown takes place in feudal-era Japan (similar to Kaneko's Shogun Warriors that was released the year prior).

Samurai Shodown was a commercial success, becoming Japan's sixth highest-grossing arcade game of 1993 and one of America's top five highest-grossing arcade conversion kits of 1994. Samurai Showdown then appeared on other platforms, including the Takara-published Super NES, Sega Genesis, Game Boy and Game Gear versions in 1994.

Samurai Shodown evolved from what was originally planned to be a traditional side-scrolling beat 'em up, featuring monsters as the dark heroes (similar to Data East's Night Slashers). However, after considering what would sell to a global audience, series-creator and director Yasushi Adachi decided that a fighting game with distinctly Japanese characters, such as samurai and ninjas, would do better.

The programming team for Samurai Shodown consisted of a combination of veteran SNK programmers and former Capcom employees. The idea to spell the English title as "Shodown" rather than "Showdown" came from SNK's U.S.

They felt the Japanese title "Samurai Spirits" did not adequately explain the game, and believed the spelling could be a play on the word shogun, based on the lingering popularity of the Shogun miniseries. In Japan, Game Machine listed Samurai Spirits as the most popular table arcade game of August 1993.

In North America, RePlay reported Samurai Shodown to be the top-performing arcade software kit from August to October 1993, and then again in December. In the February 1994 issue of Japanese magazine Gamest, Samurai Shodown was awarded Best Game of 1993 in the Seventh Annual Gamest Grand Prize; it was also the first to win in the category of Best Fighting Game (Street Fighter II Dash, the previous Game of the Year, won Best Action Game).

Samurai Shodown also placed first in Best VGM, Best Album and Best Direction, and second place in Best Graphics. Samurai Shodown won multiple awards from Electronic Gaming Monthly in their 1993 video game awards, including Best Neo-Geo Game, Best Fighting Game, and Game of the Year.
