A ninja passes through isometric locations, fights, and solves obstacles. Exceptional presentation and the joining of action with exploration rank among the peaks of the C64 aesthetic school.

A ninja moves through isometric gardens and palaces, jumps over precisely placed obstacles, collects objects, and in close combat tries to hit an angle that the camera deliberately complicates. Each screen is a painted scene. This is not about tiles repeating forever, but about a place that is meant to look like an illustration, even though you have to walk across it.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/095-cover-1.jpg)

System 3 published it in 1987 on the Commodore 64. It belongs among the peaks of the British effort to show that the C64 can be a gallery, not just an arcade port, and the controls pay for that gallery with severity. Isometric view on a home machine means that a jump to the right on the screen is not a jump to the right in the world. The player translates the joystick over and over, as in Q*bert, except that here the punishment is a fall from a palace.

The decision to join sightseeing and combat had the consequence that the beauty of the picture and the readability of an attack argue with each other. What looks like a poster plays like a test of a millimeter. Objects and passwords between levels hold the campaign together. It is not one room; it is a path that wants you to endure even an ugly fight for the sake of the next beautiful screen.

In 1987 the C64 is still the main European home machine, and beside systems games such as Paradroid there is a school that sells astonishment at the picture. The Last Ninja is its loudest example. The combat is delayed and easy to die in. Some players love the game for the places and some hate it for the hands, and both reactions are reading the same design.

What has aged is exactly this unfairness of the angle and a fight that, after an hour, is still not a language but an obstacle in front of the next picture. What has not aged is the ambition for a limited computer to look like a painted world, even if you pay for it with the controls.

From this isometric view, the British scene measures when presentation is the content and when it is a tax. In a seminar, let the students describe a screen before they jump. If the description is richer than the attempt at a jump, you have seen what System 3 wagered on.
