A wizard flies over deformable landscapes, collects mana, and fights for territory. Free 3D flight, terrain deformation, and a strategic economy were unusual for their time, both technically and in design.

On a carpet you fly over a landscape you can punch holes into, you collect mana, and by balloon you send it to a castle that is your score and your base. The spells are not just a shot. Some of them change the terrain or your relation to hostile creatures, so the action has an economic consequence.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/172-cover-1.jpg)

Bullfrog and Peter Molyneux released the game in 1994 on DOS through Electronic Arts. After Theme Park this is their technically wild opposite: not a friendly park, but a deformable world. The engine computes a height landscape that can be rewritten in flight. In 1994 that is unusual even beside Doom, because Doom does not dig the terrain.

The decision to join free flight to mana harvesting has a consequence. You are not in a shooter that ends with an empty level; you are in a strategy that is controlled like flight. In 1994 this is a demonstration of what Bullfrog is trying outside the isometric sandbox. Populous raised the ground with a cursor hand; here you raise it by flying over it.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/172-screenshot-2.jpg)

Rival wizards hold territory. The map is not a campaign with one correct path; it is a contest over whose balloon carries more. What has aged is the readability of combat and the short horizon. The beauty of flight runs into the fact that an enemy and your own castle sometimes vanish into the distance.

What has not aged is the pleasure of a landscape you scarred yourself. Terrain deformation still feels like a spell, not like an effect in a menu. Compare the tempo with Dune II. There, a cursor and barracks; here, a body in the air and a resource you have to pick up in person.

In a seminar, let a player fly for a minute and build nothing. Then turn the mana on. You will see when a tech demo becomes a rule. Molyneux is still selling a system that can be watched. The difference from Theme Park is that watching hurts when someone shoots you down.

Magic Carpet is a 3D flying video game developed by Bullfrog Productions and published by Electronic Arts in 1994 for MS-DOS, PlayStation, and Sega Saturn platforms. An expansion pack, Magic Carpet: Hidden Worlds, was released for MS-DOS compatible operating systems in 1995 and added 25 levels and winter-themed graphics.

A compilation package, Magic Carpet Plus, which included the main game and the expansion, was used as the basis for PlayStation and Sega Saturn ports that were released in 1996. The player plays a wizard on a magic carpet flying over water, mountains and other terrain while destroying monsters and rival wizards (which are controlled by the computer) and collecting "mana" which is gathered by hot air balloons and stored in the player's own castle.

A port for the Atari Jaguar CD was in development after Atari Corporation made a deal with Electronic Arts to bring some of the latter's titles to the system in the middle of 1995, with Magic Carpet being among them. Mike Diskett, one of the playtesters of the title and programmer of the Atari Jaguar versions of Syndicate and Theme Park, revealed in a 2015 forum post at AtariAge that before Atari bought the rights to the title, he had tried porting Magic Carpet to the Jaguar during his spare time and concluded that it was impossible due to the Jaguar's difficulty with texture mapping.

A 3DO Interactive Multiplayer version of Magic Carpet was announced to be in development, but this port was never released for unknown reasons.
