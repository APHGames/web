Astro rescues the crew across dozens of playful planets. The design of almost every mechanic is linked to DualSense haptics and the long history of PlayStation; Sony states a release of September 6, 2024, and the BAFTA 2025 awarded it Best Game.

Astro in Astro Bot rescues the crew across small planets, and the player jumps, punches, and in every level receives a different toy of the controller: a pull, a shake, trigger resistance, or a tilt. The DualSense is not an accompanying effect; it is the timing of the mechanic. The levels are short and dense like a box with one idea. The bots you rescue are both a collection and a memory of PlayStation history, from old mascots to games the studio expects you to recognize.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/497-cover-1.jpg)

Team Asobi and director Nicolas Doucet released the game through Sony on September 6, 2024 on PlayStation 5. It continues from Astro's Playroom, which was a technical demonstration packed in with the console, and from the older VR Astro Bot. In 2024 it takes The Game Awards' game of the year, and in 2025 the BAFTA for best game. The decision to tie almost every mechanic to haptics means a port to another controller would lose half the joke. The consequence is a platformer that is both an advertisement for the hardware and an honest game about the jump.

There is no open world and no service. The studio bets that in 2024 a short linear joy can still be sold if every world is finished and does not delay you with a map. The cameos of console history are both a tone and a risk. Anyone who does not know the history still jumps; anyone who does gets a second reading that can feel like a corporate memory book.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/497-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 2024 this is the center of the mainstream, which is itself a historical sentence: a precise 3D platformer with no multiplayer season takes the main prizes. Beside live services it feels like a relief and like an exception. The difficulty of the main path is welcoming, and optional challenges wait aside. The studio separates a household playthrough from the places where the jump starts to hurt.

Only the part of the cameos that will need a footnote in ten years has aged. The jump, the impact, and the haptics do not fall behind, because they are craft, not a reference. The density of ideas per minute holds. It is still the best contemporary example of how to turn a technical demonstration of a controller into a whole game without it remaining a demo disc.

Rescuing bots gives collection a face. You are not in a numerical list; you are in a level that ends with someone thanking you in an animation. In class, use it for hardware as level design. Ask which level students could carry over to a controller without adaptive triggers, and which one would fall apart.
