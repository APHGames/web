Civilizations advance through epochs and wage war in antiquity. It joined a Warcraft-like RTS structure with historical technology and civilizational progression.

Villagers gather, build, and at a certain moment you press the age button. Stone Age becomes Tool Age, and the village changes its contract with the opponent both visually and militarily. The civilizations share one technology tree and differ by bonuses, not by wholly different units. You learn one game and then the shades.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/224-screenshot-1.jpg)

Ensemble Studios, founded by Tony Goodman, released it in 1997 with Microsoft. Rick Goodman led the design, and beside him was Bruce Shelley, who brought experience from Civilization at MicroProse. The engine is a two-dimensional RTS with historical campaigns. Unit pathfinding is notoriously fragile, and it belongs to what you remember about the game even as a flaw.

The decision not to create radically different factions, but historical variants, had a consequence for readability. The player still recognizes a villager, a barracks, and an archer, and still feels that the civilizations are not interchangeable skins. In 1997 it sits between Warcraft II and the StarCraft that is on the way. Ensemble takes the fantasy structure of an RTS and exchanges it for epochs, so war has a history lesson instead of a mythology.

The economy is still about the paths of workers. The consequence is that macro and micro are not yet separate sports; lose the lumberjacks and you lose the battle before it starts. What has aged is exactly the pathfinding and the unit selection limit, which forces the army to be split into inconvenient groups. The feeling that advancing an age is a visible historical event has not aged.

In seminar, compare one civilization's bonus with the asymmetry of Red Alert. Students will see two schools: a shared trunk with a deviation, or two different armies. The campaigns are a textbook that lets you play. The historical figure is a frame and the system remains a sandbox, which is a contract the series will keep.

Shelley's presence is a bridge. Turn-based Civilization and real-time Age of Empires are not relatives by accident; they share an appetite for the progress of a civilization. The game founded Microsoft's historical RTS. The second entry, two years later, will take the same contract and turn it into a long-term sport.

Age of Empires is a series of historical real-time strategy video games, originally developed by Ensemble Studios and published by Xbox Game Studios. The first title in the series, Age of Empires, focused on events in Europe, Africa, and Asia, spanning from the Stone Age to the Iron Age; the expansion game explored the formation and expansion of the Roman Empire.

The sequel, Age of Empires II: The Age of Kings, was set in the Middle Ages, while its expansion focused partially on the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire. Age of Empires III and its two expansions explored the early modern period, when Europe was colonizing the Americas and several Asian nations were on the decline.

Another installment, Age of Empires Online, takes a different approach as a free-to-play online game utilizing Games for Windows Live. A spin-off game, Age of Mythology, was set in the same period as the original Age of Empires, but focused on mythological elements of Greek, Egyptian, and Norse mythology.

The fourth main installment in the series, Age of Empires IV, was released on October 28, 2021, also focusing on the Middle Ages. The Age of Empires series has been a commercial success, selling over 25 million copies.
