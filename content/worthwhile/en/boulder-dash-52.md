Rockford digs dirt, collects diamonds, and manipulates falling rocks. Simple material physics enables chain reactions and emergent puzzles.

Rockford digs dirt, collects diamonds, and has to dodge a boulder that falls once it has been dug out; often the right move is to let a whole row fall, not just one rock. Enemies and amoebas change the field while you dig. A calm puzzle can turn, within five seconds, into an avalanche you can no longer stop.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/052-cover-1.jpg)

Peter Liepa designed it, and First Star Software published it in 1984 on the Atari 8-bit. It is not an arcade port, but a home game whose physics are clear enough to be read as a puzzle. A fall, a slide, and what a neighboring square can support are rules made of few states. The computer does not have to simulate a rock as a body; it is enough that the player believes in the gravity of the grid.

The decision that every dug-out square could start a chain had the consequence that plan and panic alternate. A level you solved on paper can decide otherwise on the screen. A diamond is both a goal and a danger, because it lies where a rock will fall. Collecting and surviving are not two screens, but one gesture.

In 1984 it stands as a quiet opposite of the shooters: the tension is made by the material, not by a projectile. It therefore spills easily into the arcades and onto further home machines. The player learns to read the field like a clockwork. Whoever digs rashly closes their own path, and the game considers that a correct result.

What has aged is the severity of the late levels, which want a learned sequence to the pixel, and an interface that does not warn a newcomer about the avalanche. What has not aged is the pleasure of a chain you start and then only watch, to see whether the physics agrees with your sentence.

From Rockford, every game in which the terrain is a store of energy and the player only pulls out the right brick learns something. In a seminar, freeze one level in the middle of a fall. Ask what happens next. Whoever can say it knows the game, even if they have not finished it.

Mean Machines gave the Game Boy port of Boulder Dash a score of 90%, praising it as "one of the finest video games ever written", describing the game as "one to buy as soon as possible" and noting its faithfulness to the original Commodore 64 version. The ZX Spectrum version of Boulder Dash received a positive review from Computer and Video Games magazine.

They concluded by stating "though it doesn't look like much, Boulder Dash rocks". Boulder Dash was included in the top 30 Commodore 64 games by C't Magazin in Germany.
