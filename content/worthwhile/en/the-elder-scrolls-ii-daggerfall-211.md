The player travels the enormous procedural region of Iliac Bay. Extreme scale, factions, and free character creation represent a radical branch of the computer open-world RPG.

In the Iliac Bay you assemble a character from skills, advantages, and disadvantages, and then you walk a province that is not generated as one hand-drawn map. There are so many quests and guilds that the emperor's main mission can be left lying. The game counts on you choosing a life, not one chapter.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/211-cover-1.png)

Bethesda released it in 1996 for DOS. Julian LeFay led the programming, and Ted Peterson substantially shaped the writing and the rules of the world; the studio at that time is not yet a brand of large single-player blockbusters. The world is procedural 3D. Bethesda compared its area to Great Britain, which you should take as a period claim about scale, not as geographical proof, and a generator assembles towns and dungeons from parts.

The decision to generate the province instead of building it by hand has a double consequence. The scale has no equivalent in a 1996 RPG, and at the same time the corridors and streets begin to repeat, and the game is notorious for bugs. Character creation is radical even by computer-RPG standards. A custom class and a list of disadvantages mean that a limitation is part of the fantasy, not a punishment from a designer hiding in a tutorial.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/211-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 1996, hand-written party games and Japanese console RPGs stand beside it. Daggerfall is the other pole: a simulation of a place where the story does not lead you by the hand and the map does not spare you. What has aged is the emptiness of travel, the repetition of generated interiors, and an interface that wanted to be a spreadsheet for simulating a life.

The courage to let the player be anyone in a province full of factions has not aged. Later Elder Scrolls will tame this branch with hand-built cities, but the freedom begins here. In seminar it is a good demonstration of the price of a procedural world. Size is not free; you pay for it with the authorship of every street.

Anyone expecting Morrowind will be disappointed by the pace. Anyone expecting a tool for a life in a foreign province will see a design the hardware of the time could barely carry. The bugs are not a footnote. They are part of this game's history, and they remind you that ambition without a stable tool is still ambition, it is just hard to play.
