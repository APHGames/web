The player leads a civilization from antiquity to the modern age. A "one more turn" structure, a technology tree, and multiple paths to victory created one of the foundational strategic templates. The GDC preserves Meier's postmortem.

You lead a civilization from antiquity to the modern age, found cities, research, and watch whether a neighbor overtakes you, or your own neglected economy does. A turn is short and there are several goals: conquer, send a ship to the stars, or survive culturally and diplomatically. That is why the urge arises to click once more.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/127-cover-1.jpg)

Sid Meier designed it in 1991 at MicroProse with Bruce Shelley. It is the game in which Meier's method, a simple rule and a deep consequence, receives history as a playground. The engine is a tile map on DOS. Units move across squares, cities produce, and the technology tree is a table that pretends to be fate.

The decision to offer more than one path to victory changes what a mistake is. The consequence: a military defeat need not be the end if your science or your wonders are still running. In 1991, beside Railroad Tycoon, Meier stretches the same appetite for a system from one railway onto whole epochs.

Governments, from despotism to democracy, change how cities and units behave. Politics is not the recoloring of a flag. What has aged is the combat. Two units on a square are a coarse war model, and later entries will wrestle with it under the nickname of the stack of doom.

What has not aged is the fantasy of a long horizon. The player still believes that the next discovery will rewrite the meaning of the last twenty turns. The GDC preserves Meier's postmortem. For a seminar it is useful as an author's reading, not as a substitute for the rules themselves.

Barbarians, wonders, and the palace you build as a reward are a valve between a dry table and a game. Without them, history would be only an account. When you say 4X, do not forget that the word is only just settling in around Civilization. Here the tree and the turn matter more than the later label.
