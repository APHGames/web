Heroes develop a fantasy kingdom and assemble armies on the map. Extremely readable rules, faction variety, and the map as a puzzle created an exceptionally durable multiplayer strategy.

On the map you read the mines, the creature banks, and the distance to the enemy castle before the hex battle even arrives. The map is the puzzle and the army is the answer, not the other way around. The factions share rules and do not share units or the mood of the castle. Readability is high: after one battle a student knows what a shooter is, what a walking corpse is, and why it stands in the second row.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/265-cover-1.jpg)

New World Computing and Jon Van Caneghem released it in 1999, already under the wing of 3DO. The map editor and the later expansions, led by Armageddon's Blade and The Shadow of Death, turn the box into a platform for many years. The decision to leave the rules almost transparent had a consequence for multiplayer and for hotseat. A match can be judged because both players see the same mathematics, and that is why the game outlives the monitor resolution it was born on.

In 1999, beside Age of Empires II, these are two durable strategies. One is real-time and historical, the other turn-based and fantasy, and both believe a faction should be a personality, not only a bonus. What has aged is the resolution and some behavior of the computer hero, whom you fool with a repeated trick. What has not aged is a battle you can learn and the map as an order of rewards and risks.

In seminar, hand out a small random map and let students only read for the first three turns. Whoever sets off without reading loses a resource, not a reflex. The hero is a carrier of skills and spells. An army without him is material, and he without an army is a target for an enemy patrol, which keeps both layers in the game.

Against the second game this one is stricter in balance and more generous in maps. The formula of 1996 grows up here, and that is why the third game is cited as the one people stay with. Custom maps are a second life. A design that does not let the player at the editor would die with the campaign, and the authors of the series already know that.

The hex is not nostalgia. It is a way to have flanks and shooting lines without the unfair corner of a square grid. The game works as an introduction to TBS because a loss is explainable. A student can say why they lost the battle, and that is more than the atmosphere of a castle.
