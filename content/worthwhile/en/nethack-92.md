A procedural dungeon is full of items with very complex mutual reactions. Extreme systemic quality and a multitude of emergent solutions became the measure of deep roguelike design.

You descend into a dungeon that is generated anew, you eat corpses, you drink unknown potions, and you die permanently, often from an interaction you did not expect, because nobody wrote it as a single scene. An animal you go to feed, a shop you rob, and a writing on the floor that changes how monsters behave are the same language: things react to one another even when you are not standing in a fight.

The NetHack DevTeam has been assembling the game since 1987 as an open successor to Hack, which itself followed Rogue. It is not one company's product; it is shared code that spreads across Unix and across schools. ASCII and the keys are an interface that is poor on purpose. All the ambition is in the table of effects, not in the picture, and so the game fits in a terminal and yet has no bottom.

The decision to let objects react to one another, not only to the player, had the consequence that solutions appear which the authors did not have to list. An emergent move is the measure of quality here, not an accident to be patched. The amulet and the climb upward give a long game a direction. It is not pure wandering: a goal exists, but the path to it is a forest of interactions, not a corridor.

In 1987 the commercial dungeon on the Atari ST looks like Dungeon Master, full of sound and a mouse. NetHack is the opposite wager of the same year: no spectacle, a maximal system. Death is comic and cruel at once, because the cause is usually a concrete sentence of the rules. The player learns to read their own obituary.

What has aged is a control scheme that without a crib sheet will not let a newcomer past the first stairs, and a humor that assumes you will endure ten futile runs. What has not aged is the density: two ordinary things in one pocket can be a plan, or they can be the end.

From NetHack on, we measure what a deep roguelike means once a random map and permadeath are no longer enough. In a seminar, let the students die and then read aloud what killed them. The lesson is not that they died, but that the cause is a rule that can be named.

NetHack is a free and open-source, single-player roguelike video game developed by the NetHack Development Team (also known as the DevTeam), a group of developers who collaborate over the internet. NetHack has been cited as an influence on later games, including Diablo.

As a roguelike, NetHack features turn-based, grid-based dungeon crawling gameplay, procedurally generated levels, and permadeath. There are three major antagonists in NetHack: the Luciferesque god Moloch, who stole the Amulet of Yendor from the creator god Marduk; the high priest (or priestess) of Moloch, who holds the Amulet of Yendor; and the most prominent antagonist, the Wizard of Yendor, who will stalk the player throughout the rest of the game after the first encounter by resurrecting and attacking them periodically.

As in many other roguelike games, all items in NetHack are either "blessed", "uncursed", or "cursed". As in other roguelike games, NetHack features permadeath: expired characters cannot be revived.

Although NetHack can be completed without any artificial limitations, experienced players can attempt "conducts" for an additional challenge. Such deaths are considered part of learning to play NetHack as to avoid conditions where the same death may happen again.

NetHack is a software derivative of Hack, which itself was inspired by Rogue. They called themselves the DevTeam and renamed their branch NetHack since their collaboration work was done over the Internet.

Knowing of the multiple forks of Hack that existed, the DevTeam established a principle that while the game was open source and anyone could create a fork as a new project, only a few select members in the DevTeam could make modifications to the main source repository of the game, so that players could be assured that the DevTeam's release was the legitimate version of NetHack. The core DevTeam had expanded with the release of NetHack 3.0 in July 1989.

Stephenson said that despite the number of roguelike titles that had emerged since the v3.4.3 release, they saw that NetHack was still being talked about online in part due to its high degree of portability, and decided to continue its development. NetHack is released under the NetHack General Public License, which was written in 1989 by Mike Stephenson, patterned after the GNU bison license (which was written by Richard Stallman in 1988).

Like the Bison license, and Stallman's later GNU General Public License, the NetHack license was written to allow the free sharing and modification of the source code under its protection. The NetHack General Public License is a copyleft software license certified as an open source license by the Open Source Initiative.

The NetHack General Public License allows anyone to port the game to a platform not supported by the official DevTeam, provided that they use the same license. Bugs, humorous messages, stories, experiences, and ideas for the next version are discussed on the Usenet newsgroup rec.games.roguelike.nethack.

A public server at nethack.alt.org, commonly known as "NAO", gives players access to NetHack through a Telnet or SSH interface. During the whole month of November, the annual /dev/null NetHack Tournament took place every year from 1999 to 2016.

The November NetHack Tournament, initially conceived as a one-time tribute to devnull, has taken place each year since 2018. The Facebook artificial intelligence (AI) research team, along with researchers at the University of Oxford, New York University, the Imperial College London, and University College London, developed an open-source platform called the NetHack Learning Environment, designed to teach AI agents to play NetHack.

The base environment is able to maneuver the agent and fight its way through dungeons, but the team seeks community help to build an AI on the complexities of NetHack interconnected systems, using implicit knowledge that comes from player-made resources, thus giving a means for programmers to hook into the environment with additional resources. Facebook's research led the company to pose NetHack as a grand challenge in AI in June 2021, in part due to the game's permadeath and inability to experiment with the environment without creating a reaction.

NetHack has influenced ADOM, Minecraft, Spelunky, Diablo, and Mystery Dungeon. NetHack was also included in a list of the best open source video games by The Gamer.
