A trainer catches, trains, and trades monsters on the way to the championship. The Link Cable turned single-player collecting into a social economy of trade and competition.

On the Game Boy you catch creatures in balls, teach them moves, and lead them through the Kanto championship, but you cannot close the Pokédex alone. Some monsters live only in Red, some only in Green, and some evolve only through a trade over the cable. The collection is deliberately impossible for one player to finish.

Satoshi Tajiri, Ken Sugimori, and Junichi Masuda built it at Game Freak over many years; Tajiri started from insect collecting and from the idea that a cable between two Game Boys was enough for a social game. It runs on the eight-bit Game Boy from 1989, in 1996 already a machine the industry treated as fading. The data fit on the cartridge partly thanks to programming help from Satoru Iwata at HAL Laboratory.

The decision to make a trade a condition of completion has a consequence that turned the console into a playground. Children meet not only to battle, but to make a deal the game refuses to settle for them. In Japan, Red and Green came out on 27 February 1996. Western Red and Blue arrived two years later and stand on a revised version, not on exactly the same code.

In 1996 a big RPG is expected on the SNES or on PC. Game Freak puts one hundred and fifty-one creatures in a pocket and bets that the social loop will beat graphics. The type chart, a team of six, and a rival who returns are simple rules. Their strength is that they can be explained on a school bench without a manual.

What has aged is a random encounter every few steps, the need to carry a creature in the team only for the hidden HM techniques, and the need for a guide at a few puzzles. What has not aged is the collection as an unfinished thing, the trade as an economy, and the feeling that a monster in the pocket is a project, not only an enemy in a fight.

In seminar it is an example of a game that needs another person as part of the system, not as an extra in a menu. If students today open only the solo campaign, they see half the design. The other half begins at the moment the cable is missing.

In Japan, Pocket Monsters Red and Green were the first versions released. Using Blastoise as its mascot, the code, script, and artwork for Blue were used for the international releases of Red and Green, which were renamed to Red and Blue.

Despite these setbacks, the reprogrammed Red and Blue versions with their original creature designs were eventually released in North America on September 28, 1998, over two and a half years after Red and Green debuted in Japan. Two years after Red, Green and Blue Nintendo released Pokémon Yellow, an enhanced version of Red and Blue, in Japan in 1998, and in North America and Europe in 1999 and 2000.

As was the case with its original release, Green is exclusive to Japanese consumers. In Japan Red, Green, and Blue sold 1.04 million units combined during 1996, and another 3.65 million in 1997.

Pokémon Red, Green and Blue ultimately sold 10.23 million copies in Japan, and until November 2022, was the country's best-selling video games. In 2017, The Strong National Museum of Play inducted Pokémon Red and Green to its World Video Game Hall of Fame.
