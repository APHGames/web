A shipwrecked pilot moves freely around a planet and looks for a way to escape. A surprisingly open 3D world foreshadowed the sandbox exploration tradition.

A shipwrecked pilot moves around a planet in a wireframe city, takes commissions from rival sides, and looks for a way to leave, without the game telling them the single correct path. Benson, the onboard computer, is your partner in the text. The world is large, the information is piecemeal, and freedom also means you can betray the side you are currently working for.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/068-cover-1.jpg)

Paul Woakes wrote it for Novagen, and in 1985 it was released on the Atari 8-bit and the Commodore 64. It is an auteur simulation of space from a small British workshop, not a large American team. The city of Targ is wireframe, and yet you can live in it for hours: the hangar, the ship, the ground battle, and the palace are modes of one planet, not separate games in a menu.

The decision to let the player choose both a faction and a way of escape had the consequence that progress is not a level. It is an accumulation of money, access, and the courage to change sides. The rivals, the Palyars and the Mechanoids, are not scenery of good and evil. They are two employers, and you are a mercenary, which the title of the game states honestly.

In 1985 Elite is still teaching trade between the stars beside it. Mercenary pulls that lesson down onto one planet and asks whether openness can also carry a city, not only hyperspace. The graphics are poor exactly as far as the machine can bear, and yet they feel like a place. Finding your way by the corners is part of the skill.

What has aged is the slowness of wireframe movement and an interface that, without notes on paper, lets a newcomer out into an empty street. What has not aged is the freedom to have a goal, to leave, and still to choose whom you will harm on the way.

A thread runs from Targ to later cities that do not lead you with an arrow and yet promise that a ship can be obtained somewhere. In a seminar, do not hand out a map. The first ten minutes of being lost are a more precise account of openness than a shot of the whole city.

Mercenary is a 3D action-adventure game written for the Atari 8-bit computers and published by Novagen Software in 1985. It was also released as Mercenary: Escape from Targ and Mercenary: A Flight Simulator Adventure.

Mercenary was favourably reviewed and followed by two sequels: Damocles and Mercenary III: The Dion Crisis. Mercenary received strong positive reviews from gaming magazines across all platforms, with the original Mercenary receiving a Gold Medal from Zzap!64 magazine.

The ZX Spectrum version of Mercenary was voted number 15 in the Your Sinclair Readers' Top 100 Games of All Time. Steve Jarratt, editor of Edge, listed Mercenary as one of his favorite games of all time and the game was ranked 67th in the magazine's list of top 100 games in 2000.

Damocles (also advertised as Mercenary II) is the second game in the series. Mercenary III (also known as Mercenary III: The Dion Crisis or Damocles II) is the third game in the series.

Mercenary and The Second City were later combined into the Mercenary Compendium. The program, called MDDClone (Mercenary, Damocles, Dion crisis Clone) is freeware, includes all three games, selectable through a drop-down list.

In 2018, an open source port of Damocles and Mercenary III was released. Called Mercenary Reloaded, this port offers support for higher resolutions, a higher frame rate with smoother movements, virtual reality as per the specifications of the Oculus Rift head-mounted display, and a separate side-by-side stereoscopic mode.
