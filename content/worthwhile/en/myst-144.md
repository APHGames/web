The player explores a quiet island and reconstructs the story of the Atrus family. The CD-ROM made visually rich, slow exploration possible; the world communicated mainly through the environment instead of a UI.

You stand on an island, turn through pre-rendered shots, and assemble the story of Atrus's family from books, machines, and silence. You have neither a weapon nor a score. Progress is in understanding how each age works, and only then turning the page of a linking book.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/144-cover-1.jpg)

The brothers Rand and Robyn Miller made the game in 1993 at Cyan on the Macintosh, and Broderbund published it. Robyn's music is part of the space, not an underscore at the end. Technically it is HyperCard and a CD-ROM. Each shot is a painted image, not an engine that would compute the world in real time.

The decision to hide the interface inside the environment has a consequence. A player who does not play action games is not afraid to enter, because there is nothing for a reflex to ruin. In 1993 Myst gives a reason to buy a CD drive. Doom in the same year gives a reason to have a network; both games widen who sits at a PC at all.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/144-screenshot-2.jpg)

Sirrus and Achenar are two voices in the books. The moral choice comes late and it is short, because the main time was spent on understanding, not on fighting. What has aged is the obscurity of some puzzles and the stillness of the shots. Without a hint the island is beautiful and a mute test of patience.

What has not aged is storytelling through space. A machine you leave running says more than a character who would read the plot to you. The individual ages, from the island to the mechanical one and the forest one, are separate lessons. The game is a collection of places, not one long corridor.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/144-screenshot-3.jpg)

Do not cite sales charts. Say that a quiet game without combat became a defining CD-ROM experience in 1993, which itself changes whom the industry considers a player. In a seminar, turn the sound off and try one puzzle. Then turn it on. For the Millers, sound is often the only hint the game is willing to give.
