A fighter penetrates a fortress and fights one-on-one duels. Rotated animation references and cinematic staging foreshadowed Mechner's Prince of Persia.

A fighter runs through a fortress in side view and, in one-on-one duels, waits for the right moment to strike, not for a shower of projectiles. The walk has weight. The animation is not decoration on top of a hitbox, but the way the game tells you when you are covered and when you are open.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/054-cover-1.jpg)

Jordan Mechner wrote it as a student, and Broderbund published it in 1984 on the Apple II. He filmed the movement and redrew it, so the body on the screen comes from filmed reference, not from a hand assembling a sprite square by square. The Apple II can carry this rotoscoping only in a limited palette and at a limited speed. That very limit gives the fights the character of a film shot: one figure, one background, a clear cut.

The decision to film real movement had the consequence that the player reads intention from the body's posture. The duel is understandable even at the moment the controls are strict. The ending, in which it matters whether you approach the princess in a fighting stance, punishes the player for having thought of the whole game as only a brawl. The gesture is a rule.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/054-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 1984 a platformer is mostly a jump and an enemy on a ledge. Karateka offers the duel as a scene with a director, and thereby opens Mechner's road to Prince of Persia. It is not a tournament with health in the corner of the screen like later fighting games. It is a path through a fortress in which the fist is part of the walk.

What has aged is the severity of the timing, which without patience feels like unfairness, and the simplicity of the path once you know the duels. What has not aged is the weight of the step and the fact that animation can be information, not padding between two hits.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/054-screenshot-3.jpg)

Prince of Persia, five years later, takes the same rotoscope and adds a dungeon, a time limit, and a jump that has physics. In a seminar, turn the sound off and watch only the approach. If the students recognize the strike before it lands, Mechner's wager still holds.

Karateka was ported to the Amstrad CPC, Atari 8-bit computers, Atari 7800, Atari ST, Commodore 64, MS-DOS, Nintendo Entertainment System, ZX Spectrum, PC-98, MSX, and Game Boy. Karateka uses gameplay elements found in both side-scrolling 2D platformers and fighting games.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/054-screenshot-4.png)

Karateka was developed by Jordan Mechner while he was a student at Yale University as a side project between classes. A Game Boy port was done with the name Master Karateka for Asia and featured changes such as the inclusion of an experience system.

In early 1985, Jeff Hurlbert of Hardcore Computist said the "recently released" Karateka "is the most recent and best illustration of a trend towards computer games that look like movies". In January 1985, Computer Entertainer rated the Apple II version 7½ out of 8 stars, praising the visuals, animation, sound effects, music, karate moves, and story line, but criticizing the blonde-haired appearance of the karateka and princess in a Japanese setting.

Although critical of the "often slow" joystick control system, the magazine concluded that Karateka was "fun and extremely addicting". In February 2012, Mechner announced that he was leading a small independent development group to create a remake of Karateka for the Xbox 360 via Xbox Live Arcade and PlayStation 3 via PlayStation Network.
