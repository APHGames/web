An Earth expedition discovers the living planet Albion, threatened by mining. It combines 2D towns, first-person dungeons, and ecological sci-fi in an ambitious European RPG.

An Earth expedition lands on a planet that is alive, and in a party you alternate towns drawn from the side with dungeons in the first person. The two images are one world, not two games in the box. The threat is mining. The conflict is not a dark lord at the end of the map; it is an industry that wants to take the planet apart, so ecological science fiction is a rule of the quest, not a blurb.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/193-cover-1.jpg)

Blue Byte released the game in 1995 on DOS. After The Settlers this is their second ambition of the same decade: not the flows of resources in a settlement, but a European RPG that is not afraid to be strange. The engine pastes two-dimensional towns onto a stepped first-person view. Technically it is a hybrid of a time when Underworld had already shown a free step, and here the dungeon is still a grid with an illustration.

The decision to split the presentation has a consequence. A town can be social and a dungeon claustrophobic, but the seam between them is felt, and the player has to accept it as the price of the ambition. In 1995 the large computer RPG in America is heading toward the dungeon or toward Ultima. Albion sits to the side: a Celtic-technical planet, a German studio, no attempt to pretend to be Dungeons and Dragons.

The party and the dialogue hold the genre. You are not a lone marine; you are an expedition that has to understand a culture before it starts defending it. What has aged are the interface and the slow first-person combat. The passage between modes can be labor, not a cut that would join the world.

What has not aged is the premise that a planet can be a side in the conflict. Later games about mining and a living world still deal with the same guilt of an expedition. Compare the hybrid with Dungeon Master. There, one view and no town; here the town is necessary, because without it the ecological dispute would have no one to talk to.

In a seminar, do not judge the game by the smoothness of the transition. Judge whether the two modes say one sentence about what is happening to the planet. With this, Blue Byte shows that a European studio in 1995 can want more than an economic simulation. Albion is that ambition, and its seam.

Albion is a role-playing video game, developed and published by Blue Byte for MS-DOS. Albion received positive reviews following its launch, earning praise for its originality in combat, story and puzzles.

Albion is a role-playing video game and features a number of gameplay elements typical of that genre. Albion uses a hybrid 2D/3D graphical system to depict its environments.

Albion takes place in the distant future during the 23rd century, where human technology on Earth has developed to the point that spaceships can venture out to distant star system with hyperspace drives, funded by corporations rather than local governments. Two months after the crash, the pair, learning that the planet holds life, find themselves amongst the iskai, a sentient species living on the planet, which they call Albion.

Seeking knowledge required to forge the weapon, Tom eventually leads an assault on the Toronto, dealing with Ned's security measures and informing the colonists about the truth concerning Albion; they eventually abandon the colony. Tom, relieved the situation is resolved, decides the group's next job is to inform the human colonists that they must now make a new life on Albion.

Writing for PC Gamer US, Michael Wolf called Albion "a fun, engrossing, and involved roleplaying experience", with "all the elements of a great roleplaying adventure." which ultimately went to The Elder Scrolls II: Daggerfall. The reviewer for Next Generation called Albion "well-thought out and definitely worth checking out", and praised it as a "bright spot in the desert" of computer role-playing games at the time.

Chuck Klimushyn of Computer Games Strategy Plus considered Albion to be "a breath of fresh air for the RPG crowd", and he wrote that "those who cut their teeth on the likes of Might and Magic, Wizardry, and Ultima may find that the future looks a little brighter because of this latest offering from Blue Byte." Albion was a runner-up for Computer Game Entertainment s 1996 "Best Role-Playing Game" prize. The magazine's editors wrote, "Albion was refreshing, not only because it helped fill a void, but because it carefully balanced story, puzzles, combat and exploration." It was also nominated in this category by CNET Gamecenter, whose editors gave the prize to Meridian 59.

On July 29, 2015, Albion was re-released on the digital distributor GOG.com, albeit only the original version and DOSBox, not the newer port.
