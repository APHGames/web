A Roman family conquers the Mediterranean in a turn-based campaign and real-time battles. Large-scale 3D armies gave the Total War formula visual readability and mass appeal.

In Rome: Total War you manage a family, the Senate, and the provinces in turns, and a battle you do not want calculated you play in three dimensions with thousands of soldiers at once. Morale, a cavalry flank, and the terrain decide more than the sum of attacks. A unit that turns its back is lost even when, on paper, it still has men.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/344-cover-1.jpg)

Creative Assembly published it in 2004 through Activision as the third Total War after Shogun and Medieval. The three-dimensional mass is readable enough here, for the first time, that a player who had previously played only hero combat can see the strategy. The consequence of the large armies is that the tactical layer stops being an abstract icon on the map. The player sees the envelopment and therefore believes that a campaign decision had a body.

In 2004, beside Warcraft III, this is a different strategy. There, a hero and a base. Here, a house, a front, and a battle that lasts until someone breaks. Sieges and diplomacy have aged into the series' familiar complaints. The spectacle of a coherent mass and the readability of the flanks have not, and every later Total War inherits them.

Generals are members of a family, not an anonymous stack. A death in the field is a political event on the next turn, and therefore the campaign is not only a map with numbers. Automatic battle resolution is a temptation. Whoever uses it always misses the reason the game has a second layer at all, and the campaign flattens into a table.

For scale students it is a class about how many bodies one screen can carry before they become a texture. Creative Assembly found a boundary here that still feels like an army. Barbarian Invasion later breaks the order of the empire. The base game is still the one that teaches how a Roman line holds until cavalry gets around it.

The historical simplifications are conscious. This is not a simulation of an archive; it is a toy about why a phalanx fears its flank. When you play it today, play the first battle without pausing for as long as you can. In that confusion one can still see what three-dimensional Total War added in 2004 to the turn-based map.

Rome: Total War is a strategy video game developed by The Creative Assembly and originally published by Activision; its publishing rights have since passed to Sega. The game is the third title in The Creative Assembly's Total War series, following Shogun: Total War, and Medieval: Total War.

The game's main campaign takes place from 270 BC to 14 AD, showcasing the rise and final centuries of the Republican period and the early decades of the imperial period of Ancient Rome. Upon its release, Rome: Total War received widespread acclaim from critics.

As with previous games in the Total War series, Rome: Total War has two primary modes of play: a turn-based, single-player campaign that takes place on an overhead map of the world and a real-time battle system that occurs on 3D battlefields. Both series also used the same music track as the battles in Rome: Total War.

Rome: Total War allows for the manipulation of some game resources, including its text files and textures, which has led to the creation of modifications. According to The NPD Group, Rome: Total War was the 20th-best-selling computer game of 2004.

At the time, this led Edge to declare it the country's 40th-best-selling computer game, and best-selling Total War title, released since January 2000. The series as a whole, including Rome, sold 1.3 million units in the United States by August 2006.

By 2013, Rome: Total War alone had totaled 876,000 sales in the region. Rome sold at least 100,000 units in the German market by December 2004.

Computer Games Magazine named Rome: Total War the fifth-best computer game of 2004. The editors wrote, "If there's a magic formula for how to make a great strategy game, Creative Assembly has it down pat." The editors of Computer Gaming World nominated Rome as their 2004 "Strategy Game of the Year (Real-Time)", although it lost to Warhammer 40,000: Dawn of War.

On July 2, 2012, The Creative Assembly announced the development of Total War: Rome II as the next edition of the Total War series. Rome II became its successor on 3 September 2013 when it was released, featuring gameplay during the time of the Roman Republic and Empire, a larger campaign map, as well as a number of game mechanics both new and carried over from previous Total War entries.

On March 25, 2021, 16 and a half years after its initial release, a remastered edition named Total War: Rome Remastered was announced. The reception was positive with reviews stating that the remastered edition was the best way to play Rome: Total War today, but that the game mechanics felt outdated compared to more recent releases in the Total War series.
