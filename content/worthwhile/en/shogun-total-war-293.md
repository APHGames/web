A Japanese daimyo leads a turn-based empire and real-time armies. The split into a strategic map and giant tactical battles established the Total War formula.

On a turn-based map of Japan you are a daimyo: provinces, diplomacy, seasons. When a battle comes, the camera drops to thousands of soldiers, and morale, formation, and terrain decide whether the army runs. The two scales are not two modes for decoration. A campaign without a battle would be a board game, and a battle without a campaign would be a skirmish; Total War is the join, and the studio will repeat that join for decades.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/293-screenshot-1.jpg)

Creative Assembly released it in Britain in 2000 with EA. The bet is technical: an army, not a squad, is supposed to fit on the screen, and in 2000 the PC finally begins to carry that as a spectacle, not as icons. The decision not to break the war into a single real time had a consequence for pace. You think in the turn and you get frightened in the battle, and autoresolve is a temptation that reveals whether the player came for the map or for the clash.

In 2000 Homeworld and Age of Empires II stand beside Shogun. Three ways to command, and this game is the one that takes the scale of the soldier literally: a person in the field is small, and the number is the argument. What has aged is the thinner campaign if you compare it with later entries, and the diplomacy, which cannot be as interesting as the roar of cavalry. What has not aged is the rout according to morale and the feeling that you command a crowd, not a hero with a sword.

In seminar, let them finish one battle and calculate one with autoresolve. Students will see where the game hides its identity and where it sets it aside. The seasons on the strategic map are more than a color. They change movement, and by that they say that Japan here is not only a skin on generic provinces, even if the depth is not yet in every system.

Against Heroes III there is little readability of a single unit here and a great deal of mass. Fantasy turns and a historical crowd share the word strategy in one year and do not mean the same thing by it. Formation is a rule, not an animation. Whoever breaks it with terrain has won systemically, and a seminar should show that on a hill, not on a slide about genre.

Shogun: Total War is a turn-based strategy and real-time tactics video game developed by The Creative Assembly and published by Electronic Arts for Microsoft Windows personal computers. Released in June 2000, the game became the debut title in The Creative Assembly's Total War series.

Announced in early 1999, Shogun: Total War was The Creative Assembly's first high business risk product; previous products had involved creating video games for the EA Sports brand. Through the course of development, Shogun: Total War evolved into a real-time tactics game with a focus on historical authenticity; military historian Stephen Turnbull advised The Creative Assembly in this regard.

Shogun: Total War was well received critically, though complaints surfaced regarding glitches in the game. The game's positive reception and sales paved the way for the development of successive Total War releases set in different times and regions.

Total War: Shogun 2 is a sequel which returns to the Sengoku period albeit with all the improvements in graphical technology and gameplay since the first game. Shogun: Total War is focused on samurai warfare in the Sengoku period of Japanese history, which lasted from the mid-15th century to the beginning of the 17th century.

Shogun incorporates two main areas of play: The turn-based campaign map is where the player moves their armies, conducts diplomacy, builds the infrastructure of their provinces and performs various other tasks necessary to run their faction. The main campaign of Shogun: Total War involves a player choosing a clan and moving to eliminate their enemies and become shogun of feudal Japan.

Originally, Electronic Arts hosted the multiplayer for Shogun: Total War. There were two separate servers; one for Shogun: Total War, and one for Warlord Edition.

In Shogun, any army could win over another using clever, fast and precise strategies. Before Rome: Total War was launched by Activision, EA shut down both the Shogun: Total War and the Warlord Edition servers.

The players turned to the other Total War series, while the new players avoided the Shogun series. Shogun: Total War was announced in early 1999, developed by The Creative Assembly under Electronic Arts.

According to Simpson, Shogun was conceived when the Creative Assembly established a secondary development team from their EA Sports designers to develop an alternate, low business risk product for the company. Shogun: Total War was followed by The Mongol Invasion expansion pack, set in the Mongol invasions of Japan and released only in Europe on 8 August 2001.

Shogun: Total War received "favorable" reviews according to the review aggregation website Metacritic. During the 4th Annual Interactive Achievement Awards, the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences nominated Shogun: Total War for the "Computer Innovation" award, which ultimately went to Deus Ex.
