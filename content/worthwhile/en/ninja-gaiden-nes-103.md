Ninja Ryu Hayabusa fights through fast levels and follows a story of revenge. "Cinema scenes" between levels substantially expanded the narrative ambitions of console action.

As the ninja Ryu Hayabusa you run along walls, cut with a sword, and in each stretch look for a jumping rhythm that carries you on without stopping. Between levels the game stops and plays a revenge scene. On the Famicom in 1988, a story this long inside an action game is still unusual.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/103-screenshot-1.jpg)

Tecmo developed it as Ninja Ryūkenden. Hideo Yoshizawa took the direction, and the Western NES release came shortly after the Japanese one. The engine is classic 8-bit side-scrolling: fixed enemy screens, a limited sprite count, and physics that do not forgive a late press.

The decision to separate plot from jumping has a clear consequence. Even a player who cannot clear a stretch receives the next chapter, so the story holds together despite a high death rate. In 1988, alongside Double Dragon and the early beat 'em ups, Tecmo bets on the speed of one body, not on two players side by side.

The difficulty is part of the design. The supply of lives is short and a few falls rewind a whole section, so mastery is memory of the route. What has aged is precisely this punishment. A player today expects more frequent saving and more readable attack telegraphs, which eight bits draw only roughly.

What has not aged is the joining of a wall climb and a strike in one movement. The controls are still a legible promise: a run-up, a blade, the next ledge. The film scenes are static portraits and text, not video. On the hardware of the time Tecmo extracts from them a serial that an action game had rarely had.

Revenge, a father, and a hostile clan give the jump a reason. Without those interludes this would be another ninja; with them, a death in a pit has a plot. In a seminar, take it as proof that console storytelling did not have to wait for CD. A cut between a run and a conversation was enough.

Ninja Gaiden, released in Japan as and as Shadow Warriors in Europe, is a 1988 hack and slash platform game developed and published by Tecmo for the Nintendo Entertainment System. Set in a retro-futuristic version of 1988, the story follows a ninja named Ryu Hayabusa as he journeys to America to avenge his murdered father.

Ninja Gaiden has an elaborate story told through anime-like cinematic cutscenes. Director Hideo Yoshizawa named Ninja Gaiden as his most commercially successful project, with over cartridges sold worldwide.

The game was followed by Ninja Gaiden II: The Dark Sword of Chaos (1990) and Ninja Gaiden III: The Ancient Ship of Doom (1991). In Japan, Ninja Ryu Hayabusa finds a letter by his father, Ken, who was seemingly killed in a duel by an unknown assailant.

Ninja Gaiden was developed and released around the same time as the beat 'em up arcade version of the same name; neither of the games were ports of each other but were parallel projects developed by different teams. The game was released in Japan on December 9, 1988, under its final title Ninja Ryūkenden, which roughly translates to Legend of the Dragon Sword.

They decided to use the title Ninja Gaiden (its original working title) when the game was released in the U.S. The title literally means "Ninja Side-Story", but the game was not intended as a spin-off of any prior work.

It was retitled as Shadow Warriors—just as Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles was renamed Teenage Mutant Hero Turtles—as ninjas were considered a taboo subject in Europe. It was one of many ninja-related video games around the time, such as The Legend of Kage, Ninja Warriors, and Shinobi.

Upon Ninja Gaiden s North American release, Nintendo of America, whose play-testers liked the game and gave it high ratings, decided to help with its marketing. Ninja Gaiden received strong publicity in Nintendo Power in 1989 and 1990.

Ninja Gaiden received preview coverage in the January–February 1989 issue of Nintendo Power in its "Pak Watch" section. Its display featured a demo of the game and a live person dressed as a ninja.

A PC Engine port of Ninja Ryūkenden was produced in 1992, published by Hudson Soft and released only in Japan. Ninja Gaiden appeared as a remake of the Ninja Gaiden Trilogy compilation for the Super NES in 1995.

Along with the other two games in the Ninja Gaiden trilogy, the SNES version was featured as an unlockable game in Ninja Gaiden for the Xbox. In July 1990 Scholastic Corporation published a novelization of Ninja Gaiden under the Worlds of Power series of NES game adaptations, created and packaged by Seth Godin under the pseudonym F.

In 2017, Brave Wave Productions released a vinyl box set, Ninja Gaiden- the Definitive Soundtrack, mastered by original composer Keiji Yamagishi. According to Tecmo USA's Dimitri Criona, high demand led to repeated orders for Ninja Gaiden in increments of 60,000 cartridges per order.

Ninja Gaiden went on to sell about 300,000 units in Japan and units in the United States, for a total of over cartridges sold worldwide. The staff said that Ninja Gaiden "proved to be an instant winner" with its cinematic cutscenes and unique gameplay.

The July 1990 pilot issue of UK magazine Mean Machines featured Ninja Gaiden on the cover; the magazine was distributed as part of the July 1990 issue of Computer and Video Games. He said it is one of the best arcade-style games on the NES as well as the best ninja-related game on the system.

The first installment, titled Ninja Gaiden Episode I: Destiny, was released on July 15, 2004; it included only the first Act from the NES version but added two new levels. The mobile phone port of Ninja Gaiden was met with some praise and criticism.

Retro Gamer took a look back at Ninja Gaiden in its March 2004 issue, when the Xbox reboot was released. A 1UP.com review noted that the two games have different dynamics and that several actions possible in Ninja Gaiden would be impossible in Castlevania.

IGN said that the game was one of the most difficult video games of all time, setting the trend for the rest of the series; however, they pointed out that its difficulty and graphics are "defining characteristics [that] have carried over through the years into modern day [Ninja] Gaiden sequels". Ninja Gaiden has maintained its position as one of the most popular games for the NES.
