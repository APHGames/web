A castaway survives in the ocean of an alien planet and proceeds ever deeper. Depth, pressure, oxygen, and biomes give survival crafting a strong vertical and ecological structure.

In Subnautica you crash on an ocean planet, and the player dives deeper and deeper, because oxygen, pressure, and hunger are three clocks that tick differently on the surface and in a trench. You build a base, the Seamoth, the Prawn, and the Cyclops, and you gradually discover that a biome is a vertical chapter. You read the story in a PDA and in the architecture of an alien civilization, not in mandatory firefights.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/472-cover-1.jpg)

Unknown Worlds and Charlie Cleveland, known from Natural Selection, kept the game in early access from 2014 and released the full version in January 2018. Unity carries the water, the bases, and animals that have a territory rather than a boss ladder. The decision to make depth the progression means better equipment is above all permission to go lower. The consequence is a fear of dark water that is not a jump scare, but distance from air.

Leviathans are inhabitants of a biome, not a mandatory duel. The studio often rewards you for surviving by going around them, which is a different fantasy from a shooter about trophies. Food, water, and base power keep the survival grounded. Anyone who wants only the story still has to cook and recharge, because the planet is not a corridor.

In 2018 the full release closes four years of public development. In the meantime the game became the main example of how early access can mature into one dense expedition, not into an endless service. Below Zero will later take the same language into another region. The original diving remains the cleaner vertical from the shallows to the trench.

The crafting interface has aged, as have some routes that lose their secret once the map is revealed. Some of the middle game is also repeated gathering of materials between the big descents. The ecology of depth holds. It is still the best example of a survival game whose map has a downward direction and whose horror is that you can see too far into the dark.

The Cyclops and the Prawn change the scale of the body. Suddenly you are a submarine, not a diver, and the animal that was hunting you has a different size. In class, use it for vertical level design: let students name what changes when the next area is not beyond a mountain, but beneath you and without air.
