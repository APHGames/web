The USA and the USSR compete for global influence without triggering a nuclear war. It models conflict as escalation and diplomatic risk instead of conventional conquest of the map.

You play a superpower and try to gain influence in the world without the escalation with the other side ending in a nuclear war in which everyone loses. You do not send armies across hexes as in a classic war game. You send aid, pressure, and insurgents, and then you read whether the opponent will back down or harden.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/067-cover-1.jpg)

Chris Crawford wrote it, and Mindscape published it in 1985 on the Macintosh. Crawford had previously made strategy games at Atari; here he is on his own and he takes contemporary politics as a system, not as the scenery of a shooter. The interface is the serious window of the Macintosh: maps, tables, no soldier sprite. A computer sold as a tool here receives a game that pretends to be a tool.

The decision that a nuclear ending should be a loss for both sides had the consequence that victory is moderation. A player who wants to conquer the map gets a black screen, not a fanfare. A crisis escalates in steps you can refuse. The tension is not in a reflex, but in whether you can bear a loss of prestige in exchange for the world remaining.

In 1985 the Cold War is still the frame of the news. Balance of Power takes that frame literally, in the same season when Missile Command in the arcades is still painting explosions as a sport. The game is strict with heroism. There is no secret weapon that will buy you out of an escalation without a consequence.

What has aged is the abstraction of countries into numbers and the certainty of a bipolar world, which after 1989 will stop being an obvious map. What has not aged is the mechanic of deterrence: the power you must not use is a larger part of the game than the power you do use.

From Crawford comes a branch of strategy games that take politics more seriously than a count of tanks. In a seminar, let the students win at any price. The nuclear ending, if they reach it, is a lecture you do not have to finish for them.

Balance of Power is a strategy video game of geopolitics during the Cold War, created by Chris Crawford and published in 1985 on the Macintosh by Mindscape, followed by ports to a variety of platforms over the next two years. Balance of Power was successful on the Mac, and combined with ports it ultimately sold over a quarter million units.

In 2006, The Guardian listed it first on its list of "The 10 political games everyone should play". After Balance of Power, Crawford began work on an entirely new game, Trust & Betrayal: The Legacy of Siboot, released in 1987 and selling only a few thousand copies.

Mindscape was disappointed, and pressured Crawford to do a follow-up to Balance of Power. This sequel was released in 1989 as Balance of Power: The 1990 Edition on the Apple IIGS, Windows, Macintosh, Amiga and Atari ST.

Crawford does not consider it a proper sequel, saying he was simply "tidying up, adding some bells and whistles." Balance of Power: The 1990 Edition adds more countries, advisors to help the player, a new "multi-polar" level that allows countries to generate events of their own (such as declaring war on other countries) and a 2-player hotseat mode. As a demo, Crawford used Storytron to build Balance of Power: 21st Century.

Balance of Power has been the basis for a number of play-by-mail versions, manually run and based on moves posted in internet forum systems.
