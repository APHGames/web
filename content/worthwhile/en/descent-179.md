A ship flies through robotic mines with complete freedom in three axes. A true six degrees of freedom forced the player to think about FPS space without a fixed "up."

In a ship you fly through shafts where there is no fixed up. You roll, and the floor you just left is a ceiling, so orientation is part of the fight. You shoot robots in the mines, and you hold a primary and a secondary weapon as two inventories. A level is a tube, not a corridor with a floor.

Mike Kulas and Matt Toschlog at Parallax released the game in 1995 through Interplay on DOS. After Doom this is an argument that true 3D also means losing the certainty of where the ground is. The engine is a polygonal software renderer with six degrees of freedom. Shareware again lets players in, but the contract of the space is different from id's.

The decision to abolish a fixed up has a consequence. Level design stops being a floor plan and becomes the question of whether, after a minute, the player knows the way back. In 1995 this is an answer to Doom's flatness. Descent does not only want you to look up, as Marathon does; it wants you to fly upward and get lost.

Vertigo is not a control error. It is an emotion the design wants, and that is why the first hour hurts even a player who knows shooters. What has aged are the textures and part of the disorientation, which to a player today feels like a punishment without a reward. The map in the head builds slowly.

What has not aged is the sentence of six axes. Later games that bring back free flight in a tunnel still deal with the same problem: how to mark a path when the ground is an agreement. Descent is a first-person shooter (FPS) game developed by Parallax Software and released by Interplay Productions in 1995 for MS-DOS, and later for Macintosh, PlayStation, and RISC OS. The game's success spawned expansion packs and the sequels Descent II (1996) and Descent 3 (1999).

]] Descent is a first-person shooter and shoot 'em up The ship respawns at the start of the level at the cost of a life and the player can return to the area of the demise of their previous ship to recover these power-ups. To design the levels, Descent s graphics engine uses portal rendering, which uses collections of cubes to form rooms and tunnels.

A modified version of Descent with stereoscopic graphics was released as a bundle with StereoGraphics's SimulEyes VR 3D glasses. November 22, 1995 also saw the release of Descent: Levels of the World, an add-on containing over 100 winning level submissions from a design competition held by Interplay, plus one level designed by Parallax Software.

Also in March 1996, Descent: Anniversary Edition was released, which bundled Descent, Levels of the World, as well as additional exclusive levels. On October 29, 1997, Interplay published Descent I and II: The Definitive Collection, a compilation containing the full versions of Descent, the Levels of the World mission pack, Descent II, and Vertigo mission packs, and a mission editor by Brainware.

Besides a choice of the original Descent II levels (subtitled Counterstrike), or the Vertigo Series levels, the first Descent levels (subtitled The First Strike) can be started in the Descent II game UI where robots adopt the Descent II sounds and improved AI. The original Descent program is included for players that prefer the unmodified The First Strike, as well as to run Levels of the World.

Descent was later ported to RISC OS by R-Comp Interactive in late 1998, which received a 32-bit update in 2003. A Sega 32X version of Descent was planned as the first console version, Likewise, a Panasonic M2 version was also announced but never released due to the system's cancellation.

Interplay had plans dating to mid-1996 to port Descent to Nintendo 64 under the name Ultra Descent. The port was delayed before it was eventually cancelled in 1998 in favor of Descent 3, with Parallax's Jim Boone explaining that it never reached the design phase in development.

Descent uses package files to store and load level data such as level structures, graphics, objects, and sound effects and music—similar to the WAD file format used for Doom. However, the game was withdrawn from Good Old Games in December 2015 along with Descent II and Descent 3, and later from Steam.

Interplay owned the Descent trademark and the publishing rights to those games, but their developers still retained the copyrights to them. However, in November 2017, Good Old Games announced that the Descent series would be available for sale again on their platform.

Customer reception of the shareware version of Descent was very positive, with players praising the fully 3D environment and commentators noting perceived "loyalty and goodwill" that both Parallax and Interplay fostered. Interplay estimated in March 1995 before Descent s full release that shareware copies of Descent were distributed 900,000 times via online services, on the Internet, or at retail.

The PlayStation port of Descent also received praise, which was often directed to the port's use of impressive lighting effects.
