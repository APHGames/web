Bally Midway released the machine in 1983. The game takes the mood of car chases from spy films and translates it into a vertical scroll that an arcade already knows how to read. The road branches only a little; the tension is made by the density of traffic and by the passage between asphalt and water. The scene changes, the controls stay.

The decision to allow the car to turn into a boat had the consequence that a level does not have one metaphor. The player accepts that a weapon on wheels can be a weapon on water, if the rhythm of fire remains. The music picks up the Peter Gunn theme. The cabinet promises noir before you even understand which button is the oil.

In 1983 the American market is collapsing beside it, and the machines that survive are the ones readable from the queue. Spy Hunter is readable: a car, a road, an enemy in the mirror. It is not a race for a lap as in Pole Position. It is a drive whose score is survival and destroyed traffic.

What has aged is the flatness of enemies who are types of equipment, not drivers, and the repetition of the road once you know the order of the gear. What has not aged is the transition between modes without stopping: from car to boat, and still the same hands.

Later action races in which you shoot from a vehicle still use this agreement between wheel and weapon. In a seminar, mark the second of the transformation. Ask whether the game changed, or only the scenery. A good answer is uncomfortable: a bit of both.

Spy Hunter is a 1983 vehicular combat video game developed and published by Bally Midway for arcades. Spy Hunter was a commercial success in American arcades, where it was one of the top five highest-grossing arcade games of 1984 and 1985.

Spy Hunter was followed by Spy Hunter II, which added a 3D view and two-player split-screen play, a pinball tie-in, and a successor series of games bearing the Spy Hunter name. Spy Hunter is a vertical scrolling driving game with the player in the role of a spy driving an armed sportscar.

In the United States, Spy Hunter topped the RePlay upright arcade cabinet from April to November 1984. The original Spy Hunter was followed by an arcade sequel, Spy Hunter II in 1987.

After Japanese video game developer Sunsoft ported Spy Hunter to the Nintendo Entertainment System, the company created Battle Formula (japanese: バトルフォーミュラ) with similar gameplay in 1991. Sunsoft America signed a deal with Bally Midway to release it outside Japan as Super Spy Hunter.

Spy Hunter was cloned as Major Motion, released by Microdeal for the Atari ST in 1986. The player character from Spy Hunter also appears as part of a sidequest where the player has to destroy 20 cars in the Super Sprint racetrack.

Spy Hunter was included in Midway's Greatest Arcade Hits: Volume 1 for Nintendo 64; Midway Arcade Treasures, a 2003 compilation of arcade games available for the GameCube, PlayStation 2, Xbox, and Microsoft Windows; Midway Arcade Treasures: Extended Play for PlayStation Portable; and Midway Arcade Origins, a 2012 compilation available for PlayStation 3 and Xbox 360. In the summer of 2003, Universal Pictures acquired the rights to the arcade game Spy Hunter from Midway Games.

Spy Hunter was slated to begin its budgeted $90 million production in spring 2004 in time for a summer 2005 release. By August, production had been delayed, pushing Spy Hunter back to be released in summer 2006.
