A ship flies through isometric fortresses and must watch its altitude. Isometric pseudo-3D created a distinctive sense of space and height in the shooter genre.

A ship flies obliquely over a fortress, shoots at targets, and must watch its altitude, because a wall that is an obstacle for low flight is only a line in the distance for high flight. The shadow under the ship and the altimeter are two ways of reading a third dimension on a screen that is not calculating real 3D.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/040-poster-1.jpg)

Sega released it in 1982 as an arcade game. The name refers to axonometry: the view is isometric, and that view is the main effect, not a license and not a character. The hardware assembles diagonal scrolling and the overlap of objects so that the fortress feels like a space with height. It is a trick, but a trick that holds the whole game.

The decision to make altitude a resource you have to spend had the consequence that shooting is not enough. The same enemy is a different threat depending on how high you fly. A gap in the rampart and a fuel tank teach you to plan the route, not just the reaction. A level is a fly-through with a map you read in flight.

In 1982 Pole Position stands beside it as the perspective of a road, and Q*bert as an isometric pyramid. Zaxxon takes the oblique view and puts it on a shooter. It is a distinctive Sega game in years when the arcade picture is trying to escape a pure grid from above or from the side.

What has aged is the unreadability of height at the moment too many sprites meet on the screen, and the severity of a crash into a wall you misjudged. What has not aged is the pair of shadow and altimeter: an interface that states depth without a sentence in the manual.

Later isometric action, from the arcades to home computers, still solves the same problem of how to show height where the camera lies at an angle. In a seminar, cover the altimeter and let the students fly by the shadow alone. You will learn which half of the design is the picture and which half is the instrument.

Sega released a modified version as Super Zaxxon the same year and the Zaxxon-like shooter Future Spy in 1984. At the end of the second fortress is a boss in the form of the Zaxxon robot.

Between 1982 and 1985, Zaxxon conversion of Zaxxon were released for the Apple II, Atari 8-bit computers, Atari 2600, Atari 5200, MSX, ZX Spectrum, Commodore 64, Dragon 32, ColecoVision, Intellivision, IBM PC compatibles, SG-1000, TRS-80 Color Computer, and TRS-80. II Computing listed Zaxxon fourth on its list of top Apple II games as of late 1985, based on sales and market-share data.

On home computers in the United States, Zaxxon received a "Gold Award" from the Software Publishers Association (SPA) in December 1987 for software sales above 100,000 units. Gold's home computer version of Zaxxon was ranked number two on the UK software sales chart in early 1985.

Video Games in 1983 called the ColecoVision version of Zaxxon a "coup for this new system". The magazine praised its "excellent three-dimensional graphics", and said "Zaxxon is a game that can't be praised enough".

The magazine also liked the graphics of the Apple II and TRS-80 versions despite those computers' hardware limitations, and predicted that Zaxxon would be a "long-lived bestseller". In January 1985, Electronic Games magazine included Zaxxon in its Hall of Fame.

Zaxxon is a bonus game in the Sega Genesis Collection for the PlayStation 2. US gaming critics generally found it was not similar enough to Zaxxon to justify the use of the brand.

Zaxxon Escape was released on October 4, 2012, for iOS and Android devices. In 2012, Zaxxon was shown at "The Art of Video Games" exhibition at the Smithsonian.
