A helicopter rescues hostages and fights the defenders at the same time. The task of rescuing civilians gave the shooter a strong spatial and logistical goal.

A helicopter lands in hostile territory, picks up hostages, and at the same time fires on the defenses that will not let it through in either direction. The cabin capacity is limited. You cannot rescue everyone at once, so each flight is a choice of whom you will leave on the ground under fire.

Dan Gorlin wrote it for Broderbund, and it was released on the Apple II in 1982. It is not an arcade team with a custom board, but an auteur game that carries arcade action onto a home computer. The view is from the side and scrolls horizontally. The Apple II here makes a shooter with helicopter physics: thrust, gravity, and landing, not just moving a sprite in four directions.

The decision to make the rescue of people the main score had the consequence that shooting is a means. Anyone who clears the screen and does not fly the hostages out is not doing the job. The base you return to is logistics. Resupply and drop-off are part of the rhythm just as much as a shot.

In 1982 Defender stands beside it as the arcade model of rescuing people. Choplifter takes that model, slows it into a helicopter, and adds a limited number of seats on board. Gorlin builds the game at a time when kidnapping and rescue are a public subject. The game is not a document, but the mood of the period is felt in the assignment.

What has aged is the severity of a landing on a home joystick and the repetition of the same airfield once you understand the rhythm of a wave. What has not aged is the morality of capacity: a weapon that has no room for a civilian is a badly designed weapon.

Later games in which evacuation is the goal, and fire only a condition, learn from this cabin. In a seminar, count how many people the students leave on the ground because they wanted one more pass. That number is a criticism of their priority, and the game can measure it.

Choplifter (stylized as Choplifter!) is a 1982 horizontally scrolling shooter video game developed by Dan Gorlin and published by Broderbund for the Apple II. Coming off a stint working for the Rand Corporation, Dan Gorlin initially developed Choplifter using an Apple II loaned to him by his grandfather.

Initially, Gorlin envisioned Choplifter as a 3D game, but he switched to a traditional 2D game environment due to technical limitations. II Computing listed Choplifter seventh on the magazine's list of top Apple II games as of late 1985, based on sales and market share data.

In Japan, Game Machine listed Sega's arcade version of Choplifter as the most successful table arcade unit of October 1985. After being dethroned by Taito's The Legend of Kage on November 15, Choplifter returned to the top of the chart on December 1.

In 1983, its readers named Choplifter fourth on the magazine's Top Thirty list of Atari 8-bit programs by popularity. Choplifter II, subtitled "Rescue Survive", was released for the Game Boy in 1991, then remade for both the Game Boy and Game Gear as Choplifter III in 1994.

1994 Amiga game Apocalypse was seen as significantly inspired by Choplifter. In late 1997, Gorlin and his development team, Ariok Entertainment, were working on a Choplifter game for IBM PC compatibles that would be in 3D and feature multiplayer functionality.

Sega released a pair of spiritual successors without the Choplifter brand: Air Rescue (1991) for the Sega System 32 arcade hardware is a first-person, pseudo-3D take on the concept. Air Rescue (1992) for the Master System more closely resembles the 2D Choplifter, but features stages that scroll in all directions.

Programmer Will Botti cited Choplifter as a major inspiration for his 1996 game Black Dawn. In 2004, Xicat Interactive published ChopLifter: Crisis Shield exclusively in Europe.
