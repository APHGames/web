A border official checks documents and decides the fates of migrants. The routine interface is an ethical system: speed, family income, regulations, and compassion come into direct conflict.

In Papers, Please you sit on the border of Arstotzka and stamp papers. The player compares names, dates, and photographs and at the same time hears that the family needs heat and food, which come out of the pay for a correctly finished shift. The day is short and the line is long. Every careful look raises justice and lowers the number of people processed, and therefore the money.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/434-cover-1.jpg)

Lucas Pope, previously a designer at Naughty Dog, wrote the game, drew it, and in August 2013 published it under the name 3909 almost on his own. In 2014 he received the top prize of the Independent Games Festival for it. The interface is the entire technology: a desk, rules that change by decree, and stamps. There is no world beyond the window that you would enter; the window is the place from which documents are handed to you.

The decision to make bureaucracy an ethical system means compassion has a price in the household budget. The consequence is a game in which fraud, escape, and regulation are not a mood but a column of numbers. The rules grow stricter across the days. The tutorial is the history of the regime itself: yesterday a passport was enough, today you need a vaccination, and tomorrow a reason not to let someone in.

In 2013 this is the opposite of the big worlds. A small pixel booth says more about a state than an open map with flags, and it immediately becomes a teaching text. Some people in the line are a quest, others are noise. The player has to decide whom to remember, because the game will not highlight an important face forever.

Few of the systems have aged. The period pixel style is deliberate, and the interface still serves as an example of how to teach rules without a paragraph of text outside the game. The conflict of speed, family, and regulation holds. That is a structure still cited by games about work and games about morality.

The endings depend on how long you obeyed the regime and when you betrayed it. There is no single correct cleanliness; there is the survival of a household. In class, let students play it for twenty minutes without advice. They will name for themselves the moment they stopped reading the papers honestly.

Papers, Please is a 2013 puzzle simulation video game by Lucas Pope, developed and published independently through his production company 3909 LLC. The player may be challenged with moral dilemmas as the game progresses, such as allowing the supposed spouse of an immigrant through despite lacking complete papers at the risk of accepting a terrorist into the country.

]] Papers, Please was developed by Lucas Pope, a former developer for Naughty Dog on the Uncharted series. Work on Papers, Please began in November 2012, funded by Pope's personal savings from his time at Naughty Dog.

By March 2014, Pope admitted he was "kind of sick to death" of Papers, Please, feeling that he had spent far more time on it than originally intended. Papers, Please received positive reviews on release, receiving "generally favorable reviews" from 40 reviews on Metacritic.

CBC News' Jonathan Ore called Papers, Please a "nerve-racking sleuthing game with relentless pacing and dozens of compelling characters – all from a desk job". Simon Parkin writing for The New Yorker blog declared Papers, Please the top video game of 2013.

You have to decide if you want to create a better world or just look after you and yours." Wired listed Papers, Please as their top game for 2013, recognizing that the game's title alongside the drab presentation captured the ideas of living as a lowly worker in a police state. I was torn between wanting to find out more, and just wanting it all to stop." Papers, Please is considered by several journalists as an example of video games as an art form.

Papers, Please is frequently categorized as an "empathy game", a type of role-playing game that "asks players to inhabit their character's emotional worlds", as described by Patrick Begley of the Sydney Morning Herald, or as described by Pope himself, "other people simulators". Papers, Please won the Seumas McNally Grand Prize, "Excellence in Narrative", and "Excellence in Design" awards at the 2014 Independent Games Festival Awards and was nominated for the Nuovo Award.

Papers, Please also won an Interactive Narrative and Game + Play Peabody Award in 2021. Two Russian filmmakers, Liliya Tkach and Nikita Ordynskiy of Kinodom Productions, developed an 11-minute live-action film based on Papers, Please, entitled Papers, Please: The Short Film, starring Igor Savochkin as the passport inspector.
