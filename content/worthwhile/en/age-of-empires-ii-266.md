Medieval civilizations build an economy and wage historical campaigns. Civilization variation, economy micro, and excellent multiplayer created one of the longest-viable classic RTS games.

Medieval civilizations gather, build an age, and only then fight a war that the campaign hides inside a historical figure. Joan, Saladin, or Genghis Khan are a frame, and the villager is still the main unit. Each civilization has a unique unit and a set of bonuses on top of a shared economy. You learn one game and then a deviation, not three incompatible armies as in StarCraft.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/266-cover-1.jpg)

Ensemble Studios released it in 1999 with Microsoft, with Bruce Shelley among the authors of the rules. It is Age of Empires II, The Age of Kings, and it becomes an RTS that outlives its own client in community and later official revivals. The decision to leave the economy deep and the battle as a consequence had a result for skill. The build order is a macro sport separate from the micro of the fight, and a loss in the seventh minute is often a loss about wood, not about knights.

In 1999, beside Heroes III and Homeworld, strategy shows three different boards: the hex, a volume of space, and this historical RTS. Ensemble wins on the intelligibility of the villager. What has aged is the original pathfinding and part of the farming micro, which is extra labor. What has not aged is the advance of ages and a campaign that lets you play a history lesson instead of only describing it.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/266-screenshot-2.jpg)

In seminar, pause the game in the Dark Age and ask who is ahead. Students will see that the war has not started and the score already has. The monk, the ram, and the castle are three answers to a different distance. The campaign's level design teaches the tool in order, and multiplayer speeds that same order up into a punishment.

Against the first game there is more personality of civilization here and less apology that a bonus is only a number. It is still one trunk of rules, and that is why the game can be played for decades. The editor and the community are part of the longevity. The same lesson as with Heroes: a box that cannot be rewritten dies with the fashion of the year.

The historical campaigns are pedagogy and a simplification. In class, say what the game leaves out, otherwise students will leave with the feeling that the Middle Ages were a build order. The game is a measure of the classic RTS beside StarCraft. One is an asymmetrical mirror sport, the other a shared economy with a national accent.
