Multiple players inhabit a text fantasy world at the same time. The foundational ancestor of MUDs, MMORPGs, and persistent social online spaces.

Several players at once inhabit a text fantasy world: they walk between rooms, talk to each other, collect objects, and kill monsters that are not just their private puzzle. The world keeps running when you leave the terminal. The others stay in it, and your character is part of a shared room, not a saved solo game.

Roy Trubshaw and Richard Bartle began writing it in 1978 at the University of Essex on a PDP-10; Trubshaw laid down the engine, and Bartle turned it into a game you could play with other people. It is not a commercial server in the modern sense, but a university process that people join from terminals, first at Essex and later over the network from elsewhere.

The decision to let more than one player into a single description had the consequence that social play, betrayal, and help are mechanics just as much as a sword. Bartle's later thinking about player types grows from exactly this: someone came to fight, someone to talk, someone to rebuild the world.

In 1978 Zork is still solitude at one account, and Space Invaders is solitude at a machine. MUD1 builds a third option, a shared world without a picture. The name Multi-User Dungeon states the program: take a dungeon and let a queue of people into it, not one hero.

What has aged is the pure text, the slow pace, and rules that leave a newcomer lost without a map and without a guide. What has not aged is the idea that persistence and the presence of other people are the content, not a supplement to the graphics.

A direct line runs from Essex to later MUDs, to graphical online worlds, and to what we will call the MMORPG. In a seminar, stress that the founders of the genre did not have a client to download; they had an account on a machine and an agreement that the world was shared.
