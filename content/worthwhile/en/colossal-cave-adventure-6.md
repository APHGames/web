A vast cave world is explored through a text parser. It formed the basic grammar of the text adventure: rooms, inventory, word commands, and space as a puzzle. The CHM preserves its source code.

The player types verbs and nouns and, through the computer's replies, moves through a cave: taking objects, unlocking places, guessing what is allowed. The world is not a picture but a list of places. The map forms in your head and in notes on paper, because the game will not draw you a floor plan.

Will Crowther, a caver and programmer, wrote the core from the real Colossal Cave in Kentucky; Don Woods expanded it at Stanford in 1976 with fantasy, scoring, and further puzzles. The original game runs on a PDP-10 and understands short commands in English. It is not a free sentence, but a vocabulary you have to guess.

Crowther's decision to describe real places, and Woods's decision to add magic, had the consequence that the adventure is both a guide to a space and a treasure hunt. An inventory and a limited carrying capacity force the player to come back. The cave is not a corridor but a network you solve in pieces.

In 1976 Spacewar! is still running in laboratories beside it, and Zork is only being prepared at the universities. Colossal Cave gives the text game its basic grammar: room, exit, object, word. It spreads across networks between computers, not in a box. Commercial microcomputer versions come only after it.

What has aged is the two-word parser and the cruelty of dead ends where you cannot tell whether the program lacks the word or you have missed the puzzle. What has not aged is the idea of space as a construction: what you can carry, what you say, and where you are allowed to return.

Without this game, Adventureland, Zork, and the later graphic adventures have no shared language to start from. The Computer History Museum preserves its code; for a seminar this is a rare case where a founding game can be read as a text, not only as a memory.

Colossal Cave Adventure (also known as Adventure or ADVENT) is a text-based adventure game, released in 1976 by Will Crowther for the PDP-10 mainframe computer. In the game, the player explores a cave system rumored to be filled with treasure and gold.

It is the first well-known example of interactive fiction, as well as the first well-known adventure game, for which it was also the namesake. The original game, written in 1975 and 1976, was based on Crowther's maps and experiences caving in Mammoth Cave in Kentucky, the longest cave system in the world; further, it was intended, in part, to be accessible to non-technical players, such as his two daughters.

Colossal Cave Adventure was one of the first teletype games and was massively popular in the computer community of the late 1970s, with numerous ports and modified versions being created based on Woods's source code. It directly inspired the creation of numerous games, including Zork (1977), Adventureland (1978), Mystery House (1980), Rogue (1980), and Adventure (1980), which went on to be the foundations of the interactive fiction, adventure, roguelike, and action-adventure genres.

The player must explore the cave system and solve puzzles by using items that they find to obtain the treasures and leave the cave. The player types in one- or two-word commands to move their character through the cave system, interact with objects in the cave, pick up items to put into their inventory, and perform other actions.

The program acts as a narrator, describing to the player their location in the cave and the results of certain actions.
