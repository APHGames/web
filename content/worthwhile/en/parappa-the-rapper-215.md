PaRappa repeats the rhythmic phrases of the teachers in order to gain self-confidence. One of the games that established the rhythm game as a separate console genre.

PaRappa repeats a teacher's rap and hits the rhythm; the point is not to compose your own song, the point is to last through the lesson and not lose your confidence. The grade is not only good and bad. If you play too exactly to the model, the game lets you into a freer mode, so obedience and courage are two different scores.

Masaya Matsuura and the studio NanaOn-Sha released it on the PlayStation in 1996, with the artist Rodney Greenblat. Matsuura comes from the pop group Psy·S, and the game is his musical instrument, not a licensed song pack. Paper characters stand in a simple 3D space. The technique is poor on purpose, so that the drawing and the beat carry the whole screen without a fight over polygons.

The decision to make the game a test in front of a teacher had a consequence for the genre. A rhythm game on a console gains the structure of a lesson, an audience, and a failure that is personal, not only a missed note. In 1996 the worldwide wave of Dance Dance Revolution is not running yet. PaRappa shows that music can be the core of a console game before it becomes an arcade sport of the feet.

The control is a dance only in the fingers. Even so the body is in the game, because failure is read from PaRappa's face and from the teacher's voice, not from a number in the corner. What has aged is the strictness of the window, in which the player often does not know why they just fell from Cool back down. The rhythm interface here does not yet have the later pedagogy of a note highway.

The courage to entrust the game to one artist and one musician has not aged. An authorial look is the design, not a skin. In seminar, play it with no hint about the timing. Students will feel on themselves how little it takes to frustrate when the rule can be heard but cannot be seen.

Greenblat's drawing holds the game together even at the moment the rhythm fails. The world is not a reward for the score; it is the reason to endure the score. For the history of rhythm games it is the start of the console branch. The arcade branch with a dance platform will arrive immediately after and will want the whole body.

PaRappa the Rapper was acclaimed by critics, who praised its music, story, animation, and gameplay, though its short length was criticized. It spawned two follow-up titles; a guitar-based spin-off titled Um Jammer Lammy, released in 1999 for the PlayStation, and a direct sequel, PaRappa the Rapper 2, released for the PlayStation 2 in 2001.

PaRappa the Rapper is a rhythm game in which the main character, PaRappa, must make his way through each of the game's six stages by rapping. The player must then make PaRappa rap in response to the teacher by pressing the buttons with the correct timing to match the teacher's line.

The player takes on the role of PaRappa, a paper-thin rapping dog, determined to procure the heart of a flower-like girl he has a crush on named Sunny Funny. In order to win Sunny, PaRappa learns to do karate at a dojo, and earns a driver's education course to get a license.

When Sunny's birthday comes up, PaRappa has to get her a cake but ends up ruining it after an encounter with Joe Chin. Then one night, PaRappa is invited to Club Fun, and asks Sunny to go with him, to which she agrees.

PaRappa then raps on stage with everyone, rapping solo at the end of the song showing that he's interested in Sunny. The game's title is a wordplay referencing the flat characters; "PaRappa" is a variant of the Japanese word for "paper thin".

Many staff members at Sony felt that PaRappa did not qualify as a game and Matsuura himself recalls initially being unsure as well. The game received near-universal acclaim from critics, who hailed PaRappa the Rapper as a unique game with irresistibly catchy songs.

Electronic Gaming Monthly awarded it "Most Original Game of the Year" and "Best Music", and the character PaRappa "Best Mascot" (beating out Lara Croft and Crash Bandicoot, both of whom had critically acclaimed games released that year), at their 1997 Editors' Choice Awards. A bonus mode was included in which the entire game could be replayed with PaRappa as the protagonist, complete with his own versions of the game's stages.

A direct sequel, PaRappa the Rapper 2, was released for the PlayStation 2 in 2001 in Japan and 2002 in North America and Europe. Several media adaptations outside of the video game industry were also developed, including an eponymous anime television series which aired in Japan between April 2001 and January 2002, which served as a tie-in to PaRappa the Rapper 2.

PaRappa the Rapper was released for PlayStation Portable in Japan in December 2006 and in North America and Europe in July 2007 for the game's tenth anniversary. PaRappa the Rapper was featured as a challenge on the video game-based show GamesMaster in 1997.
