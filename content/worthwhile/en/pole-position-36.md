A Formula car races along a pseudo-3D track. Perspective scrolling and a qualifying structure became the model of an entire generation of racing games.

The player must first qualify on the track and only then races a formula car against the other cars, in a view that converges toward the horizon. The road is not top-down as in Gran Trak. It is a pseudo-three-dimensional corridor: the corner bends, the billboards grow, and the car ahead of you is a sprite that gets larger.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/036-cover-1.jpg)

Namco released it in 1982, and Atari licensed it in America. The track refers to Fuji Speedway; the game sells a specific race, not an abstract oval. The board can scale objects by distance. Depth here is not a grid as in Battlezone, but a series of sprites and strips of road that behave like perspective.

The decision to insert a qualifying lap had the consequence that the game has the structure of a race weekend, not just an endless coin. First you earn the place, then you defend it. Lap time and race position are two different tensions. You can drive cleanly and still lose if you do not read the traffic.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/036-screenshot-2.jpg)

In 1982 arcade racing is still often a top-down view or a simple scroll. Pole Position makes perspective the norm that a whole decade will copy. The sit-down cabinet and the wheel continue the language of Gran Trak, but the picture now promises that you are driving forward, not around a plan.

What has aged is the narrow track without real tire physics, and opponents who are obstacles more than drivers with a plan of their own. What has not aged is the contract of the race: qualification, a starting grid, a horizon, and a punishment for driving faster than you can read the corner.

Out Run, four years later, takes this horizon and exchanges racing for position for a drive against the clock with a fork in the road. In a seminar, freeze the game in a corner and ask where the apex is. Even if the game does not calculate it, the students will see that perspective is already asking the question.

It was developed as a successor of Namco's earlier arcade racing electro-mechanical games, like F-1 (1976), whose designer Sho Osugi worked on Pole Position. Pole Position spawned ports, sequels, and a Saturday morning cartoon, although the cartoon has little in common with the game.

Pole Position is regarded as one of the most influential video games of all time, and is considered to be the most influential racing game, as well as one of the most important titles from the golden age of arcade video games. A sequel, Pole Position II, was released in 1983 with four tracks instead of one.

A successful qualification awards bonus points and sets the player's starting position among seven computer-controlled cars, based on the lap time. Pole Position was the first racing video game to feature a track based on a real racing circuit.

While earlier three-dimensional arcade driving games emphasized staying on the road while avoiding crashes, Pole Position gives a higher reward for passing rival cars and finishing among the leaders. In Japan, Game Machine magazine listed Pole Position as the highest-grossing arcade game of 1982, and later listed it as the second top-grossing upright arcade unit of May 1983, before it returned to being the top-grossing game of October 1983.

Upon its North American debut at AMOA, Pole Position was reviewed by Video Games magazine, which listed it among the show's top ten games. They compared it favorably with Sega's Turbo (1981), referring to Pole Position as "Turbo Deluxe" in "a speedway, not a cross-country race".

They called Pole Position the "ultimate test of driving skill" for racing players. Pole Position is regarded as one of the most influential video games of all time, and was the most successful racing game of the golden age of arcade video games.

In 2015, Pole Position topped IGN s list of The Top 10 Most Influential Racing Games Ever. Parker Brothers also published a board game based on Pole Position in 1983.
