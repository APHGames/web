A paddle destroys blocks and collects modifying power-ups. It revived Breakout through a multitude of ball states, lasers, paddle expansions, and structured levels.

The Vaus paddle returns the ball into a field of blocks and along the way catches capsules that split the ball, widen the paddle, or turn it into a laser. Compared with pure Breakout there is no longer one physics for the whole game. Each capsule rewrites, for a second, which sport you are playing.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/077-poster-1.jpg)

Taito released the machine in 1986 as a conscious heir of Breakout. The field is now a designed level, not just a wall, and at the end a boss waits that turns the puzzle into a duel. The picture is a fixed arena with a clear reading: block, gap, capsule falling downward. The player has to decide whether to catch the capsule or to let it fall, because a bad power can kill you too.

The decision to pour ball-states into Breakout had the consequence that mastery is not one angle. It is the willingness to throw away a habit the moment the ball splits into three. Some levels are drawings. The field is not only a density of bricks, it is a picture that is being broken, and the puzzle thereby becomes a poster as well.

![Artwork](/img/worthwhile-games/media/077-artwork-2.jpg)

In 1986 it is ten years since Breakout, and the arcade already knows bosses, power-ups, and structure. Arkanoid is a lesson in how to take a perfect simple rule and deliberately complicate it without letting it fall apart. The ending with the boss Doh gives the abstraction a villain. The story is thin and is not meant to be more than a pretext for the next field.

What has aged is the dependence on a specific capsule in a specific second, and levels that are a test of endurance, not of a new idea. What has not aged is the paddle as a readable tool and the tension of one fallen ability changing the whole screen.

From the Vaus comes a whole school of blocks that will never again be only a brick and an angle. In a seminar, play a minute without capsules, if the setup allows it, and a minute with them. The students should say which game is Breakout's heir and which is its argument.

Arkanoid was ported to many home systems, including the Commodore 64, Nintendo Entertainment System, and ZX Spectrum. Created by Taito designers Akira Fujita and Hiroshi Tsujino, Arkanoid was part of a contest within Taito, where two teams of designers had to implement a block breaker game and determine which was superior to the other.

Early location tests for Arkanoid surpassed Taito's initial expectations. Arkanoid spawned a long series of sequels and updates over the course of two decades.

Its plot involves the starship Arkanoid being attacked by a mysterious entity from space named DOH. If the player succeeds in defeating "DOH", the game rewards them by showing the ending, in which time starts to flow backwards, and Vaus escapes the distorted space just in time to return to the Arkanoid, which has also reversed back to perfect condition.

Arkanoid was designed by Akira Fujita and Hiroshi "ONIJUST" Tsujino, both of whom were members of Taito's Yokohama Research Institute. Arkanoid was Japan's highest-grossing table arcade game during the second half of 1986, and the overall sixth highest-grossing table arcade game of 1986.

Computer Gaming World stated in 1988 that Arkanoid on the Amiga was "a perfect version of the arcade game ... Along with Breakout, the magazine noted Arkanoid also has elements of Pong and Space Invaders as well as Pac-Man in its use of power-ups.

Arkanoid and its home releases received several awards, including the "Silver Award" from the Gamest Awards, "Games of the Year" from Compute! Tournament Arkanoid was released in 1987 exclusively in the United States by Romstar.

Developed by Taito America rather than Taito Japan, it has the same gameplay as Arkanoid, but adds new levels. Arkanoid: Doh It Again and Arkanoid Returns were published in 1997, followed by Arkanoid DS in 2007.

The first Touhou Project game, Highly Responsive to Prayers, which was originally released in 1997, was heavily influenced by Arkanoid gameplay. A modernized version of the game, titled Arkanoid: Eternal Battle, was developed by Pastagames and published by Microids.
