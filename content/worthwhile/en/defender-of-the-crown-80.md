English nobles conquer territory through a map and cinematic minigames. The production quality of the Amiga showed it was possible to present strategy as an "interactive film" with high visual appeal.

A Saxon noble on a map of England gathers territory, rides in tournaments, raids castles, and in between watches short scenes that want you to feel a film, not a table. The duel, the ride, and the siege are minigames. The grand strategy is thin; what is thick is the spectacle meant to hold you between turns.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/080-screenshot-1.jpg)

Cinemaware published it in 1986 on the Amiga. Jim Sachs's illustrations are the reason people went to look at the game in a shop: a computer that can do more colors and larger sprites than a home eight-bit here receives its poster. The Amiga allows a picture that, next to it on the C64, feels like another decade. Cinemaware builds a whole company on that: strategy as an interactive film, even if the word interactive here mainly means a choice on the map and then a viewing.

The decision to put politics into a set of small scenes had the consequence that the player forgives a simple economy if the tournament looks expensive. The form sells a content that would, on its own, be short. Robin Hood, the Normans, and the Saxons are scenery the game does not excavate. History is a mood, not a model, and a lecture ought to distinguish that from Balance of Power.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/080-screenshot-2.png)

In 1986 the Amiga is new and it needs a reason why it is not just a more expensive game machine. Defender of the Crown is that reason in the catalogue: you look, and you understand what you are paying for. The game repeats easily, and an experienced player can break it. Its fame is not in the depth of the system, but in the home screen beginning to compete with an illustration.

What has aged is exactly that imbalance: the minigames are a reflex test stuck onto a map that no longer has anything to say. What has not aged is the appetite for strategy to have a face, music, and a knight, even if the table underneath is thin.

From Cinemaware comes a whole branch of games that sell a historical spectacle before they sell a rule. In a seminar, show the picture first and only then a turn. The order in which the students understand the game is the order in which the company sold it.

Defender of the Crown is a strategy video game designed by Kellyn Beeck. A sequel, Defender of the Crown II, was released in 1993 for the CDTV and Amiga CD32.

Two remakes have been released: Robin Hood: Defender of the Crown in 2003 and Defender of the Crown: Heroes Live Forever in 2007. The cover art of Defender of the Crown was put together by Peter Green Design and painted by Ezra Tucker.

Defender of the Crown for MS-DOS and NES have poor graphic and audio quality, but have more in-depth strategic elements compared to the original. The first public demonstration of Defender of the Crown occurred at the Los Angeles Commodore Show in September 1986, before its November release, and attracted a huge crowd.

That year the magazine gave Defender of the Crown a special award for "Artistic Achievement in a Computer Game", but in 1990 and 1993, surveys of wargames in the magazine gave the game two-plus stars out of five.

Defender of the Crown II was published by Commodore International in 1993 for the CDTV and Amiga CD32. In 2000, however, Lars Fuhrken-Batista and Sean Vesce bought Cinemaware's name and assets, and founded Cinemaware Inc., naming a remake of Defender of the Crown for modern PCs as one of the reformed company's first projects.

The new version, titled Robin Hood: Defender of the Crown was released in 2003 for the PlayStation 2 (September 30), Xbox (October 6), and Windows (October 15). The new company also created "Digitally Remastered Versions" of classic Cinemaware games, including Defender of the Crown.

In February 2007, a second remake to the game called Defender of the Crown: Heroes Live Forever was released by eGames, who had acquired Cinemaware in 2005. During the second half of the 1980s, some games directly inspired by Defender of the Crown were released.
