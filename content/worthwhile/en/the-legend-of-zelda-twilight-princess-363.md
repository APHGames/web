Link alternates between human and wolf form while fighting the Twilight Realm. It carried traditional Zelda dungeon design to extraordinary spatial complexity and at the same time brought the series to the Wii.

In Twilight Princess, Link is both a human and a wolf, and Midna, the small guide from the twilight, rewrites where each form lets you go. The wolf smells trails and the human solves a temple the wolf only ran through. The temples are spatially more complex than in many games of the series. Snowpeak, the Arbiter's Grounds, or the City in the Sky are lessons in how large one Zelda building can be before it falls apart into a map.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/363-cover-1.jpg)

Eiji Aonuma directed it at Nintendo EAD, and in 2006 it came out on the Wii and on the GameCube. The Wii version mirrors the world for the right hand with the controller, and thereby also changes the orientation of places the GameCube left in the original direction. The consequence of the sword gesture on the Wii is that a cut is a swing, not a precise vector. The game reads intention much as Wii Sports does, and therefore the combat is not one-to-one fencing, even if it looks like it.

In 2006, when the industry goes toward cover and toward an adult palette, Nintendo is still building a monumental temple. It is proof that the old structure of item and lock can carry a new console. The opening hours, before the first large temple arrives, are long and have aged into impatience. The temples themselves have not aged as spatial problems.

The more realistic drawing has aged faster than The Wind Waker's cel shading. The pair of games on one list is a class about fidelity to the face aging faster than a conscious line. The wolf's collection of tears and some interludes are padding between temples. The consequence of the two forms is richness and also an obligation that the wolf not be merely a faster walk.

For students of Zelda structure it is the peak of dungeon complexity before the series, in Breath of the Wild, gives up the dungeon as the main form. Here the temple is still a novel. Ganondorf and Zant hold two layers of villain. One is political in the twilight, the other is the old contract of the series, and the game needs both so the ending has weight.

The horse Epona and the fields between temples recall Ocarina of Time deliberately. The return is not laziness; it is reassurance that the new console did not cancel Hyrule. When you play it today, play the GameCube version if you can, for the direction of the world, and the Wii version for the gesture. They are two readings of the same corridors.
