Alyx Vance fights the Combine before the events of Half-Life 2. Hands-on manipulation, spatial sound, and physical interactions showed how high-budget FPS design can be rebuilt around VR instead of merely adapted.

Alyx Vance in Half-Life: Alyx reaches for magazines, pulls arrows out of grates, and throws bottles at headcrabs, because the hands are the interface. The player moves around a room in VR, and reloading is not an animation on a button; it is work for the fingers. The Combine, headcrabs, and darkness are still Half-Life, but distance from an enemy is measured by the body, not by a sight on a flat screen. The game offers smooth locomotion and teleport so that different stomachs can pass the same chapter.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/482-cover-1.jpg)

Valve released the game in March 2020 on Source 2 as a full VR title, not as a transcription of Half-Life 2. It comes out at the start of the pandemic, so it is played at home and only where someone has a headset, which is both a historical condition and a barrier. The Gravity Gloves pull objects from a distance and thereby solve the fact that a real room is smaller than a game warehouse. The consequence is that the design can build on physics and still not assume a warehouse the size of a hall.

The decision to rebuild an FPS around the hands, not only to glue a camera to the head, means different puzzles, different weapons, and a different pace. The consequence is a measure for VR by which you recognize a mere adaptation of a flat shooter. The story sits between the first and second Half-Life, and the ending reaches forward into the series. It is not a safe side story that would not be allowed to touch the continuation.

In 2020 this is the most expensive argument that virtual reality can carry a big single-player campaign. Beside social VR and short attractions it is a long, authored game with quiet corridors as well as a firefight. Russell on the radio and Alyx's work in the quarantine of City 17 keep the tone of the series: a professional who comments on what she is holding. The studio knows that a voice in the ear matters more in VR than a cutscene.

The hardware threshold has aged. The game still requires equipment most Half-Life players do not have, so its influence on design is wider than its audience. Physical interaction and spatial sound hold. It is still the reference campaign for what a magazine, darkness, and an object you really have to set down should look like in VR.

Optional locomotion is accessibility that does not change the canon of the levels. Two bodies pass the same rooms at a different cost in nausea. In class, take it as a watershed of input: as long as a game only moves aiming into the goggles, it is a port; Alyx changes the verb of the hand and therefore changes the level design.
