An adventurer carries out Lord British's tasks in wireframe 3D dungeons. An important precursor of Ultima, joining an overmap, statistics, and first-person dungeon exploration.

The player carries out Lord British's tasks: walking a map, descending into wireframe dungeons in first person, and returning with treasure and experience. Combat is a dice roll and a statistic, not fencing in real time. The tension is whether your number survives the next floor.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/015-screenshot-1.jpg)

Richard Garriott wrote it in 1979 on the Apple II in BASIC and at first sold it himself, before California Pacific took it on as publisher. The dungeons are a wireframe 3D view of the kind the Apple II can bear: lines instead of walls, a slow redraw, and still the feeling that you are standing in a corridor.

The decision to join a top-down map to an eye-level dungeon had the consequence that one game contains two ways of being in the world, and the player switches between them. Lord British is not a licensed character but Garriott's own mask from earlier paper games and from family mythology. The author is inside the rules.

In 1979 the home computer is still a machine for BASIC, and the arcade belongs to Space Invaders. Akalabeth takes the dungeon from a paper game and asks whether a microcomputer can carry it. It is short and raw beside the later Ultima, but that rawness is exactly what shows the skeleton: statistic, map, descent, reward.

What has aged is the slow wireframe drawing and text that assumes the player will endure an interface from a year when programs were written at school. What has not aged is the appetite for receiving a task from a character who owns the world, and then walking that world in first person.

Ultima I is a direct continuation of this skeleton, now with a larger world and the ambition of a series. In a seminar, play it as the author's first public proof, not as an Apple II curiosity: Garriott discovers here that a personal campaign can be a product.

Akalabeth: World of Doom ( ) is a role-playing video game created in 1979 for the Apple II by Richard Garriott, and published by California Pacific Computer Company in 1980. In creating Akalabeth, Garriott was primarily inspired by Dungeons & Dragons, for which he held weekly sessions in his parents' house while in high school, Akalabeth was written in Applesoft BASIC, allowing users to modify the source code.

Akalabeth is included in the 1998 Ultima Collection where it officially picked up the nickname Ultima 0. Scorpia of Computer Gaming World, a fan of Ultima, agreed in 1991 and 1993: "Bluntly, it wasn't all that terrific." She did, however, note that the game was the first to offer 3-D perspective dungeon graphics.

Copies of the original Akalabeth are much rarer than those of other games that sold fewer than 30,000 copies.
