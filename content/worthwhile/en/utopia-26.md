Two rulers build island economies and respond to disasters. One of the early ancestors of real-time strategy and management simulation on consoles.

Two players rule neighboring islands in real time: they build, harvest, fish, and react to a storm, hunger, or revolt before the opponent does. This is not about units you send into an attack as in later strategy. It is about where you put money and attention before the clock strikes.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/026-cover-1.jpg)

Don Daglow designed it for Mattel, and it was released on the Intellivision in 1981. The console controller, with its numeric keypad, is not an obstacle here but the interface of government. The picture is the coarse raster of a home console. A field, a ship, and a fort are signs whose meaning must be carried by the rule, not by the illustration.

The decision to let both islands run at once had the consequence that waiting is a loss. Administration is a race against time, not correspondence chess. Sabotage and a shared disaster force the player to look at the opponent's island. The economy is a relationship, not a table for one.

In 1981 strategy is still mainly board games, and the main computer models are the adventure and the arcade. Utopia tests whether a television can carry the administration of territory. This is not a god smoothing the terrain as in the later Populous. It is a governor with a budget and a catastrophe that will not wait for a turn.

What has aged is the abstract square and a control scheme that, without a manual, will not say what each number on the disc does. What has not aged is the pressure of two economies side by side and the feeling that nature is a third player.

A long line runs from this pair of islands to city builders and to strategies where the economy is faster than the army. In a seminar, play it with two people. A solo demonstration kills the very thing it was made for on the Intellivision.

Ars Technica cites Utopia as being the "birth of a genre", that "prior to the mid-1990s strategy games were turn based", the "idea of adding a real-time element to force players into instant, impulsive decisions were virtually unheard of." Ars Technica states that it is "arguably the earliest ancestor of the real-time strategy genre".
