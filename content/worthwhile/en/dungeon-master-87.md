A party moves through a physical dungeon in real time. Real-time combat, direct manipulation, and spatial sound signals radically increased the immersion of the dungeon crawler.

A party of four champions crawls a dungeon in real time, picks objects straight off the floor with the cursor, and fights by standing in the right direction, not by choosing an attack from a long menu. The torch burns down and the food runs out even when you are not solving a puzzle. Time is an enemy even in an empty corridor.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/087-cover-1.jpg)

FTL Games published it in 1987 on the Atari ST, where the mouse and the screen can finally carry a dungeon as a physical space, not as a text description step by step. The sound gives away a monster behind the door before you see it. The information is not in a line of the journal, it is in the ear, and so it pays to stop and listen.

The decision to let time run even in combat had the consequence that hesitation is a hit you take. Wizardry gave you calm for a turn; Dungeon Master gives you a pulse. The hand on the screen is the interface. You pick up an object, throw it, put it in a mouth. Direct manipulation replaces the verb you would otherwise have had to guess.

In 1987 Dungeon Master is an event on the Atari ST and a reason why players take that machine seriously. Beside it on the PC, turn-based parties still rule, and they suddenly look like a spreadsheet. The four characters do not have names from a novel, but roles in a formation. Whoever stands in front takes the blow, and that is the whole tactic in one sentence.

What has aged is the slow first hour and puzzles that, without patience for the switches on the wall, feel like a hidden hint. What has not aged is the sound behind the door and the fact that a dungeon can be a body in space, not a list of rooms.

From this mouse comes a whole wave of real-time crawlers, including those that carry the system onto the PC and into licensed worlds. In a seminar, turn the lights down and let the students walk by sound. If they slow down before a door, FTL has taught more than the manual.

The game was an influential early example of the first-person party-based dungeon crawler genre, with later examples including Eye of the Beholder (1991). In contrast to the traditional turn-based approach that was, in 1987, most common, Dungeon Master added real-time combat elements (akin to Active Time Battle).

Dungeon Master was, however, responsible for popularizing these elements. Other features of Dungeon Master included allowing players to directly manipulate objects and the environment by clicking the mouse in the enlarged first-person view.

As Theron, the player cannot progress past the first section of the game until they have selected at least one and up to four champions from a small dungeon containing 24 mirrors, each containing a frozen champion. Many champions have been sent into the dungeon with the quest to recover Librasulus' (the Grey Lord) firestaff.

The player is Theron, the apprentice of the Grey Lord, that goes into the dungeon with the task to resurrect or reincarnate four champions, and guide them through the dungeon, to find the firestaff and defeat Lord Chaos. But there is also an alternative ending if the player finds the firestaff and then leaves the dungeon without destroying Lord Chaos.

Originally, Dungeon Master was started with the name Crystal Dragon coded in Pascal, and targeted the Apple II platform. According to "The Definitive CDTV Retrospective: Part II" by Peter Olafson, Dungeon Master was ported to the Amiga CDTV but this version was never completed because FTL could not obtain reliable information from Commodore about saving games to memory cards.

There exists a prototype for the Atari Lynx under the name Dungeon Slayers. The painting itself is 25 to 30 inches high and does not contain the word "Master".

Darrow's painting portrays a scene from the prologue in the manual for Dungeon Master. A soundtrack album, titled Dungeon Master: The Album, was released later.

Dungeon Master debuted on December 15, 1987 on the Atari ST, and by early 1988 was a strong seller, becoming the best-selling game for the computer of all time; Bell estimated that at one point more than half of all Atari ST owners had purchased the game. Because of FTL's sophisticated copy protection, many who otherwise pirated their software had to purchase Dungeon Master to play the game.

It was the first prominent Amiga game to require more than the standard amount of memory, likely causing many to purchase additional RAM; at least one manufacturer of Amiga memory bundled Dungeon Master with its memory-expansion kit. The reviewer said Dungeon Master is an example of a title which "changes the way we think about games" and a "must for all roleplayers".

The presentation - an interesting and evocative novella neither too involved to prove turbid not too short to be unhelpful - is superb." The reviewer summarised: "Dungeon Master is a role-player's dream, but capable of providing a good deal of enjoyment for any ST owner." STart told readers to "be prepared to shed every preconception you ever had about computer games. Noting the strong sales, the reviewer called it "a true video game phenomenon" and reported that "not talking to my boyfriend for a week because he lost our master spell list was certainly not an overreaction".

Jerry Pournelle named Dungeon Master his game of the month "and maybe of the year" in July 1988: "Everything you heard about this is true; the graphics are incredible". Jim Trunzo reviewed Dungeon Master in White Wolf #15 (April/May 1989), rating it a 4 out of 5 and stated that "This product is highly recommended to anyone interested in fantasy gaming.

It summarized: "you'll be playing for months" and said Dungeon Master was "The best game we've ever seen". Also reviewing the Amiga version, Graham Kinsey of Amazing Computing wrote that Dungeon Master "completely blows away any other RPG on the Amiga market today, and may do for some time".

Dave Eriksson of Amiga Computing praised the "brilliant" graphics, sound effects and replay-value and said "Dungeon Master is the most stunning role-playing game I have seen on the Amiga". The reviewer called the game "amazing" and recommended: "If you never buy another game, by [sic] this one." Andy Smith of Advanced Computing Entertainment several months after its release called Dungeon Master "one of the all time classics" and said "What makes Dungeon Master really special (apart from the marvellous 3D graphics and eerie sound effects) are the puzzles".

In 1997, ten years after release, Dungeon Master got again a 5 out of 5 stars score in a review. Dungeon Master received the Special Award for Artistic Achievement from Computer Gaming World in 1988.

While Dungeon Master itself was inspired by early Ultima games, it amazed Ultima developer Origin Systems's employees; Origin founder Richard Garriott said that he was "ecstatic" at discovering the "neat new things I could do" in the game. Game journalist Niko Nirvi wrote that no 3D role-playing title before Ultima Underworld (1992) could challenge Dungeon Master as a game.

In 1989, FTL Games released a Dungeon Master sequel, Chaos Strikes Back. To date, Dungeon Master retains a small but faithful following online, with several fan-made ports and remakes available or in development.

The villagers in the sandbox game Minecraft were inspired by the shopkeepers from Dungeon Master 2.
