A ship uses six combinations of three weapons against choreographed waves. The weapons are available from the start, and scoring chains from enemy colors turn the shooter into a long-term puzzle discipline.

From the first second the ship has six weapons composed of three buttons, and no power-up on the ground will extend the arsenal. All that changes is which combination you release into which wave. The score hangs on a chain of colors. Killing whatever is nearest is a worse strategy than reading the choreography and holding one color for as long as the chain holds.

![Poster](/img/worthwhile-games/media/253-poster-1.jpg)

Hiroshi Iuchi designed it at Treasure, and Sega put it into arcades and onto the Saturn in 1998. The Saturn version stayed Japanese, and the reputation traveled west before a regular official edition did. It is a classic vertical shooter with an authored wave, not procedural chaos. Treasure's technique is in the readability of the pattern: a shot is geometry, and you have a tool that fits it if you know it.

The decision not to give weapons as rewards from enemies had a consequence for the long-term game. Mastery is not a collection of equipment; it is the memory of a level, and that is why the game is practiced as a discipline, not as a trip. In 1998, when consoles are selling 3D and story, Radiant Silvergun is almost a scandal of another kind. It wants you to die and come back smarter, and by a campaign it means the order of the waves.

What has aged is the arcade economy of continues if you play it as a home story. What has not aged is the system of six weapons and the color chain as a reason why surviving is not enough. In seminar, play one wave twice, the second time with a ban on switching weapons. Students will see that the choice of tool is a puzzle and not an effect.

Iuchi's team builds the shooter as a musical score. Level design is temporal and colored, and that is why it can be taken apart like music, not only like difficulty. Against Panzer Dragoon Zwei there is less world here and more score. Both Japanese games of the period teach that action can be a craft of repetition.

Later ports pulled the game out of a rare disc into an ordinary archive. Historically it is fair to say that the canon long depended on who had a Saturn in Japan. The game does not need lore in order to be serious. A rule that refuses to simplify after the first hour is what binds you.

Treasure was primarily known for developing action and platform games for home consoles before Radiant Silvergun. Radiant Silvergun was developed by ten people, with Gonzo outsourced for animated cutscenes.

Despite its region exclusivity, Radiant Silvergun was imported by Western critics and received critical acclaim. IGN called Radiant Silvergun a "milestone in shooter design" and in 2008 listed it as their number one classic shoot 'em up.

Following Ikaruga 's rerelease, fans began asking Treasure why Radiant Silvergun had not been rereleased as well, so Treasure began work on a port. Sanjo stated that bringing Radiant Silvergun to the Xbox 360 was more difficult than anticipated.
