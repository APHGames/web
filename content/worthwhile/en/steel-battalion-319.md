A pilot controls a heavy mech through a specialized giant controller. One of the most radical examples of software designed around a unique physical interface.

In Steel Battalion you climb into a heavy mech, and every launch begins with a row of switches, pedals, and levers. The game refuses to start until you start the machine as a machine. The controller has two sticks, pedals, dozens of buttons, and a covered eject button. The software is designed around that plastic, not the other way around.

Capcom published it in 2002 on the Xbox. Atsushi Inaba produced it, Hifumi Kono directed it, and Nude Maker developed it together with Capcom Production Studio 4. When the machine is falling and you fail to eject in time, the game erases the saved position. The punishment is not a game-over screen but the loss of a pilot you cannot bring back.

The consequence is that the interface is the fiction. You cannot play Steel Battalion as ordinary action on a gamepad, because half the rules are in which lever you must physically flip. In 2002 it is an extreme example between dance mats and the later music controllers. The console here is not only a screen but a reason to build a new piece of furniture.

Without the original controller the game is almost unplayable today. That dependence is a historical lesson, not merely a collector's obstacle. The idea that a pilot's body has a procedure has not aged. What aged is access to hardware that is not manufactured with every next console.

The online follow-up Line of Contact later carried the same cockpit into networked combat. The first game is still the one that forces you to survive the startup itself. For control students it is an extreme on which the price of embodiment is visible. The more buttons belong to the fiction, the less an ordinary translation onto another pad can save the game.

The difficulty of the procedures is not an ornament of the manual. It is the tempo by which action becomes a shift. When you talk about it without the controller in your hand, you are talking about half the work. The other half is the plastic the player had to buy together with the software.

To control the tank and play, the game requires the use of a large controller made specially for Steel Battalion. Steel Battalion was developed by Nude Maker (former Human Entertainment developers) in collaboration with Capcom Production Studio 4.

Steel Battalion was released for the Xbox in Japan on September 12, 2002. Steel Battalion was the fifth best-selling game during its week of release in Japan at about 15,092 copies.

The third installment called Steel Battalion: Heavy Armor was released in June 2012.
