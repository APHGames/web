Ezio Auditore becomes an assassin in Renaissance Italy. Urban parkour, an economy, and the long-term development of a charismatic protagonist dramatically repaired the shortcomings of the first game.

You do not move through Florence like a museum visitor: Ezio runs along ledges, disappears into the crowd, and between contracts returns to Monteriggioni, which turns from a ruined villa into a Brotherhood seat. The player's day is made of rooftop chases, short fights, town management, and missions in which the social situation itself is the weapon, such as the carnival or the space of a basilica.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/401-cover-1.jpg)

Ubisoft Montreal, under director Patrice Désilets, made the second game as a repair of the first Assassin's Creed from 2007, whose repeated informant rescues and eavesdropping players saw through quickly. The Anvil engine in 2009 can hold a dense street and the layers of roofs above it, but climbing still runs along prepared holds, so the vertical space is an authored track, not free physics.

The decision to let Ezio pass through several years of life and to give him a family, a craft, and a town that grows between missions changed the series from a parade of historical backdrops into the biography of one character. The villa economy follows directly: income from workshops and shops is a reason to return to Monteriggioni, and the Brotherhood expands as a consequence of play.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/401-screenshot-2.jpg)

At the end of 2009, beside Left 4 Dead 2 and Dragon Age: Origins, the game stands as proof that big action in an open city can have the pace of growing up, not only a map with icons. The Renaissance is read here through shortcuts across the roofs. Family wars, the Venetian carnival, and political murders are scenes that parkour moves through.

What has aged is above all close combat and the climbing itself, which later entries smoothed over, and also the sci-fi frame of the Animus, which already functioned as a parenthesis around the Italian story. The arc of the protagonist holds. There are still few big games in which you feel the childhood, the loss, and the professional confidence of the same person.

The modern Animus passages are shorter than in the first game, so the future frames the Renaissance and does not shout over it every few minutes. When you teach the series, take this entry as the moment when repeatable parkour gained an economic and biographical reason.
