A pilot faces a devastating Shivan invasion in large fleet battles. The scale of capital ships, dynamic orders, and strong moddability represent the peak of the classic PC space combat sim.

In a fighter cockpit you fly beside capital ships that are the size of a level, and orders to the wing change a battle that is not a film on rails. The mission is a fleet action. The dogfight is a skill, and reading what a cruiser is doing is a second skill, without which you are only a tourist at the hull.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/280-screenshot-1.jpg)

Volition, the studio of people from Descent, led by Mike Kulas and Matt Toschlog, released it in 1999 with Interplay. It is a peak of the classic PC space-combat simulation of that decade, and the campaign about the Shivans is a pretext for scale. The decision to put the player beside the ships, not inside them as an admiral, had a consequence for the emotion. Homeworld in the same year gives an overview, FreeSpace 2 gives the fear of size, because you are small and you still have the order.

A source release later extended the game's life through community updates. Moddability here is not an appendix; it is the reason a simulation from 1999 can still be shown today without an apology for the textures. In 1999 space action on PC is still a genre with an audience. This game takes it seriously: dynamic objectives, a wing that obeys, and a capital ship that is not a boss with one hit point.

What has aged is the original briefing interface and the graphics if you leave them without community updates. What has not aged is the scale and the mission design, in which a battle can turn on an order, not on a cutscene. In seminar, hide the orders and let students only shoot. The game falls from an operation to an arcade game, and that names what the authorship was.

Against Homeworld there is a pilot here. Comparing those two games of the year is a clean class on the distance of the camera from the war. The wing is a system, not a radio for effect. If you do not use it, you are playing a smaller game than the one Volition wrote.

The Shivans are pressure from outside, a relative of the late crises in 4X. The difference is that here you solve the crisis with your hands on the stick, not with research in a turn. The game closes the genre in its classic form. The next decade will shrink space action or hide it in another camera, and this cockpit remains the measure.

FreeSpace 2 is a 1999 space combat simulation video game developed by Volition as the sequel to Descent: FreeSpace – The Great War. The game continues on the story from Descent: FreeSpace, once again thrusting the player into the role of a pilot fighting against the mysterious aliens, the Shivans.

This code became the core of the FreeSpace 2 Source Code Project, which continuously improves it and enables new features. In cooperation with the FreeSpace Upgrade Project the game's graphics are kept up to date.

FreeSpace 2 s gameplay involves the player piloting a starfighter using mounted weapons to destroy enemy starfighters, performing reconnaissance behind enemy lines, or escorting other starships. The implementation of nebulae as an interactive environment is one of the most distinctive aspects of FreeSpace 2.

FreeSpace 2 s story is brought out via narrative pre-rendered cutscenes, the pre- and post-mission briefings, as well as in-game chatter between non-player characters, and scripted mission events. The news of FreeSpace 2 being in development was confirmed in a chat on November 6, 1998.

The team set themselves the goals of setting new standards for both single-player and multiplayer space combat simulations, and started to modify the FreeSpace game engine for FreeSpace 2. This team was the same team which had worked on Descent: FreeSpace, plus several new members.

The team made major improvements to the same FreeSpace engine from the first game. FreeSpace 2 was released on September 30, 1999, one month ahead of schedule, although the team had to quickly come up with and release a patch (version 1.01) for a software bug which prevented recognition of a CD during the installation process.

The release of FreeSpace 2 was considerably muted compared to its predecessor Descent: FreeSpace. Despite Volition's interest and desire to develop add-ons and expansions for FreeSpace 2, Interplay told them to stop.

In the years since, no sequels to FreeSpace 2 have been made and Interplay has only published a limited re-release of it on February 2, 2004, to commemorate the company's 20th anniversary. Derek Smart, creator of Battlecruiser 3000AD, had casually mentioned his interest in the Freespace license, but nothing significant came out of this.

In 2013, Interplay acquired the remaining rights to the FreeSpace franchise for $7,500 after THQ went to bankruptcy court. FreeSpace 2 received numerous "Game of the Year" awards for 1999, and was nominated for "Computer Simulation Game of the Year" during the 3rd Annual Interactive Achievement Awards.

Combatsim even claimed FreeSpace 2 was unrivaled among its space combat peers in the graphics department. GameSpot felt FreeSpace s story was "both deeper and darker" than either the Wing Commander and X-Wing series, establishing invincible foes who never lost their stature despite the player learning of plausible ways to defeat them.

GameSpot, in electing FreeSpace 2 as one of the "Greatest Games of All Time", pointed out that while most of the game's features could be found in its predecessor or peers, its "sheer quality of presentation and gameplay" was the key reason for their choice. Ars Technica also posed FreeSpace 2 as the last significant stage in evolution of the space combat genre .

Fans of the FreeSpace series have created modifications (mods) of FreeSpace 2. The first mods were just custom campaigns, with series of missions created through FRED2, the mission editor freely packaged with FreeSpace 2.

One such mod which gained notability was Inferno, which sets its story decades after the conclusion of FreeSpace 2. Released in July 2003, the mod was hosted on established sites, such as GameSpot and CNET, as part of their FreeSpace 2 contents.

FreeSpace 2 was a runner-up for GameSpot s 1999 "Best Game No One Played" award, which ultimately went to Disciples: Sacred Lands. FreeSpace 2 s sales were acknowledged as disappointing, and described as awful by Kulas.

Led by Edward Gardner and Ian Warfield, the FreeSpace 2 Source Code Project was formed to standardize changes and maintain a core engine for others to take advantage of. The components of its website were, however, later handed over to the FreeSpace 2 Source Code Project to help them create a similar service in tracking statistics and rankings.
