Tim Anderson, Marc Blank, Bruce Daniels, and Dave Lebling wrote it in MIT's Dynamic Modelling Group on a PDP-10, in the language MDL, as a student and research project, not as a product. The original Zork of 1977 is one enormous game on a mainframe. Only later do the same people, now as Infocom, cut it into parts that fit on home microcomputers.

The decision to model an object as a thing with properties, not as a line in a description, had the consequence that a puzzle can take account of what you are carrying, what is open, and what is in the dark. Darkness is a rule, not a mood. Without light the world punishes you, because not seeing means dying, not merely reading a shorter description.

In 1977 the home Atari VCS is released and the arcades are on their own road. Zork is the opposite pole: a game for people who have an account on a large computer and patience for a sentence. The name Zork was MIT slang for an unfinished thing; Infocom only takes it seriously as a title at the moment it turns a laboratory game into a company.

What has aged is the need to guess the vocabulary and the punishment for a synonym the author did not include. What has not aged is the density of a world in which an object has more than one use and a room has more than one reason to return.

Infocom turns this group, at the turn of the decade, into the most influential workshop of text games in America. In a seminar, compare a paragraph of Zork with a paragraph of Crowther: you will see how a description of a cave becomes a simulation of things.

Zork is a text adventure game first released in 1977 by developers Tim Anderson, Marc Blank, Bruce Daniels, and Dave Lebling for the PDP-10 mainframe computer. The original developers and others, as the company Infocom, expanded and split the game into three titles Zork I: The Great Underground Empire, Zork II: The Wizard of Frobozz, and Zork III: The Dungeon Master which were released commercially for a range of personal computers beginning in 1980.

In Zork, the player explores the abandoned Great Underground Empire in search of treasure. Blank and Joel Berez created a way to run a smaller portion of Zork on several brands of microcomputer, letting them commercialize the game as Infocom's first products.

Zork was a massive success for Infocom, with sales increasing for years as the market for personal computers expanded. Infocom was purchased by Activision in 1986, leading to new Zork games beginning in 1987, as well as a series of books.

Reviews of the episodes were very positive, with several reviewers calling Zork the best adventure game to date. In 2007, Zork was included in the game canon by the Library of Congress as one of the ten most important video games in history.
