A captain trades, fights, searches for family, and changes careers in the Caribbean. A series of linked systems without a single obligatory activity is an early demonstration of a sandbox "story machine."

A captain in the Caribbean trades, raids ships, dances with governors' daughters, looks for his lost family, and can end as an official, a pirate, or a person who simply got rich and left. The duel on deck and the naval battle are minigames. The large game is a calendar of seasons in which you decide what you are even going to do today.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/091-cover-1.jpg)

Sid Meier designed it at MicroProse, and in 1987 it was released on the Commodore 64. It is not a war simulation like Gunship; it is a collection of systems that pretend to be a biography. One diskette holds the map, the ports, the fencing, and the politics, because none of those modes is deep on its own. The depth comes from their cancelling and supporting one another.

The decision not to give the player a single obligatory activity had the consequence that progress is a style. Whoever does not want to dance can plunder; whoever does not want to plunder can carry sugar. The game takes that as a valid reading. The historical period you choose at the start changes who is at war with whom. The scenery is not one fixed map of a year, it is a switch for political facts.

In 1987 dungeon crawlers are coming into being on the PC beside it, and Contra in the arcades. Pirates! is a home game that trusts fun can be a series of afternoons, not one reflex. Meier's signature, known also from later strategy games, is here in his cutting a complicated period into readable moves and still leaving you the choice of which move is yours.

What has aged are the minigames, which today feel like thin tests, and an interface that without a manual hides half the career. What has not aged is the freedom to have a bad biography and still finish the game: the ending is not a victory, it is a balance sheet.

From this ship, a sandbox that is called a story machine learns something: the systems narrate because you choose among them, not because the author wrote one path. In a seminar, after an hour let the students say what they became. If their sentences do not meet, the game has done what Meier assembled.

It was designed by Sid Meier, and was the first game to include his name in its title as an effort by MicroProse to attract fans of Meier's earlier games, most of which were combat vehicle simulation games. An enhanced remake, also named Sid Meier's Pirates!, was released in 2004.

Gameplay is open-ended; the player may choose to attack enemy ships or towns, hunt pirates, seek buried treasure, rescue long-lost family members, or even avoid violence altogether and seek to increase their wealth through trade. During peacetime, the player can only benefit from the capture of pirates on the high seas, which is seen as favorable by all nations, but is a rare occurrence.

In early 1986, Meier and fellow MicroProse designer Arnold Hendrick wanted to create a role-playing adventure game, but Meier's business partner Bill Stealey was skeptical of producing non-vehicle simulations. With five successful years behind him, Microprose considered star designer Sid Meier a selling point and chose to put his name on the box of his next game, despite a shift away from combat simulators he had become known for.
