A deity raises a giant creature and influences villages through good or fear. Creature AI learns from the player's rewarding and punishing; the interface tries to minimize traditional menus.

In Black & White you do not cast from a spell bar. You drive a divine hand into the landscape: pet a villager, uproot a tree, or throw a boulder through a roof. Beside you grows a giant creature that remembers rewards and punishments and, from them, begins to feed, pray, or destroy on its own.

![Cover](/img/worthwhile-games/media/301-cover-1.jpg)

Peter Molyneux built it at Lionhead, the studio he founded after leaving Bullfrog, as proof that a simulation could replace a menu. Electronic Arts published it in 2001. You trigger many miracles by drawing a gesture in the air. The interface is free and imprecise at once, so you often fight your own hand as much as a rival god.

A village's morality is not a dialogue option. People pray to you or flee from you according to whether you heal them or treat them as raw material. The creature's learning is real and hard to read. Two playthroughs can raise different animals, but the player usually only guesses why it chose as it did.

In 2001, when strategy still relied on a clear panel, the bet on gesture and on a moral sandbox sat deliberately outside the ordinary genre. Landscape and water were meant to feel like the body of the world, not a mat under units. The impression from a distance was stronger than the precision of control up close.

Combat has since aged into an unclear flailing, and the promise that the animal would truly understand your ethics stayed half kept. Lionhead's question still holds: can divine power be a toy in the palm, and can a companion carry a memory of your rewards.

A reputation for grand promises was already forming around Molyneux. For a seminar it is more useful to test what the hand can actually do than to repeat stories from interviews. The sequel made the system clearer and brought it closer to ordinary strategy. The first game remains the extreme experiment of its year.

Black & White is a god video game developed by Lionhead Studios and published by Electronic Arts for Microsoft Windows in 2001 and by Feral Interactive in 2002 for Mac OS. The core gameplay mechanic of Black & White is the interaction between the player and an avatar creature, who carries out the player's instructions and whose personality and behaviour change in reaction to how they are treated.

Black & White was written from scratch, and the intention was to have the main user interface free of icons, buttons, and panels. Black & White received universal acclaim on release; reviewers praised the artificial intelligence, uniqueness, and depth, although the system requirements and bugs were criticised.

Black & White won awards from several organisations, including the British Academy of Film and Television Arts, the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences and the Guinness World Record, for the complexity of the artificial intelligence, selling over two million copies. An expansion, Black & White: Creature Isle, and a sequel, Black & White 2, followed.

Black & White took over three years to develop, beginning on 14 February 1998, and was released on 27 March 2001. In 1998, Black & White was shown at the E3 trade show in Atlanta, Georgia, and incorporated elements of Populous and Dungeon Keeper.

By this time, the engine was being developed by three people: Alex Evans, Jean-Claude Cuttier, and Scawen Roberts (who had joined from a courier company called, coincidentally, Black And White). Black & White was shown at E3 1999, where it was judged the most original game.

Molyneux later said that Black & White was amongst the first video games to have fan sites, and that Lionhead was accused of over-promising when the sites described features the game did not have when shown. Black & White s online community was handled by two servers in London, where the clan creatures were stored to minimise the possibility of cheating.

Molyneux said Black & White was the most important and difficult game he had made. The Japanese version was released on 24 May 2001, and re-released as Black & White Special Edition under the EA Best Selections branding on 18 March 2004.

A spinoff of the series, Black & White Creatures was announced for the Nintendo DS in May 2005. Reviewing the Macintosh version, Kit Pierce of Inside Mac Games remarked "Black & White is a gorgeous game", and commended its addictiveness.

Black & White was named by PC World as the Best Video Game of 2001, and appeared in the 2003 Guinness World Records for having the "most intelligent being in a game".
