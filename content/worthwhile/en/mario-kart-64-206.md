Four players race in 3D and use items. The four-port N64 and battle mode made karting one of the pillars of couch multiplayer.

Four people on one screen circle in karts, drop peels, and discover that the winner of the last corner was often not the fastest. The race has laps and items; battle mode takes the race track away and leaves you in a small arena where the goal is to hit your friends, not to pass a gate.

The PlayStation in the same year needs a multitap if four people are to play. Nintendo builds the couch into the hardware, and Mario Kart 64 is the game that actually puts those ports to work.

The decision to allow four players and items that compress the field has a consequence familiar from every party. The best driver is not automatically the winner of the evening, and that is exactly why the evening gets repeated. The track is three-dimensional, but it reads as a toy: the banana, the shell, the lightning, and the star have silhouettes you recognize even in the chaos of a split screen.

In 1996, 3D on a console is still proof of technology. Here the technology serves so that four people fit in a living room and the track has hills, not so that it impresses a solitary player. Rubber-banding, the catching up of drivers in the pack, is a conscious unfairness. The consequence is a party game that falls apart the moment you start judging it as a pure sport.

What has aged is the simple drift compared with later entries, and also the resolution, in which a distant kart blends into the track. The politics of items has not aged. Friendship in this game is a system that one blue shell in the final lap can ruin.

Developed and published by Nintendo, it is the second installment in the Mario Kart series after 1992's Super Mario Kart. The game retains the gameplay of its predecessor: the player, controlling a Mario franchise character, races opponents around tracks based on locales from the Super Mario platform games.

Mario Kart 64 contains different single-player and local multiplayer game modes, including a Grand Prix racing mode and a last man standing battle mode. Developers were bounded by the N64's processing power and made use of rendering techniques to save on memory, and used car physics simulations to aid the game's kart design.

Mario Kart 64 was released in late 1996 in Japan and in 1997 worldwide, months after the launch of the N64. Mario Kart 64 was nominated for awards following its release, and retrospectively has been considered one of the greatest video games of all time.

The player controls one of eight Mario franchise characters and races opponents in karts around tracks based on locales from the Super Mario platform games. Princess Peach, Toad, and Yoshi are lightweights; Luigi and Mario are middleweights; and Bowser, Donkey Kong, and Wario are heavyweights.

Mario Kart 64 was developed concurrently with Super Mario 64 (1996) and The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (1998) by Nintendo's Entertainment Analysis & Development division. Mario Kart 64 was intended to be released alongside the N64 itself in mid-1996 but was delayed after development resources were directed towards Super Mario 64, itself a launch title for the system.

Developers did not focus on innovating significantly on the gameplay of its predecessor Super Mario Kart, intending for the game to appeal to a wide audience. Developers created a simulation of remote-controlled vehicles and did research on the physics of cars to aid in designing the game's kart mechanics.

Mario Kart 64 was released in Japan on December 14, 1996, in the United States on February 10, 1997, and in Europe on June 24—the American release of the game was moved forward a week from the initially announced date. It was the second entry in the Mario Kart series after Super Mario Kart.

Coinciding with the game's release in Japan, Nintendo released a special edition of Mario Kart 64 which came with a specially-styled black-and-grey N64 controller. Mario Kart 64 was digitally rereleased on the Wii and the Wii U through the Virtual Console, releasing on the Wii worldwide across January 2007.
