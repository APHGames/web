Joe enters a film world and controls slow/fast effects. Film tempo becomes a mechanic of combat and of puzzles; cel-shading underscores the meta-cinematic identity.

Viewtiful Joe sends an ordinary spectator into a film where villains hold his girlfriend. The VFX modes Slow, Mach Speed, and Zoom are film editing rewritten as buttons. Slow motion does not merely make a stylish shot. It opens a window in which you land more blows, and at the same time it breaks puzzles that cannot be read at normal speed.

Hideki Kamiya directed it and Atsushi Inaba produced it at Capcom in 2003, originally on the GameCube. It continues the idea from Devil May Cry: a fight is a performance. The cel shading holds the metaphor of celluloid. The world is not supposed to look like a room but like a shot someone is just exposing.

The consequence is that the player is both the stunt performer and the editor. A bad tempo is not only a mistake in a combo; it is a badly chosen shot. In 2003, when action on the GameCube runs from Zelda to Resident Evil 4, Joe is deliberately a small, loud game about the grammar of film.

The difficulty and the repetition of some fights have aged into stubbornness. The verb of changing tempo itself has not. Zoom enlarges an enemy and makes him a cinematic victim. Scale is an attack, not merely a camera looking at you.

For students of Kamiya's line it is the middle link between Devil May Cry and the later PlatinumGames. Everywhere you pay for style; here you pay for the edit. Captain Blue and the film quotations are a period joke. Under them is a serious system that punishes the player for fighting in a boring rhythm.

Later ports carried the game to other consoles. The original GameCube version is still the one that sits in Capcom's year of experiments on Nintendo. When you play it today, switch tempo before you look for a stronger blow. The game will not give you a stronger blow; it will give you a better shot.

It follows avid movie-goer Joe, who is pulled into the world of the movies to become the superhero "Viewtiful Joe". The series has been largely dormant since the 2005 game Viewtiful Joe: Double Trouble!, however its characters have made appearances in later Capcom fighting games.

It was the second title to be released under the Capcom Five, developed by a team of developers within Capcom Production Studio 4 known as Team Viewtiful. Viewtiful Joe 2 features both Viewtiful Joe and Sexy Silvia as playable characters with similar gameplay elements to the first game.

In an interview with Dengeki PlayStation in 2017, he addressed his former employer that he would want to work on a remake of the first Viewtiful Joe. During PAX East 2020, Kamiya told attendees to "email Capcom" to get sequels to dormant franchises, including Viewtiful Joe.

An anime TV series based on the video game series, simply titled Viewtiful Joe, was produced by Group TAC and aired from 2004 to 2005. Viewtiful Joe appears as a playable character in the Wii version of the 2008 fighting game Tatsunoko vs.

In Japan, the GameCube version of Viewtiful Joe sold through its initial shipment of less than 100,000 copies during the week of its release. Preorders of the GameCube version sold out on Capcom's North American website prior to its ship date, and Viewtiful Joe debuted as the tenth best-selling game in the region.
