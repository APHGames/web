A six-member party maps a first-person dungeon and develops its characters. The template of a party-based dungeon RPG with classes and tactical statistics strongly influenced Western and Japanese RPGs.

Six characters in first person move through a maze, open doors step by step, and fight in turns where classes, armor, and spells decide. You draw the map on paper. The game will not give you a floor plan, because losing your bearings is part of the danger, not a lack of interface.

![In the game](/img/worthwhile-games/media/024-screenshot-1.jpg)

Andrew Greenberg and Robert Woodhead wrote it for the Apple II, and Sir-Tech published it in 1981. It runs in UCSD Pascal, which on a home machine of the time is not an obvious way to build a large game. The wireframe dungeon redraws step by step. Wall, door, and darkness are a small set of views, but they are enough for the player to believe in the corridor.

The decision to lead a whole party, not one hero, had the consequence that a member's death is a tactical loss, and a temple resurrection is an economy, not mercy. The classes and statistics are openly an heir of Dungeons & Dragons, trimmed to what a keyboard and a few kilobytes can manage.

In 1981 Ultima I is its sibling, with a more open map. Wizardry answers with the depth of one building and the severity of a party that develops over weeks. Japan reads this structure carefully. Yuji Horii and other authors of console RPGs take from it the dungeon, the menu, and the advancement of characters.

What has aged is drawing the map on graph paper and the punishment for forgetting which doors were illusions. What has not aged is the weight of the lineup: who stands in the front rank, which spell you are holding for later, and when you even dare to descend.

Dragon Quest and later dungeon crawlers still solve the same question, only they now draw the map for you. In a seminar, let the party die without a map. The students will understand why a pencil was part of the controller.

The game's lack of an automap feature, which had not been invented at the time of its release, practically forces the player to draw the map for each level on graph paper (included in the box) as they walk through the 20x20 dungeon maze, Wizardry drew influences from earlier games from the PLATO system, most notably the 1977 role-playing game Oubliette. The game took two and a half man-years to complete, but the delay benefited Wizardry by permitting almost one year of playtesting and game balancing before release, distinguishing it from others such as Ultima I.

The Commodore 64/128 versions of Wizardry 1-3 share a common code base with the Apple originals, as they all use the same run-time 6502 Pascal interpreter which provides support for overlays and low-level functions to interface with the hardware. Lengthy load time and extensive disk access was a problem with Wizardry; however, the Commodore versions, which particularly suffer from this, provided a variety of workarounds.

Wizardry 2-5 also detect if 16k or 64k of VDC memory is present and can use the 1571 drive's burst mode for faster load time. Sir-Tech published Wizardry, Revision 2 in 1982, which included new features.
